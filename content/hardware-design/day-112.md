# hardware-design — Day 112

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a boost converter's inductor when the load has fast current transients, and what parameters would you check to make sure the design stays robust across the full load range?

**Answer:** I'd start by defining the operating envelope rather than a single operating point: minimum and maximum input voltage, output voltage, maximum average load current, and the peak transient current the load can demand. For a boost converter the inductor carries the full input current continuously, so the first sizing constraint is the peak inductor current — average input current plus half the ripple — and it must stay comfortably below the inductor's saturation current at the worst-case combination of low input voltage and maximum load. Saturation is the hard limit; exceeding it causes inductance to collapse, current to run away, and efficiency and reliability to fall off a cliff.

Next I'd choose the inductance value from the ripple requirement. A larger inductance gives lower ripple current, which reduces conduction losses and output ripple, but it also slows the converter's transient response and requires a physically larger part with higher DCR. A smaller inductance responds faster but pushes ripple and peak current up. For a load with fast transients, I'd lean toward the smaller end of the acceptable ripple range and rely on output capacitance to supply the transient energy, because the inductor current can only slew at a rate set by the voltage across it — the converter simply cannot respond instantly to a step load. So the inductor and output capacitor have to be co-designed: the capacitor supplies the transient, the inductor refills it.

Other parameters I'd check: DCR, because it directly sets conduction loss and heating; core material and its loss behavior at the switching frequency; the inductance tolerance and how much it falls at rated current (many parts are specified at zero bias, which is misleading); and thermal rating at the actual RMS current, not just the peak. I'd also verify the inductor's self-resonant frequency is well above the switching frequency so it behaves inductively, and check whether the part is shielded if it sits near sensitive analog circuitry. Finally I'd simulate or bench-test at the corners — minimum input voltage with maximum transient load, and hot — because that's where saturation margin disappears first.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the transient response shortfall should be solved with more output capacitance versus a higher switching frequency? What would you look for on a bench waveform to confirm the inductor is not saturating?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a SAR ADC and a delta-sigma ADC for a slowly varying physiological signal that needs high resolution, and what trade-offs would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The first question is what "high resolution" actually needs to mean — not bits on a datasheet, but effective resolution at the signal bandwidth, and whether the requirement is absolute accuracy or just noise-free resolution around a narrow range. For a slowly varying signal like temperature or pressure, the signal bandwidth is low, which immediately favors delta-sigma: the architecture oversamples and noise-shapes, so you get very high resolution at low output rates essentially for free, and the digital filter that comes with it provides inherent anti-aliasing that a SAR needs an external analog filter to achieve.

The trade-offs cut the other way in several places. Delta-sigma has higher latency — the digital filter's settling time means multiplexing between channels is slow, so if I need to scan many channels quickly, a SAR is far better. SAR gives an immediate conversion per sample, is easy to multiplex, and has well-understood behavior, but its resolution at 16+ bits demands a very clean reference, a well-driven sampling capacitor, and an anti-aliasing filter ahead of it. Delta-sigma's input is a switched-capacitor structure too, so it still needs a good input driver, and its digital filter can produce idle tones or limit-cycle behavior if the input sits at a fixed DC value — something to watch for with a slowly varying signal that spends time near a constant.

I'd also weigh: power (delta-sigma can be very low power at low output rates but the digital filter costs something), the reference requirement (both need a good reference, but delta-sigma's noise shaping can relax the reference somewhat at low bandwidth), and system complexity — a delta-sigma with an integrated PGA and digital filter can collapse a lot of analog front-end into one part, which is attractive for a compact medical sensor. My decision rule: low bandwidth, high resolution, few channels, latency tolerant → delta-sigma. Moderate resolution, many channels, need fast per-channel sampling or tight timing → SAR.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the effective resolution you're actually getting, as opposed to the datasheet's nominal bits? What would you do if a delta-sigma converter showed idle tones at DC input?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** I'd resist the temptation to assume the MOSFET is faulty and instead work through where the power is actually going. First, confirm the measurement: a case temperature of 85°C doesn't by itself tell you the junction temperature or the dissipation — I'd want to know the thermal resistance from junction to ambient for the actual package and board, and back-calculate. If the dissipation implied by the temperature is far higher than I²R would predict, something other than conduction loss is happening.

The usual suspects, in order: the MOSFET is not fully enhanced — "gate voltage looks correct" often means it measures 10V at the gate pin, but the source may not be at ground, or the gate drive may be slow enough that the device spends significant time in the linear region during switching. For a resistive load switched infrequently that shouldn't matter much, but if it's switching at any real frequency, switching loss dominates. I'd scope the gate-source voltage and the drain-source voltage simultaneously and look at the crossover — the product of Vds and Id during the transition is the switching loss, and a slow gate drive or a gate resistor that's too large will show up there immediately.

Second, check whether the device is actually in saturation: measure Vds while on. If Vds is higher than Id × Rds(on) at the actual gate voltage and temperature, the part is either not fully on, or Rds(on) has risen with temperature more than expected — Rds(on) roughly doubles from 25°C to 125°C for many parts, so a design sized at room temperature can be marginal hot. Third, check for oscillation: a MOSFET with a long gate trace or a poorly damped gate circuit can oscillate at high frequency, and that shows up as heating with no obvious DC cause. Fourth, check the load itself — "2A resistive" may not be purely resistive at the actual operating conditions, or there may be inrush. I'd also verify the thermal path: poor soldering to a thermal pad, insufficient copper, or a package with a much higher thermal resistance than assumed will make a correctly dissipating part run hot.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish conduction loss from switching loss experimentally? What would you change first if the part is running hot only at elevated ambient?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a ferrite bead for a power rail that feeds both a sensitive analog sensor drawing a small steady current and a digital microcontroller drawing larger current with fast transient spikes?

**Answer:** The trap with ferrite beads is treating them as ideal low-pass filters. They're lossy impedances that are highly frequency-dependent, and their behavior depends heavily on the DC bias current flowing through them — the impedance can drop substantially at rated current, and a bead chosen for its 100 MHz impedance may do very little at the frequencies that actually matter. So I'd start by being clear about what I'm trying to attenuate: is it the microcontroller's transient current spikes coupling back onto the analog rail, high-frequency noise from a switching regulator upstream, or radiated pickup? Each has a different frequency range and a different fix.

For this case, the bead sits in series with the analog sensor's supply, and the microcontroller's transient current is the aggressor. The bead's impedance at the transient's frequency content provides isolation, but the bead also has DC resistance that drops voltage and dissipates power, and it forms a resonant circuit with the decoupling capacitors on either side. That resonance is the thing to check carefully: a bead plus a capacitor can peak in impedance at some frequency and actually amplify noise there, or ring. I'd look at the bead's impedance-versus-frequency curve, its rated current and the impedance derating at that current, its DCR, and its DC bias characteristics, then simulate or measure the bead-plus-capacitor network's impedance across frequency rather than trusting a single datasheet number.

I'd also consider whether a bead is the right tool at all. If the goal is to keep the microcontroller's transients off the analog rail, a small series resistor or an RC filter on the analog supply may be more predictable, at the cost of some voltage drop. If the goal is to keep switching noise out, a proper LC filter with a damped capacitor may be better. The bead is attractive when you need low DC loss and high-frequency attenuation in a small package, but it must be verified in situ — the surrounding capacitors, the layout, and the actual load transients all change its behavior, so I'd measure the rail noise with and without it on the bench rather than assume.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you damp the resonance between a ferrite bead and its decoupling capacitors? What would you check on the bead's datasheet to understand how its impedance changes with DC bias?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a cost-versus-risk trade that needs to be decided on evidence, not on who feels more strongly. My first move would be to separate the two concerns the manufacturing engineer is raising — cost and manufacturability — and make sure we're comparing the same thing. "Cheaper finish" can mean different things depending on which alternative, and the cost delta is often smaller than it first appears once you account for yield, rework, and inspection. So I'd ask for the actual cost comparison at the volumes we expect, including the downstream costs of a finish that's harder to solder or inspect.

Then I'd bring the technical argument down to specifics rather than generalities. For a high-density BGA, the finish's flatness and its behavior during reflow matter for joint uniformity and for avoiding voids or opens under the package, where rework is expensive or impossible. For the high-resolution ADC, the concern is more about surface uniformity and long-term reliability of fine-pitch joints than about the ADC's electrical performance per se — I'd want to be precise about that, because overstating the electrical case weakens the argument. I'd propose we evaluate the alternative against the actual assembly requirements: the finest pitch on the board, the BGA ball count and pitch, the inspection method, and any reliability or shelf-life requirements.

If the manufacturing engineer's alternative genuinely meets those requirements, I should be open to it — the goal is the right board, not my preferred board. If it doesn't, I'd want the decision documented with the specific risks, so it's a conscious accepted risk rather than an accident. I'd also look for a middle path: sometimes the cost concern can be addressed by changing the finish only where it's not needed, or by adjusting panelization or inspection to reduce cost elsewhere. The key is to keep the discussion anchored on the requirements and the evidence, and to make sure the person raising the cost concern feels heard rather than overruled.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the evaluation so the decision is defensible later if a field issue appears? What would you do if the manufacturing engineer's alternative met the requirements on paper but you had no production history with it?

**Possible follow-ups (Q5 continued):** How would you handle it if the disagreement escalated to the project manager as a schedule risk rather than a technical one?
