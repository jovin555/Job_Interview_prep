# hardware-design — Day 51

## Q1: How would you approach designing a snubber network for a switching node in a converter, and how would you decide whether it's actually needed?
**Answer:** I'd start by asking whether the ringing is a real problem or just cosmetically ugly on the scope. The first step is to measure the switching node with a proper high-bandwidth probe and a short ground lead — a long ground clip creates its own ringing that can mislead you into "fixing" something that isn't there. If there's genuine overshoot approaching or exceeding the MOSFET's Vds rating, or if the ringing is coupling into a sensitive analog node or radiating as EMI, then a snubber is justified.

For an RC snubber across the switch node or across the MOSFET, the approach is: measure the ringing frequency with the circuit running, add a small capacitor across the switch until the frequency roughly halves, which tells you the parasitic inductance and capacitance in play, then size the resistor to critically damp the ring — typically around the characteristic impedance of that LC. The capacitor should be large enough to absorb the ringing energy but not so large that it dumps excessive charge every cycle and kills efficiency. The resistor dissipates that energy, so there's always an efficiency and thermal cost.

The trade-off is real: a snubber reduces EMI and voltage stress but wastes power and adds components. Alternatives I'd consider first are slowing the gate drive slightly with a small series gate resistor, improving the layout to shrink the commutation loop, or choosing a MOSFET with better output capacitance characteristics. A snubber is often the last resort when layout can't be changed or the ringing is inherent to the topology. I'd also verify the snubber's own thermal margin, since the resistor can run hot.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish ringing caused by the commutation loop from ringing caused by a probe artifact? What changes if the converter is in a medical device where EMI must meet a specific standard?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit?
**Answer:** The core distinction is that a comparator is designed to operate open-loop and saturate cleanly, while an op-amp is designed for closed-loop linear operation and will behave unpredictably if you try to use it as a comparator — slow recovery from saturation, phase inversion in some input ranges, and unpredictable output states near the rails. So for a protection threshold, a dedicated comparator is almost always the right choice.

That said, the selection within comparators matters. For a protection function I'd look at propagation delay, input offset voltage and its drift over temperature, hysteresis (either built-in or added externally), and output type — open-drain versus push-pull — because that determines how it interfaces to the latch or shutdown logic. Hysteresis is critical: without it, a slow-moving input near the threshold will cause the output to chatter, which in a protection circuit could mean repeated nuisance trips or, worse, an oscillation that defeats the protection. I'd add positive feedback to set a defined hysteresis band, sized so that normal operating noise and ripple can't cross both thresholds.

I'd also consider whether the comparator needs to be independent of the main processor's supply — in a safety-relevant protection path, powering it from a separate, always-on rail means it still functions if the main rail collapses or the firmware hangs. And I'd check the comparator's behavior during power-up and power-down, since an undefined output during a supply ramp could falsely trigger or fail to trigger.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you calculate the hysteresis band given a known noise amplitude on the monitored signal? What failure modes would you analyze for the comparator itself in a risk assessment?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting and placing a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?
**Answer:** The first decision is the crystal's frequency tolerance and stability spec over the full operating temperature range, not just at 25°C. A part that's ±10 ppm at room temperature can easily be ±30 or ±50 ppm across a wide range, and if the application has a communication protocol or a timekeeping requirement, that budget has to be checked against the total error allowance — including the microcontroller's own oscillator loading and any PLL multiplication.

Next is load capacitance. The crystal datasheet specifies a load capacitance, and the two external caps plus stray capacitance must match it. If they don't, the oscillator runs off-frequency, and in the worst case it won't start reliably. I'd calculate the caps from the specified load capacitance minus estimated stray, then verify on the bench by measuring the actual frequency and, if needed, trimming. Drive level matters too — too much drive can age or damage the crystal, too little can prevent startup, especially at temperature extremes.

On the bench I'd verify startup margin across the full temperature range, not just at room temperature, because startup is hardest at temperature extremes and at low supply voltage. I'd also check the oscillator's negative resistance margin — a common rule of thumb is that the oscillator's negative resistance should be at least several times the crystal's equivalent series resistance. And I'd look at the waveform for clipping or excessive distortion, which indicates overdrive. For anything with tight timing, I'd also consider whether a TCXO or an external clock source is warranted instead of a plain crystal.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative resistance margin on a crystal oscillator? What would you do if the crystal starts reliably at room temperature but fails at cold?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the analog front-end for a sensor whose output impedance is high and varies with the measurand, and what would you watch for?
**Answer:** A high and varying source impedance is one of the trickier front-end problems because it interacts with everything downstream. The first concern is the amplifier's input bias current: with a high source impedance, bias current flowing through that impedance creates an offset voltage that changes as the impedance changes, which shows up as a measurement error that looks like sensor drift. So I'd select an amplifier with very low input bias current — a CMOS or JFET input stage — and I'd check the bias current's temperature coefficient, not just its room-temperature value.

The second concern is settling and bandwidth. A high source impedance combined with the amplifier's input capacitance and any feedback network forms a pole that can limit bandwidth and cause slow settling, which matters if the signal is multiplexed or sampled. I'd calculate the RC time constant at the input and make sure it's compatible with the required sampling rate, and if not, consider a buffer stage close to the sensor or a guard/neutralization technique to reduce effective input capacitance.

The third concern is noise. The sensor's own thermal noise scales with its resistance, so a high-impedance sensor may already have a noise floor that dominates the budget. I'd do a noise analysis that includes the sensor's Johnson noise, the amplifier's voltage noise, and the current noise times the source impedance, and pick an amplifier whose noise contributions are small relative to the sensor's. I'd also pay attention to shielding and guarding, because a high-impedance node is much more susceptible to picking up interference, and PCB leakage currents can become significant.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to buffer the sensor locally versus at the ADC? What layout techniques reduce leakage into a high-impedance node?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading the hardware design for a medical device, and a supplier notifies you that a component you've designed in — a regulator that's already qualified and in your design history file — is being discontinued, with a last-time-buy window that closes before your production ramp. How would you handle the situation?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is not panic and not immediately redesign. I'd gather the facts: what's the exact last-time-buy date, what's the minimum order quantity, what's the lead time, and is there a pin-compatible or functionally equivalent replacement already qualified by the supplier? Sometimes the "discontinuation" is a package or grade change with a drop-in alternative, which is a much smaller problem than a full redesign.

If a drop-in or near-drop-in alternative exists, I'd evaluate it against the original requirements — electrical specs, thermal, regulatory-relevant characteristics — and determine whether it can be qualified as a like-for-like substitution or whether it triggers a more formal change control process. In a medical device context, any change to a component in the design history file has to go through change control, and depending on the impact, it may require re-testing or a regulatory notification. I'd bring in regulatory and quality early rather than treating this as purely an engineering decision.

If a last-time-buy is the right bridge, I'd work with procurement and finance to size the buy against forecast demand plus a safety margin, and make sure the storage and shelf-life implications are acceptable. In parallel, I'd start the redesign or requalification work so we're not dependent on the bridge inventory indefinitely. Throughout, I'd keep the project manager and stakeholders informed with a clear risk assessment and a decision timeline, because a supply chain surprise like this can affect the schedule, and the worst outcome is discovering it late.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a last-time-buy and an immediate redesign? What documentation would you need to support a component change in a regulated device?
