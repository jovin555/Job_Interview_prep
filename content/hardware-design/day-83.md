## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor value and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start by budgeting the error and the power dissipation together, because they pull in opposite directions. A larger shunt gives more signal for the amplifier to work with, which improves signal-to-noise and reduces the relative impact of the amplifier's offset and noise — but it burns more power as I²R and drops more voltage headroom. A smaller shunt does the reverse. So the first step is to fix a maximum acceptable shunt dissipation (say, a few tens of milliwatts at full-scale current) and a minimum acceptable full-scale sense voltage that keeps the amplifier's input-referred offset and noise a small fraction of the signal.

For a bidirectional measurement, the shunt sits in series with the load and the amplifier must handle both polarities, so I'd choose a current-sense amplifier with a defined common-mode range that includes the rail the shunt sits on, and either a reference/bias pin to offset the output mid-supply or a truly bidirectional topology. For a 1A full-scale with ±2% accuracy over temperature, the dominant error terms are the shunt's own temperature coefficient and tolerance, the amplifier's input offset voltage and its drift, gain error, and CMRR. I'd pick a shunt with low TCR (ideally well under 100 ppm/°C) and a tight initial tolerance, then verify that the amplifier's offset drift over the temperature range stays within the error budget — offset drift is often the term that quietly eats the budget.

Topology-wise: for a high-side shunt (shunt between supply and load), I get the advantage that a short to ground downstream is still detected, but the amplifier must tolerate a common-mode voltage near the supply rail. For a low-side shunt, the common-mode is near ground, which is easier for the amplifier, but a load short to ground bypasses the shunt and the measurement is lost. For a motor driver where fault detection matters, high-side is usually the safer choice despite the harder common-mode requirement. I'd also consider whether the amplifier needs to reject PWM switching noise from the motor drive — if so, the amplifier's bandwidth and common-mode transient immunity become important, and I might add a small differential filter at the inputs.

Finally, I'd lay out the shunt as a four-terminal (Kelvin) connection so the sense traces don't carry the load current, and keep the sense pair tightly coupled and symmetric to preserve CMRR.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the accuracy of the current-sense chain over temperature on the bench? What would change in your selection if the motor were driven by a PWM signal at 20 kHz?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting between a linear regulator and a switching regulator for a low-current analog rail in a medical device, and what factors would drive the decision beyond efficiency?
**Answer:** For a low-current analog rail, efficiency is usually not the deciding factor — at tens of milliamps, the absolute power lost in a linear regulator is small, and the noise and simplicity advantages of a linear regulator often win. The real drivers are noise, PSRR, transient response, and the thermal budget.

A linear regulator's output noise is typically much lower than a switching regulator's, and it doesn't inject switching ripple or radiated EMI into a sensitive analog front-end. If the analog rail feeds a high-resolution ADC or a low-level sensor amplifier, that matters a lot. A linear regulator also has no inductor, no switching node, and no loop-compensation headache, which simplifies layout and reduces the number of things that can go wrong in a medical design where the analog chain must be predictable.

The trade-offs against a switching regulator: a linear regulator can only step down, so the input must always be above the output plus dropout. If the input is a battery whose voltage falls below the required rail, a linear regulator won't work and I'd need a switching regulator or a buck-boost. A linear regulator also dissipates (Vin − Vout) × Iload as heat, so if the drop is large and the current is not truly low, the thermal budget can force a switching solution. And a linear regulator's PSRR falls off with frequency, so if the upstream rail has switching noise, I need to check the PSRR at the frequencies where that noise lives, not just the DC value.

My usual approach: if the rail is low-current, the input is comfortably above the output, and the analog chain is noise-sensitive, start with a linear regulator and add input/output filtering as needed. If the drop is large, the current is higher than "low," or the input can fall below the output, move to a switching regulator — and then consider a linear post-regulator (an LDO after the switcher) to clean up the rail for the most sensitive loads. That hybrid is common in medical designs: a switcher for efficiency and a linear stage for noise.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you evaluate whether the linear regulator's PSRR is adequate at the switching frequency of the upstream converter? What layout practices reduce the noise a linear regulator couples into the analog rail?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?
**Answer:** A temperature-dependent behavior with unchanged load points at a parameter that drifts with temperature, not a load-related issue. I'd work through the candidates systematically.

First, the output capacitor. Electrolytic capacitors have significant ESR that rises at cold and capacitance that falls at hot, and some ceramic dielectrics (especially high-K types like Y5V) lose a large fraction of their capacitance as temperature rises. If the output cap's effective capacitance drops at high temperature, the ripple will increase and the loop may become marginal. I'd measure the actual ripple waveform at temperature and check whether the switching frequency or duty cycle has shifted, which would suggest the loop is losing phase margin.

Second, the feedback network and the reference. If the regulator's internal reference drifts with temperature, the output voltage will shift, and if it shifts enough to approach a dropout condition or a comparator threshold, the "occasional dropout" could be the regulator hitting a limit. I'd measure the output voltage at temperature, not just the ripple.

Third, the inductor. An inductor's saturation current falls as temperature rises for many core materials, and its DC resistance rises. If the inductor is close to saturation at room temperature, it may saturate at high temperature, causing a sudden increase in ripple and a drop in effective inductance. I'd check the inductor current waveform at temperature — a flattening or spike in the current ramp is the signature.

Fourth, the switching element itself. A MOSFET's on-resistance rises with temperature, and its threshold voltage falls. If the gate drive is marginal, the device may not fully enhance at high temperature, increasing conduction loss and possibly causing the regulator to enter a hiccup or current-limit mode. I'd check the gate waveform and the switch node at temperature.

Fifth, thermal effects on the control loop: some regulators have internal thermal shutdown or foldback that can trigger intermittently if the die is running hot, which would look like "occasional dropout." I'd check the die temperature and whether the dropout correlates with a thermal event.

My practical sequence: reproduce it in a thermal chamber or with a heat gun while monitoring the switch node, output ripple, inductor current, and output voltage simultaneously. Compare the waveforms at room temperature and at the failing temperature. The change in the waveform usually points directly at the culprit — a saturated inductor, a collapsed output cap, or a loop that's gone unstable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between an inductor saturating and an output capacitor losing capacitance, from the ripple waveform alone? What would you check in the layout if the problem only appears on some boards?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware-based POST is to verify that the analog signal chain is actually functional and within tolerance before the device is trusted to make a measurement — and to do so in a way that doesn't depend on the firmware or the ADC being correct, because those are exactly the things that could be wrong. So the test should inject a known stimulus and check the response with an independent path.

The core idea is to have a known reference or a known signal source that the hardware can switch into the front-end, and a way to compare the resulting output against an expected window using a comparator or a window detector, not the ADC. For example, a precision reference voltage can be switched into the input of the instrumentation amplifier, and a comparator can verify that the amplifier's output lands within an expected band. That checks the amplifier's gain and offset without involving the ADC. A second check can verify the ADC's reference itself — for instance, by comparing the reference against a divided-down version of a known supply or a second reference, again with a comparator.

I'd want the POST to cover: the analog supply rails (are they within tolerance?), the voltage reference (is it at the right value?), the front-end amplifier (does it respond correctly to a known input?), and the ADC's ability to convert (does a known input produce a code within an expected window?). The rail and reference checks can be done with comparators and window detectors; the amplifier check needs a switchable stimulus; the ADC check needs a known input and a way to compare the result — which could be a hardware comparator on the ADC's output or a simple digital window check that doesn't rely on the full firmware stack.

The key design principle is independence: the test should not use the same signal path it's trying to verify. If the ADC is the thing under test, the comparison should be done in hardware. If the amplifier is under test, the stimulus should come from a source that doesn't depend on the amplifier. And the test should be able to fail safe — if the POST doesn't pass, the device should not enter a mode where it could deliver a wrong measurement or therapy.

I'd also want the POST to be runnable at power-on and, ideally, periodically or on demand, and to record its result in a way that's visible to the system. The design should be simple enough that it can be verified and validated as part of the device's regulatory submission — a POST that's too clever is hard to argue is safe.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the case where the POST itself fails intermittently — how would you distinguish a real fault from a test artifact? What would you want the device to do if the POST fails?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** The disagreement is really about two different assumptions: how the device will actually be used in the field, and what the reliability requirement should be. I'd start by separating the factual question from the judgment call.

First, I'd ask the reliability engineer to walk me through their mating-cycle estimate — where does the number come from? Is it based on a use case where the cable is disconnected and reconnected regularly, or is it a worst-case assumption? If the device is genuinely intended to be connected once and left in place, the number of mating cycles over the device's life might be very small, and the current connector could be fine. But if there's any realistic scenario where the cable is replaced in the field — by a clinician, a technician, or the patient — then the reliability engineer's concern is legitimate and I need to take it seriously.

Second, I'd look at the actual datasheet ratings for both connectors: mating cycles, contact resistance over life, insertion/withdrawal force, and any derating for the environment. I'd also consider whether the connector is part of a patient-contact path, which brings in additional requirements around isolation and safety.

Third, I'd bring the question to the risk management process. If the connector fails in the field, what's the harm? If it's a nuisance (the cable needs replacing), that's one thing; if it could cause a loss of monitoring or a safety issue, that changes the calculus. The risk file should drive the decision, not just the cost difference.

If the analysis shows the current connector is adequate for the intended use, I'd document that — the use case, the expected mating cycles, the datasheet margin, and the risk assessment — and present it to the reliability engineer and the review board. If the analysis shows the concern is valid, I'd accept the higher-cycle connector, because the cost of a field failure in a medical device is almost always higher than the cost of a better connector. Either way, the decision should be made on evidence and documented, not on who argues harder.

I'd also want to make sure the decision is captured in the design history file, because if the connector is later implicated in a field issue, the rationale for the choice needs to be traceable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the connector's mating-cycle rating needs to be verified by testing rather than relying on the datasheet? If the higher-cycle connector requires a board respin, how would you weigh that against the risk?
