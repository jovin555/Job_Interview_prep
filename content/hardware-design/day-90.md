# hardware-design — Day 90

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?

**Answer:** I'd start by translating the system's timing budget into a frequency-tolerance requirement, then work backward through the error sources. The crystal's own stability over temperature is the dominant term for most parts — a standard AT-cut crystal has a parabolic frequency-vs-temperature curve with a turnover point, so its worst-case deviation typically occurs at the temperature extremes, not at 25°C. If the budget is tight, I'd look at a TCXO or a crystal with a specified tighter temperature coefficient rather than trying to compensate a marginal part in firmware. Beyond the crystal itself, I'd account for the load-capacitance pulling sensitivity (frequency shifts with the actual load capacitance seen by the crystal, so the specified CL must match the circuit's real capacitance including stray and pin capacitance), the initial tolerance at 25°C, and aging over the product life. For the oscillator circuit, I'd verify the negative-resistance (startup margin) across the full temperature range — a crystal that starts reliably at room temperature can fail to start cold if the amplifier's transconductance drops or the crystal's ESR rises. On the bench I'd measure startup time and startup reliability over temperature in a chamber, check the actual operating frequency against the nominal with a frequency counter, and confirm drive level is within the crystal's rating to avoid over-driving and long-term aging or damage. I'd also look at whether the load capacitors need to be NP0/C0G for stability, since X7R parts would drift with temperature and voltage and pull the frequency.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative resistance / startup margin on the bench? What would you do if the crystal starts reliably but the frequency is consistently off by a fixed amount across all units?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage-mode and a current-mode control architecture for a buck converter that must supply a fast-transient digital load while also feeding a sensitive analog rail through a downstream LDO?

**Answer:** The two loads pull the decision in different directions, so I'd frame it around what each control scheme is good at. Current-mode control gives inherent cycle-by-cycle inductor-current limiting, a single dominant pole in the control loop (which simplifies compensation and makes it more robust to input-voltage variation), and generally better line-transient response — useful when the input rail is noisy or varies. It also handles the fast-transient digital load well because the inner current loop responds quickly to load steps. Voltage-mode control has a two-pole LC filter in the power stage, so compensation is more involved and the loop is more sensitive to component variation, but it's often quieter at the switching node and can be easier to synchronize across multiple rails. For the sensitive analog rail, the key point is that the downstream LDO provides the PSRR and noise filtering, so the buck's job is to deliver a clean-enough, well-regulated intermediate rail with good transient response — which favors current-mode. I'd also consider that the LDO's PSRR degrades at high frequency, so the buck's switching ripple and any loop instability need to be kept out of the band the LDO can't reject. I'd verify the choice by simulating load-step response and checking phase margin across the operating range, then confirming on the bench with a load-step test and a ripple/PSRR measurement at the LDO output.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would the presence of the downstream LDO change how you measure the buck's transient response? What are the failure modes of current-mode control at high duty cycle, and how would you address them?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting the feedback resistor values for a transimpedance amplifier front-end, and what trade-offs would drive the choice between a high-value and a low-value feedback resistor?

**Answer:** The transimpedance gain is set by the feedback resistor, so the first driver is the output swing needed for the expected photocurrent range — you want the largest signal at the output that still fits the amplifier's swing and the ADC's input range, which argues for a larger Rf. But a larger Rf brings several penalties: the resistor's own thermal (Johnson) noise rises with the square root of Rf, so the input-referred noise floor goes up; the feedback pole formed with the photodiode's junction capacitance and the amplifier's input capacitance moves down in frequency, which can compromise stability and bandwidth; and the resistor's parasitic capacitance and the board leakage become more significant. A low-value Rf gives wider bandwidth and lower noise contribution from the resistor, but demands more gain from the amplifier and pushes the output signal down toward the noise floor of the following stage. I'd also weigh the amplifier's input bias current — with a high Rf, bias current flowing through it produces a DC offset that can saturate the output, so a low-bias-current (CMOS or JFET-input) amplifier is usually needed. I'd compensate the feedback pole with a small capacitor across Rf to stabilize the loop, sized from the total input capacitance and the desired phase margin, and verify on the bench with a step response and a noise measurement.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you compensate the transimpedance loop, and what would you measure to confirm adequate phase margin? How does the photodiode's capacitance affect the achievable bandwidth, and what could you do about it?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?

**Answer:** The correlation with handling and movement points strongly toward a mechanical or intermittent-connection cause rather than a pure electrical design flaw, so I'd treat it as a systematic hunt for a marginal contact or a stress-sensitive joint. First I'd try to reproduce it outside the enclosure by flexing the board, tapping components, and wiggling connectors and cables while monitoring the failing signal — this localizes whether it's a connector, a solder joint, a flexing trace, or a component. I'd inspect the suspect areas under magnification for cold joints, cracked solder fillets (especially on larger or heavier parts like connectors, inductors, and crystals), and board flex near mounting points. I'd also check whether the enclosure itself is applying pressure to a component or a cable that's causing a short or an open, and whether the failure is thermal (handling warms the board) rather than purely mechanical — so I'd separate the two by testing with controlled temperature and with mechanical stimulus independently. If the failure is a signal integrity issue that only appears when the board is in the enclosure, I'd look at grounding and shielding changes introduced by the enclosure, and at cable routing that may now run near a noise source. Throughout, I'd instrument the suspect nets so I can catch the failure in the act rather than guessing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a thermal intermittent from a mechanical one? What design or manufacturing changes would you consider to prevent this class of failure in production?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the software lead argues that the device's power-on self-test can be simplified by removing the hardware checks — rail monitors, reference checks — and relying on firmware to verify the system by reading the ADC and checking that values are in range. The software lead argues this reduces component count and board area. You believe the hardware checks are necessary because they verify the analog signal chain independently of the ADC and the firmware. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a question of what the self-test is actually supposed to prove, and get alignment on that before debating components. The core issue is independence: a firmware-only check that reads the ADC and confirms values are in range cannot detect a fault in the ADC itself, in the reference, or in the firmware — a stuck or drifting ADC can still return plausible in-range numbers, and a hung firmware can't run its own check at all. The hardware checks exist precisely to verify the signal chain independently, so removing them changes the safety argument, not just the BOM. I'd walk the software lead through concrete failure modes the firmware check would miss, and frame the discussion around the device's risk analysis and the regulatory expectation that safety-critical monitoring be independent of the main processor where feasible. I'd also be open to the cost concern: I'd ask whether the rail monitors and reference checks can be consolidated or implemented more cheaply without losing independence, and whether some checks can share existing hardware. If we still disagree, I'd escalate to the systems/risk owner with the trade-off documented, because this is a risk-management decision rather than a purely engineering preference — and I'd want the decision recorded with its rationale either way.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document this decision so it holds up in a design history file review? If the team decides to keep the hardware checks, how would you minimize their cost and board area impact?
