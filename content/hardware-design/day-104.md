# hardware-design — Day 104

## Q1: How would you approach selecting and verifying the load capacitance for a crystal oscillator, and what would you check on the bench to confirm the circuit has adequate startup margin across temperature?

**Answer:** I'd start from the crystal datasheet's specified load capacitance (CL) and back-calculate the two external capacitors using the standard relation CL = (C1·C2)/(C1+C2) + Cstray, where Cstray is the estimated parasitic capacitance of the PCB traces and the MCU pin — typically a few pF, and worth measuring or at least budgeting conservatively rather than assuming zero. The external caps are usually chosen equal, so each ends up roughly 2·(CL − Cstray). Getting this wrong shifts the actual oscillation frequency off the crystal's specified load point, which shows up as a frequency error and, more importantly, can reduce the negative resistance margin.

The parameter I care most about for reliability is the oscillator's negative resistance (or transconductance) margin. The datasheet gives a maximum drive-level and a recommended oscillation margin; the rule of thumb is that the amplifier's negative resistance should be at least 3–5× the crystal's equivalent series resistance (ESR) at the worst-case condition. I'd verify this on the bench rather than trust the calculation: inject a small signal through a series resistor at the crystal node and increase the resistance until oscillation just stops — the resistance at which it stops, minus the crystal's ESR, gives the negative resistance margin. I'd do this at the temperature extremes and at the supply voltage extremes, because the amplifier gain and the crystal's ESR both move with temperature, and startup margin is worst at cold and at low supply.

On the bench I'd also check: startup time from power-on (should be well within the MCU's oscillator startup timeout), drive level (measured with a current probe or inferred from the voltage across the crystal, to stay under the datasheet maximum and avoid long-term aging or damage), and frequency accuracy against the load-capacitance spec. For a real-time-clock crystal at 32.768 kHz, the frequency tolerance over temperature is usually dominated by the crystal's own parabolic temperature characteristic, so I'd confirm the chosen part's tolerance curve meets the system's timing budget rather than trying to trim it with load caps. Finally, I'd confirm the layout keeps the crystal traces short, away from switching nodes, with a solid ground return and a guard ring if the board is noisy — a marginal oscillator that works on the bench can fail in the enclosure once a nearby regulator starts switching.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a startup-margin problem from a drive-level problem if the oscillator starts reliably at room temperature but not at cold? What layout practices would you insist on around the crystal to keep a switching regulator from pulling the oscillator?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage-mode and a current-mode control architecture for a buck converter that must supply a fast-transient digital load while also feeding a sensitive analog rail through a downstream LDO?

**Answer:** The two architectures differ mainly in what the inner loop regulates and how the compensation behaves. Voltage-mode control senses only the output voltage and compares it to a ramp; it's simple, gives good noise immunity on the feedback signal, and handles a wide range of duty cycles cleanly, but the loop has a double pole from the LC output filter, so compensation is more involved and the transient response to a fast load step is generally slower unless the crossover is pushed high. Current-mode control adds an inner loop that regulates the inductor current cycle-by-cycle, which effectively removes the LC double pole and leaves a single dominant pole, making compensation simpler and giving inherently better line rejection and cycle-by-cycle current limiting. The cost is that current-mode is more sensitive to noise on the current-sense signal and needs slope compensation to avoid subharmonic oscillation above 50% duty cycle.

For a fast-transient digital load, current-mode's faster, more predictable transient response and simpler compensation usually win — the inner current loop reacts to a load step within a cycle or two, and the outer voltage loop only has to correct the residual. That matters when the digital load can jump by hundreds of milliamps in a few microseconds. But the downstream LDO changes the picture: the analog rail doesn't see the buck's output ripple directly, it sees what the LDO's PSRR lets through, so the buck's job for the analog side is mainly to keep its output within the LDO's headroom and not to inject noise the LDO can't reject. That argues for prioritizing transient response and clean switching behavior over absolute output ripple, and for making sure the LDO has enough headroom at the buck's worst-case output tolerance.

So my decision would be: current-mode for the fast digital load, with careful layout of the current-sense trace (Kelvin connection to the sense resistor, short and shielded from the switch node), adequate slope compensation, and a crossover frequency set well below the switching frequency. I'd verify with a load-step test at the actual transient amplitude and slew rate, and check the LDO's output for any ripple breakthrough at the switching frequency. If the current-sense signal proved too noisy in layout, I'd fall back to voltage-mode with a well-damped Type III compensation rather than fight the noise.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set the slope compensation, and what symptom tells you it's insufficient? How would you decide the crossover frequency given the downstream LDO's PSRR curve?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting a Schottky diode for a boost converter's output rectification stage, given a 5V output at 500mA with a switching frequency of 1MHz?

**Answer:** In a boost converter the output diode carries the inductor current during the off-time and blocks the output voltage plus the switch-node ringing during the on-time, so the selection hinges on reverse voltage, forward current, forward drop, reverse recovery, and thermal behavior — and at 1 MHz the switching losses start to matter as much as conduction losses.

For reverse voltage, I'd pick a rating comfortably above the output voltage plus margin for ringing: with a 5V output, a 20V or 30V Schottky is typical, giving headroom for the switch-node overshoot that layout and parasitic inductance will produce. Going much higher in voltage usually costs forward drop and capacitance, so I wouldn't over-specify. For forward current, the diode sees the full inductor current during off-time, whose average equals the load current but whose peak is higher due to ripple; I'd size for the peak repetitive current and check the datasheet's forward-voltage curve at the actual operating current and temperature, not just the headline rating. Forward drop directly sets conduction loss (Vf × Iavg), and at 500mA a Schottky's ~0.4–0.5V drop is a meaningful fraction of a 5V output, so a lower-Vf part pays for itself in efficiency.

At 1 MHz, reverse recovery and junction capacitance dominate the switching loss. A Schottky has essentially no minority-carrier recovery, but its junction capacitance still charges and discharges each cycle, and that capacitive loss scales with frequency and the square of the reverse voltage. So I'd compare candidate parts on total capacitive charge (Qc) and reverse-recovery charge rather than just Vf, and I'd check the thermal: the diode's power dissipation is conduction plus switching, and in a small package the thermal resistance to ambient may be the limiting factor. I'd also confirm the package and footprint suit the layout — a low-inductance connection to the switch node and output cap matters at 1 MHz. Finally, I'd verify on the bench by measuring efficiency across the load range and checking the diode's case temperature at full load and worst-case ambient.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a synchronous boost with a MOSFET instead of a diode is worth the added complexity at this power level? What would you check on the switch node to confirm the diode's reverse voltage margin is actually adequate?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where an op-amp's output is correct at DC but shows a slow, large-amplitude drift over minutes when the board is warmed by nearby power components?

**Answer:** A slow drift over minutes that correlates with board warm-up points to a thermal effect rather than an AC problem, so I'd first separate thermal from electrical by characterizing the drift against temperature rather than time. I'd use a thermal camera or a thermocouple on the op-amp and on the nearby power components, log the output and the local temperature together, and see whether the drift tracks temperature monotonically or shows a time constant that matches a particular component heating up. If the drift follows the op-amp's own temperature, the suspects are the op-amp's offset voltage drift, input bias current drift interacting with source impedance, or a resistor's temperature coefficient; if it follows a nearby power component, the suspects are thermal gradients across the board creating thermocouple voltages at dissimilar-metal junctions, or a component whose value shifts with temperature.

The most common culprits in a precision front-end are: offset voltage tempco (check the datasheet's µV/°C and multiply by the temperature rise — a few degrees can be tens of microvolts), input bias current tempco multiplied by the source resistance (which is why high-impedance nodes drift more), and resistor tempco mismatch in a divider or gain network (a 100 ppm/°C pair with a 20°C rise can produce a visible gain shift). I'd also check for thermocouple effects: any solder joint between dissimilar metals — a connector, a test point, a resistor lead — generates a small voltage per degree of temperature difference, and if the two input paths see different temperatures, that appears as a differential drift. Layout asymmetry that puts one input trace near a heat source is a classic cause.

My debugging sequence would be: confirm the drift is thermal by cooling the board locally with freeze spray and watching it reverse; then isolate by heating individual components with a hot-air pencil or a small resistor and seeing which one reproduces the drift; then compare against the datasheet tempco to see whether the magnitude is explainable or whether something is actually wrong. If it's inherent tempco, the fix is part selection (lower-drift op-amp, matched resistor network, lower source impedance) or thermal design (moving the op-amp away from the heat source, adding a thermal break in the copper). If it's a thermocouple or layout asymmetry, the fix is layout symmetry and keeping the two input paths thermally and electrically matched.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish offset-voltage drift from bias-current-times-source-resistance drift experimentally? What layout changes would you make to reduce thermal gradients across a precision front-end?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the mechanical engineer proposes moving a temperature sensor from the PCB to a location closer to the patient-contact surface, arguing it will give a more accurate reading of the actual patient temperature. You're concerned that the new location is electrically noisy and mechanically difficult to route, and that the sensor's own self-heating and the thermal mass of the new location may actually degrade accuracy. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a measurement-accuracy question to be settled with data and a shared model of the thermal path, rather than a turf disagreement between mechanical and electrical. The mechanical engineer's underlying point is legitimate — a sensor on the PCB reads board temperature, not patient temperature, and there's a real thermal gradient between them — so I'd start by acknowledging that and reframing the question as "what does each location actually measure, and which one gets us closer to the quantity we care about?"

Concretely, I'd want to build a simple thermal model of both options: the thermal resistance from the patient-contact surface to the sensor in each location, the thermal mass at each location, and the sensor's self-heating (excitation current times voltage, times the thermal resistance to its surroundings). Self-heating is often the dominant error for a small sensor in a poorly thermally-connected location, and moving the sensor to a spot with more thermal mass or worse coupling to the patient can make it slower to respond and more affected by self-heating, even though it's physically closer to the patient. I'd also want to quantify the electrical concern: what noise sources are near the new location, what's the sensor's signal level and the front-end's noise floor, and can shielding or a filtered twisted pair mitigate it without compromising the mechanical design.

Then I'd propose a concrete way to decide: a bench or phantom test that places both sensor locations in a controlled thermal environment and compares their readings against a reference thermometer, including a step response to see how each tracks a changing temperature. That converts the disagreement into a measurement both sides can trust. If the data shows the new location is better despite the noise, I'd support it and solve the routing and shielding; if it shows the thermal mass or self-heating dominates, I'd present that and propose a compromise — for example, keeping the sensor on the PCB but improving thermal coupling to the patient surface through a thermal bridge, or using a remote sensor with a well-defined thermal path. Throughout, I'd keep the discussion focused on the accuracy requirement and the verification plan rather than on whose proposal wins, and I'd document the decision and its rationale in the design history file so it's traceable later.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you quantify the sensor's self-heating error, and what would you do if it turned out to be larger than the thermal-gradient error you were trying to fix? If the two locations each win on a different metric, how would you decide which error matters more for the clinical use case?
