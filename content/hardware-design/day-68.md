# hardware-design — Day 68

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start by budgeting the sense voltage against the power dissipation constraint. With a 1A full-scale current, a 100 mΩ shunt dissipates 100 mW at full scale, which is often acceptable in a motor-drive context but may be too much in a tightly sealed enclosure; a 10 mΩ shunt drops that to 10 mW but yields only 10 mV full-scale, which pushes the amplifier's offset and noise requirements much harder. The trade is essentially: larger shunt → better SNR but more loss and self-heating; smaller shunt → less loss but offset voltage and drift dominate the error budget. For ±2% over temperature, I'd want the amplifier's input offset drift, gain error, and CMRR contributions each to be a small fraction of that budget — say, no single term exceeding 0.3–0.5% — so the RSS of all error terms stays comfortably inside ±2%.

For topology, a bidirectional measurement rules out a simple low-side shunt unless the motor's return path is isolated from other grounds, because low-side sensing puts the shunt between the motor and ground and can inject noise into the ground reference. High-side sensing keeps the ground intact but requires an amplifier whose common-mode range includes the motor supply rail, and whose CMRR is high enough that supply transients during PWM switching don't corrupt the reading. A dedicated current-sense amplifier with an integrated matched resistor network is usually preferable to a discrete difference amplifier, because the on-chip resistor matching directly sets CMRR and is far better than what discrete 0.1% resistors achieve. If the motor supply is above the amplifier's common-mode range, a current-sense amplifier with an integrated level-shift stage or an isolated topology is needed.

I'd also consider whether the measurement is for control (continuous, moderate bandwidth) or for protection (fast, threshold-based). For control, a moderate-bandwidth current-sense amplifier with a filtered output is fine. For protection, a separate comparator path with its own fast response is usually warranted, because the control-path amplifier's bandwidth and settling may be too slow to catch a fault. Finally, I'd verify the shunt's temperature coefficient — a 100 ppm/°C shunt contributes 0.5% error over a 50°C swing, which eats a quarter of the budget — and choose an alloy with low TCR (e.g., a four-terminal Kelvin-connected metal-element shunt) rather than a standard thick-film resistor.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you lay out the shunt and amplifier to preserve CMRR in the presence of PWM switching noise? What would change if the motor supply were 48V instead of 12V?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware POST is to give the system a way to confirm that the analog signal chain is functional before trusting any measurement — and to do so in a way that doesn't depend on the very firmware and ADC that might be the failure. So the first design question is: what can be verified with hardware alone, and what genuinely requires the ADC?

Things that can be verified purely in hardware: the presence and level of each supply rail (via a comparator or supervisor IC with a window threshold), the presence of the reference voltage (via a second reference or a divided-down comparison), and the continuity of the sensor excitation path (via a current-sense or voltage-sense comparator). These checks can drive a hardware fault line that gates the system's ability to enable a therapeutic output, independent of firmware.

Things that require the ADC but can still be verified in a firmware-independent way: the signal chain's gain and offset. A common technique is to inject a known test signal — either a precision reference divided down, or a known current into the sensor's input — and confirm that the ADC reads a value within an expected window. The key is that the test signal source is itself hardware, not firmware-generated, so a firmware hang doesn't prevent the test from running. The firmware's role is only to read the result and compare it to a stored window; if the firmware is hung, the hardware watchdog or the fault line handles it.

I'd also want the POST to verify the signal chain's *dynamic* behavior, not just DC. A slowly varying test signal or a step, injected at the front end, lets the firmware (or a hardware peak detector) confirm that the chain's bandwidth and settling are within limits. This catches failures like a dried-out coupling capacitor or a degraded op-amp that a DC-only test would miss.

The design trade-offs are: test signal injection adds components and can couple noise into the signal path if not switched out cleanly; the test must be fast enough not to delay startup unacceptably; and the test must not itself be a single point of failure. I'd want the test path to be fail-safe — if the test signal source fails, the POST should report a fault rather than silently pass.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you switch the test signal in and out without degrading the signal path's noise performance during normal operation? What would you do if the POST passes but the device later fails in the field?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?
**Answer:** Audible whining from an inductor means the switching frequency — or a harmonic of it — has dropped into the audible range, roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The most common cause is that the regulator has entered a pulse-skipping or burst mode at light load to maintain efficiency, and the burst repetition rate is in the audible band. This is a normal behavior for many modern regulators, but it's unacceptable in a medical device where the noise is a patient or clinician annoyance, and it can also indicate the regulator is operating in an unintended mode.

My first step is to confirm the mechanism with a scope: probe the switch node and look at the envelope of switching activity. If I see bursts of switching separated by idle periods, and the burst rate is in the audible range, that's the cause. I'd also check whether the whining correlates with load — if it disappears at higher load, that confirms burst mode.

Once confirmed, the options are: (1) change the regulator's mode configuration if it has a selectable forced-PWM or low-noise mode — many regulators have a pin or register setting that disables burst mode at the cost of light-load efficiency; (2) increase the minimum load so the regulator stays in continuous conduction mode, though this wastes power and may not be acceptable in a battery device; (3) change the inductor to one with a higher self-resonant frequency or better mechanical damping, which can reduce the audible amplitude but won't eliminate the underlying burst behavior; (4) change the regulator to one whose light-load behavior is inherently above the audible band, or that uses a different light-load scheme (e.g., a low-power mode that keeps the switching frequency high but reduces the number of active phases).

I'd also check for a second cause: inductor mechanical resonance. Some inductors have a mechanical resonance in the audible band, and even a small ripple current at that frequency can produce audible noise. If the switching frequency itself is in the audible band (some low-power regulators run at 20–30 kHz), that's a design choice that needs to be revisited. And I'd verify that the output capacitor isn't the source — some ceramic capacitors are piezoelectric and can sing at the switching frequency, which is often mistaken for inductor noise.

The fix depends on the root cause, but the general principle is: identify whether the noise is from the switching frequency itself, a burst-mode envelope, or a mechanical resonance, and address the specific mechanism rather than adding a blanket fix like a snubber, which may not help and can hurt efficiency.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish inductor noise from capacitor piezoelectric noise on the bench? What would you do if the regulator has no low-noise mode and the burst behavior is inherent to the part?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?
**Answer:** The starting point is the accuracy requirement. If the application needs ±20 ppm over 0–50°C, a standard AT-cut crystal with a typical ±30 ppm temperature characteristic won't meet it, and I'd need either a TCXO, an oven-controlled crystal, or a crystal with a tighter temperature curve. If the requirement is ±50 ppm, a standard crystal may be fine, but I'd still need to account for the crystal's initial tolerance, its temperature drift, the load capacitance tolerance, and aging.

The key parameters to select on are: nominal frequency, load capacitance (which must match the oscillator circuit's design), equivalent series resistance (ESR), drive level, and temperature stability. The load capacitance is critical — if the PCB's actual load capacitance differs from the crystal's specified value, the frequency shifts. I'd calculate the load capacitance from the two external capacitors plus the PCB's stray capacitance (typically 2–5 pF) and the MCU pin capacitance, and choose the external caps so the total matches the crystal's specification. I'd also check the crystal's ESR against the oscillator's maximum drive capability — a crystal with too-high ESR may not start reliably, especially at temperature extremes.

On the bench, I'd verify: (1) startup margin — does the oscillator start reliably at the temperature extremes, and how long does it take? I'd use a scope with a low-capacitance probe on the oscillator output, or better, a frequency counter, and check startup at cold and hot. (2) Frequency accuracy at temperature — I'd measure the output frequency at several temperatures across the range and compare to the crystal's specified curve. (3) Drive level — I'd verify the oscillator isn't over-driving the crystal, which can cause aging or damage; this is often measured indirectly by checking the oscillator's output amplitude against the crystal's drive-level specification. (4) Negative resistance margin — I'd measure the oscillator's negative resistance by adding a series resistor and finding the point where oscillation stops; a healthy margin is typically 3–5× the crystal's ESR.

I'd also consider whether the crystal's package and mounting affect its thermal behavior — a crystal mounted near a heat source will see a different temperature than the ambient, and the temperature curve must be evaluated at the crystal's actual location, not the board's ambient.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure the oscillator's negative resistance margin without disturbing the circuit? What would you do if the crystal's startup margin is marginal at cold temperature but the part is already qualified?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the factual question from the judgment question. The factual question is: what is the actual expected number of mating cycles over the device's lifetime, and what is the connector's rated cycle count? The judgment question is: what use case are we designing for — a cable that's connected once and left in place, or a cable that may be disconnected and reconnected in the field?

I'd start by asking the reliability engineer what use case they're envisioning. If they're thinking of field replacement — a nurse or technician disconnecting the cable to swap it, or the device being moved between patients — that's a real scenario that needs to be designed for, and it may not be captured in the current requirements. If the use case is genuinely "connected once at installation and never touched," then the reliability engineer's concern may be based on a different assumption than mine, and the disagreement is really about requirements, not about the connector.

I'd bring the question to the requirements owner — the systems engineer or product manager — and ask them to clarify the intended use case and the expected number of mating cycles. If the requirements say the cable is not user-replaceable and is connected once, then the lower-cycle connector is defensible, and I'd document that assumption in the design history file. If the requirements are silent or ambiguous, that's a gap that needs to be closed before we can make a decision.

If the requirements do call for field replacement, then the reliability engineer is right and I'd need to either accept the higher-cycle connector or find another way to meet the requirement — for example, a connector with a replaceable cable assembly, or a design that makes the cable connection more robust. I'd also consider whether the higher-cycle connector introduces other trade-offs: size, cost, availability, and whether it's qualified for the medical environment.

Throughout, I'd try to keep the conversation focused on the requirements and the evidence, not on who's right. If we can't agree on the use case, I'd propose a test: cycle the connector to the expected number of cycles and verify it still meets its electrical and mechanical specifications. That gives us data to make a decision rather than trading opinions.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document the decision if the requirements are ambiguous and you decide to go with the lower-cycle connector? What would you do if the higher-cycle connector is not available in a medically qualified version?
