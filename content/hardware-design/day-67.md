## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start with the shunt value, because it sets the trade-off between signal amplitude and power loss. At 1A full scale, a 100 mΩ shunt dissipates 100 mW and develops 100 mV — a healthy signal for a current-sense amplifier, but 100 mW in a small package is a real thermal problem. Dropping to 10 mΩ gives 10 mV full scale and only 10 mW, but now the amplifier's input offset and drift dominate the error budget: a 100 µV offset alone is 1% of full scale, and over temperature that can easily blow the ±2% target. So the shunt value is chosen by working backwards from the error budget, not by picking a convenient resistance.

For the amplifier topology, the key decision is where the sense element sits relative to the supply. A high-side shunt preserves the ground reference for the load and lets you detect a short to ground, which matters for a motor driver, but it requires an amplifier with high common-mode input range — ideally one rated to survive the full supply plus transients, since a motor's inductive kick can push the sense node well above the rail. A low-side shunt is simpler and cheaper because the common mode is near ground, but it breaks the load's ground reference and can't detect a ground fault. For bidirectional current you need an amplifier that can swing below ground at its input, or a reference-shifted output so that zero current sits at mid-supply and the ADC can read both directions.

For accuracy over temperature, the dominant error terms are the shunt's own temperature coefficient and the amplifier's input offset voltage drift — not its room-temperature offset, which you can calibrate out. A shunt with a low TCR (on the order of tens of ppm/°C) and a chopper-stabilized or zero-drift amplifier keeps the drift budget manageable. I'd also check the amplifier's common-mode rejection, because in a high-side configuration the common-mode voltage moves with the motor's PWM, and any CMRR error appears as a current-measurement artifact synchronized to the switching. Finally, I'd verify the shunt's power derating at the maximum ambient the board will see, and consider a four-terminal (Kelvin) connection so the sense traces don't add to the effective resistance.

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?
**Answer:** The decision comes down to what the circuit must do at the threshold and how fast it must respond. A comparator is purpose-built for the job: it has a large differential gain, an output stage designed to switch cleanly between logic levels, and no compensation capacitor slowing it down, so its propagation delay is short and well-specified. An op-amp used as a comparator will eventually saturate and switch, but its slew rate and recovery from saturation are usually unspecified, it may draw excessive supply current when overdriven, and its output swing may not reach the logic thresholds the downstream circuit expects. For a protection function where response time is part of the safety requirement, that unspecified behavior is a liability.

That said, an op-amp can be the right choice in specific cases: when the threshold needs hysteresis that's easier to implement with feedback around an op-amp, when the signal is slow and the response-time requirement is loose, or when you need the output to sit in a linear region rather than rail-to-rail. But for a protection comparator, I'd default to a real comparator and add hysteresis explicitly — either through positive feedback or a dedicated latch — so the output doesn't chatter when the input sits near the threshold.

The other driver is the input stage. A protection comparator often sees a signal that can exceed the supply rails during a fault, so I'd look for a comparator with input protection or a wide common-mode range, and I'd check the input offset voltage and its drift, because the threshold accuracy over temperature is usually part of the requirement. If the threshold must be accurate to a few percent over temperature, the comparator's offset drift and the reference's drift both matter, and I'd budget them together rather than treating the comparator as ideal.

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?
**Answer:** Audible whining means the inductor is being mechanically excited at a frequency in the audible range — roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz — and the fact that the output is within spec tells me the regulator is still regulating, so this is a mechanical/acoustic symptom rather than an electrical failure. The first thing I'd do is measure the switching frequency at light load with a scope on the switch node, because many regulators enter a pulse-skipping or burst mode at light load to save power, and that mode can produce a low-frequency envelope that the inductor's core and windings respond to. If the switching frequency itself has dropped into the audible band, that's the direct cause; if the switching frequency is still high but the burst repetition rate is low, the burst envelope is the culprit.

Once I know the frequency, I'd look at the inductor's construction. A loosely wound or unshielded inductor with a gapped core can sing because the gap creates a fringing field that exerts mechanical force on the windings, and the core material itself can magnetostrict. A shielded, molded, or potted inductor is much less prone to this. I'd also check whether the inductor is saturating or near saturation at light load — some regulators' burst mode drives higher peak currents during the on-pulses, which can push a marginally sized inductor into partial saturation and amplify the mechanical excitation.

For fixes, the options are: change the inductor to a shielded or molded type with better mechanical damping; adjust the regulator's mode configuration (some parts let you disable burst mode or set a minimum switching frequency); add a small RC snubber across the switch node to damp the ringing that may be exciting the inductor; or, if the regulator supports it, increase the minimum load or add a small preload to keep it out of the light-load mode. I'd verify the fix by re-measuring the switch node and confirming the audible tone is gone, and I'd check that the change didn't degrade efficiency at light load, since that's often why the burst mode was enabled in the first place.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine whether the noise is coming from the inductor itself or from a ceramic capacitor's piezoelectric effect?
- If the regulator's light-load mode is the cause, what trade-offs would you weigh before disabling it?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware POST is to verify that the analog signal chain is actually functional before the firmware starts trusting its readings — so the test should exercise the parts of the chain that firmware can't see: the reference, the amplifier's gain and offset, the ADC's ability to convert a known input, and the integrity of the supply rails. If the firmware is the only thing checking the system, a failed reference or a shorted amplifier input can look like a plausible reading, and the device may proceed with bad data.

A practical approach is to inject a known stimulus into the front-end and check that the digitized result falls within an expected window. The stimulus can come from a precision reference or a divided-down rail, switched in through an analog mux or a series switch, so the normal sensor path is bypassed during the test. The test should cover at least two points — a zero-scale or near-zero input and a mid-scale or full-scale input — so that both offset and gain errors are detectable, not just a stuck-at-zero or stuck-at-rail failure. The comparison logic can live in a small comparator or window detector, or the result can be latched and read by firmware, but the key is that the pass/fail decision is based on a hardware-generated stimulus and a hardware-defined window, not on firmware interpreting an arbitrary reading.

I'd also want the POST to verify the supply rails independently — a rail monitor or supervisor that asserts a fault if a rail is out of tolerance — and to check the reference voltage against a known-good secondary reference or a ratiometric comparison. The test should be designed so that a failure produces a deterministic, safe state: the device either refuses to start or flags a fault that the firmware must handle, rather than silently continuing. And I'd want the test to be verifiable — the stimulus and the window should be traceable to the measurement requirements, so that during design verification you can demonstrate the POST actually catches the failure modes it's meant to catch.

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** The disagreement is really about an assumption, not about the connector itself: I'm assuming the cable is mated once and stays put, and the reliability engineer is assuming it will be replaced in the field. Before defending either position, I'd want to make that assumption explicit and check it against the actual use case — the intended clinical workflow, the instructions for use, and any field service or replacement procedure. If the device is genuinely single-mate in normal use, the reliability engineer's concern may be about a scenario that isn't in the intended use; if the cable is expected to be replaced, then my assumption is wrong and the connector needs to change.

I'd bring the question back to the design inputs rather than arguing from preference. If the intended use says the cable is connected once, I'd document that and ask whether the reliability engineer has a specific field scenario in mind that contradicts it — sometimes there's a real scenario I haven't considered, like a cable that gets damaged and needs replacement, or a device that's shared between patients and the cable is swapped. If the scenario is real, then the higher-cycle connector is justified and I'd support the change, because a connector that fails in the field is a worse outcome than a higher component cost.

If we can't resolve it from the intended use alone, I'd propose a middle path: quantify the actual mating-cycle requirement from the use case, then compare both connectors against that number rather than against each other. That turns a subjective argument into a specification question. I'd also want to know whether the higher-cycle connector introduces any other issues — different footprint, different mating force, different sealing — because those could affect the mechanical design or the assembly process. The goal is to make the decision on the basis of the requirement, not on who feels more strongly about it, and to document the rationale so it's traceable if the question comes up again during verification.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you document the mating-cycle assumption so it's traceable in the design history file?
- If the reliability engineer's scenario turns out to be valid but the higher-cycle connector doesn't fit the enclosure, how would you approach the trade-off?
