# hardware-design — Day 121

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a boost converter's inductor when the load has fast current transients, and what parameters would you check to make sure the design stays robust across the full load range?

**Answer:** I'd start by establishing the operating envelope: minimum and maximum input voltage, output voltage, maximum DC load, and — critically for a transient-heavy load — the peak current magnitude, pulse width, and repetition rate. The inductor value itself comes from the standard relationship between ripple current, switching frequency, and duty cycle, but I'd size it against the worst-case duty cycle (usually minimum input, maximum output) rather than nominal, because that's where peak inductor current is highest.

For a fast-transient load, the inductor's saturation current rating is the parameter I'd scrutinize hardest. The datasheet's I_sat is typically specified at 25°C and at a defined drop in inductance — often 10–30% — so I'd derate it for the actual operating temperature and check the saturation curve, not just the headline number. I'd also look at the I_sat-versus-temperature curve, because ferrite materials lose saturation margin as they heat. The steady-state peak current plus half the ripple is the design point, but I'd also verify the converter's current-limit threshold sits comfortably below I_sat so a transient can't drive the core into hard saturation before the loop reacts.

Beyond saturation, DCR matters for efficiency and thermal rise, and the core loss at the chosen switching frequency matters more than people expect at high frequency. I'd also check the self-resonant frequency — for a boost running at a few hundred kHz to a couple MHz, an inductor with a low SRF can behave capacitively and wreck the ripple waveform. Finally, I'd confirm the inductor's thermal rating against the RMS current, not the average, since the ripple current contributes to heating.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether the transient load requires more output capacitance versus a larger inductor?
- What bench measurements would confirm the inductor isn't saturating under the worst-case transient?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision hinges on what the ADC's accuracy budget can tolerate, and the honest starting point is that a filtered supply rail is rarely good enough for a high-resolution converter unless the measurement is ratiometric — that is, the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, so supply drift cancels in the ratio. If the design is ratiometric, a filtered rail can be a legitimate and cheaper choice. If it isn't, a dedicated reference is almost always the right call.

For a reference IC, the parameters I'd actually weigh are initial accuracy, temperature coefficient, long-term drift, and — often overlooked — output noise and the reference's own PSRR. Initial accuracy can be calibrated out at manufacturing, so it's usually the least important of the four; tempco and long-term drift are what you can't calibrate away in the field. Output noise matters because it appears directly in the conversion result, so I'd compare the reference's noise density against the ADC's resolution — at 16 bits on a 2.5V reference, one LSB is roughly 38 µV, so a reference with tens of µV of low-frequency noise is already eating into the budget.

I'd also check the reference's output capacitor requirements and stability, its dropout headroom from the available supply, and whether it needs a buffer for a switched-capacitor SAR input. For a filtered rail, I'd want to know the rail's ripple and noise at the ADC's sampling bandwidth, the filter's attenuation at the switching frequency, and how the rail behaves during load transients — a reference that sags when the digital load pulses is worse than useless.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you verify on the bench that the reference, not the ADC, is limiting your effective resolution?
- What changes if the ADC is a delta-sigma rather than a SAR?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate conduction loss from switching loss, because "gate voltage looks correct" only rules out the obvious DC problem. Conduction loss is I²R, so at 2A even a modest on-resistance produces real heat — I'd calculate the expected dissipation from the datasheet R_DS(on) at the actual junction temperature (not 25°C) and compare it against the measured case temperature and the thermal resistance to ambient. If the numbers don't reconcile, something is off.

The usual culprits: the MOSFET isn't fully enhanced — the gate voltage may measure correct at DC, but if the gate driver can't supply the charge quickly, or there's a gate resistor that's too large, the device spends time in the linear region on every edge. For a resistive load switched at any meaningful frequency, that transition loss adds up fast. I'd scope the gate-source voltage and the drain-source voltage simultaneously to see how long the device is in the linear region per transition, and check whether the gate waveform has a plateau or slow tail.

Other things I'd check: whether the part is actually the one specified (a substitute with higher R_DS(on) or a different package), whether the thermal pad is properly soldered to the copper pour, whether the PCB copper area is adequate for the dissipation, and whether there's any shoot-through or body-diode conduction I'm not accounting for. I'd also verify the load is truly resistive — a load with inductance would add voltage spikes and avalanche events that heat the die.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish gate-charge-limited switching loss from a thermal-path problem?
- What would you change in the design if the switching frequency had to double?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a ferrite bead for a power rail that supplies both a sensitive analog sensor drawing a small steady current and a digital microcontroller drawing larger current with fast transient spikes?

**Answer:** The trap with ferrite beads is treating them as ideal inductors. They're lossy at high frequency, and their impedance is specified at a test frequency — usually 100 MHz — which tells you almost nothing about behavior at the frequencies that matter for a power rail. So I'd start by pulling the impedance-versus-frequency curve and looking at the whole profile, not the headline number.

The key concern with a shared rail is that the bead sits in series with the digital load's transient current. When the microcontroller draws a fast spike, that current has to come from somewhere, and if the only path is through the bead, the bead's impedance at the transient's frequency band creates a voltage droop that the analog sensor sees. So I'd size the local decoupling on the digital side to supply the transient locally, and place the bead so it isolates the analog branch rather than sitting between the supply and both loads. The DC resistance matters too — a bead with high DCR drops voltage and dissipates power under the steady analog current.

I'd also check the bead's rated current against the combined load with margin, and its impedance at the frequencies where the digital load's harmonics live, not just at 100 MHz. And I'd watch for resonance: a ferrite bead plus the decoupling capacitors on either side forms a resonant tank, and if that resonance lands in a band where the digital load has energy, it can amplify rather than attenuate. Damping that resonance — sometimes with a small series resistor or a lossy capacitor — is often the difference between a bead that helps and one that makes things worse.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you measure whether the bead is actually attenuating noise on the analog rail?
- What would you do if the bead's resonance coincided with the microcontroller's clock harmonic?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a legitimate cost conversation rather than a turf battle, because manufacturing's concern is real — surface finish is a per-board cost that scales with volume, and they're right to flag it. My job is to make sure the decision is made on evidence rather than on unit price alone.

I'd start by laying out the specific technical requirements the finish has to meet: the BGA's pitch and pad geometry, the coplanarity needed for reliable joints across the array, the solderability and shelf-life requirements, and whether the ADC's performance is sensitive to anything the finish affects — for instance, whether there's any concern about intermetallic formation or surface roughness under the fine-pitch parts. Then I'd ask manufacturing to bring the actual cost delta and the specific alternative they're proposing, so we're comparing real numbers rather than assumptions.

From there I'd look for the middle ground. There may be a finish that's cheaper than what I specified but still meets the BGA and ADC requirements — the space isn't binary. If the cheaper option genuinely can't meet the requirements, I'd want that documented with the supplier's data and, ideally, a small build or cross-section to demonstrate it, so the decision is grounded in evidence the whole team can see. If it can meet them, I should be the first to accept the saving. Either way, I'd want the outcome captured in the design record so the rationale is traceable later.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you structure a small evaluation build to settle the question without delaying the schedule?
- What would you do if manufacturing escalated the cost concern above your level?
