# hardware-design — Day 88

## Q1: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?

**Answer:** The guiding principle is that the POST should exercise the analog chain using known, on-board stimuli and compare against expected windows, so that a failure is detected even if the ADC or firmware is misbehaving. I'd structure it around a few independent checks. First, rail monitoring: a supervisor or comparator network that confirms each critical rail is within tolerance before the system is allowed to enable the signal path — this is deliberately separate from any ADC reading. Second, reference verification: switch the ADC input to a known internal reference or a precision divider and confirm the conversion lands within a tight window; this catches reference drift, ADC gain errors, and stuck codes. Third, a signal-path loopback: inject a known DC or low-frequency stimulus at the front-end input (via a mux or a calibration DAC) and verify the conditioned output at the ADC is within an expected band — this validates the amplifier, filter, and ADC together. Fourth, a continuity or bias-current check on sensor connections where practical. The key design decisions are: keep the stimulus source and the pass/fail thresholds in hardware where the risk justifies it (comparators and latches rather than firmware comparisons), make the test non-invasive so it doesn't perturb normal operation, and ensure a failed POST drives a safe state rather than just logging an error. I'd also want the POST to be fast enough not to delay clinical use unacceptably, and to be documented as part of the risk file so each check traces to a hazard it mitigates.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide which checks must be pure hardware versus which can safely be firmware-driven? What would you do if a POST check is marginal — passing but close to its threshold — and how would that feed into the device's fault handling?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting the input filter for a switching regulator, and how would you decide whether a simple capacitor is enough versus needing an LC or π-filter?

**Answer:** I'd start from what the filter is actually protecting against and what it must not disturb. The first question is whether the concern is conducted emissions back onto the input supply, ripple feeding forward into a sensitive load, or both — because those push the design in different directions. A single input capacitor handles high-frequency ripple current by providing a local low-impedance source, but it does nothing for lower-frequency ripple or for isolating the regulator's switching noise from upstream circuitry. If the load is sensitive or the input rail is shared, I'd move to an LC or π-filter. The design steps: estimate the ripple current the regulator draws (a function of duty cycle, inductor ripple, and load), choose the capacitor for RMS current rating and ESR at the switching frequency, then add the inductor to set the filter corner well below the switching frequency but well above any control-loop bandwidth so I don't destabilize the regulator. A π-filter (cap–inductor–cap) gives better attenuation and damps the resonance better than a single LC. Critical practical points: the filter's resonant peak can actually amplify noise near its corner if undamped, so I'd add damping (a series RC across the cap, or a lossy ferrite instead of an ideal inductor); I'd check the filter's output impedance against the regulator's input impedance to avoid Middlebrook-type instability; and I'd verify with a network analyzer or at least a scope on the input under worst-case load steps. For a purely local decoupling need at a single IC, a well-placed capacitor is often enough; for a shared rail feeding analog circuitry, I'd default to a damped LC or π.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you damp the filter's resonance without hurting efficiency or adding significant cost? What would you measure on the bench to confirm the filter isn't causing instability at the regulator input?

## Q3: How would you approach choosing between a voltage-mode and a current-mode control architecture for a buck converter that must supply a fast-transient digital load while also feeding a sensitive analog rail through a downstream LDO?

**Answer:** I'd frame it around what each architecture is good at and what the downstream LDO changes. Current-mode control gives inherent cycle-by-cycle inductor current limiting, better line-transient rejection, and a simpler compensation network (a single dominant pole plus a zero), and it handles the fast-transient digital load well because the inner current loop responds quickly to load steps. Its weaknesses are noise sensitivity on the current-sense signal and the need for slope compensation above 50% duty cycle. Voltage-mode control has a cleaner, less noise-sensitive feedback path and handles wide duty-cycle ranges without slope compensation, but it needs a more complex type-III compensation network and responds more slowly to line transients. The presence of the downstream LDO is the deciding factor: the LDO provides excellent PSRR at low frequency and cleans up the buck's residual ripple, so the analog rail doesn't see the buck's switching noise directly — which relaxes the buck's output ripple requirement and lets me optimize it for transient response and efficiency rather than for a pristine output. That tilts me toward current-mode for the fast digital load, with the LDO handling the analog cleanliness. I'd still verify the LDO's PSRR at the buck's switching frequency (PSRR rolls off with frequency, so a 1 MHz buck may not be well rejected), and I'd check that the buck's control loop is stable across the load range including the LDO's relatively constant draw. If the LDO's high-frequency PSRR were poor, I'd reconsider and possibly add a post-filter or choose a lower switching frequency.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the current-sense signal's noise sensitivity in a current-mode design? What would you check to confirm the LDO is actually rejecting the buck's ripple rather than passing it through?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?

**Answer:** The correlation with handling and enclosure installation points strongly at a mechanical or environmental root cause rather than a pure electrical design flaw, so I'd treat it as a systematic isolation problem. First, I'd reproduce it deliberately: flex the board, tap components, wiggle connectors and cables, and apply gentle pressure at suspect points while monitoring the failing signal — this converts an intermittent into something observable. Second, I'd narrow by subsystem: does the failure follow a particular connector, a particular board region, or a particular cable routing? I'd use a scope with a long record length or a logic analyzer in trigger-and-capture mode to catch the event, and if the failure is a reset or a communication dropout, I'd monitor the relevant rails and reset lines simultaneously. Common culprits I'd check in order: connector seating and contact fretting, solder joints on large or heavy components (especially connectors and crystals) that crack under flex, board flex stressing components near mounting holes, cables that rub or get pinched in the enclosure, insufficient clearance causing intermittent shorts, and crystals or oscillators whose startup margin degrades when the board is stressed or the temperature shifts inside the enclosure. I'd also check for ground-plane discontinuities or shield contact issues that only manifest when the enclosure is closed. The fix depends on the cause — a staked connector, a strain-relief change, a reflow profile or solder-joint rework, a mechanical redesign of a mounting point — but the discipline is the same: reproduce, isolate to a subsystem, then to a component or joint, then verify the fix under the same handling and enclosure conditions that exposed it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cold solder joint from a connector contact problem when both produce intermittent behavior? What would you add to the design or the test procedure to catch this class of failure before it reaches the field?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements question first, not a component argument, because the disagreement is really about an unstated assumption: how many mating cycles will the device actually see? I'd start by getting the intended use and the field-service model on the table — is the cable truly single-connect, or is there a realistic path where a clinician or technician disconnects and reconnects it during cleaning, transport, or replacement? I'd pull the reliability engineer's concern into that discussion rather than dismissing it, because "intended to be connected once" is an assumption that needs to be validated against actual use, not just the design intent. Then I'd quantify: the connector's rated cycle count versus the realistic worst-case cycle count over the device life, with margin. If the numbers show the current connector is genuinely adequate with margin, I'd document that rationale and the assumption it rests on, and propose that the assumption be captured in the risk file and verified in use. If the numbers are marginal or the use case is uncertain, I'd accept that the reliability engineer has a legitimate point and evaluate the higher-cycle connector on total cost — not just unit price but field-failure cost, service burden, and regulatory risk. I'd also look for a middle path: a connector in the same family with a higher cycle rating at modest cost increase, or a design change that reduces the number of cycles the connector actually sees. The goal is a decision both of us can defend in a design review and in the risk documentation, not winning the argument.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document a decision to keep the lower-cycle connector so it holds up under regulatory scrutiny? If the higher-cycle connector were adopted, how would you assess whether it introduces any new risks (size, mating force, contact resistance)?
