# hardware-design — Day 101

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the switching frequency for a buck converter that must supply a sensitive analog rail, where the output ripple must stay below a few millivolts and the converter sits close to the analog front-end on the same board?

**Answer:** I'd start by recognizing that this is a case where the "obvious" answer — push the switching frequency high to shrink the inductor and output cap — can actually make the analog problem worse, so the decision has to be made jointly across ripple, EMI, efficiency, and layout coupling rather than on the converter alone.

The first axis is ripple. Output ripple in a buck has a capacitive term (ΔI through the output cap ESR and capacitance) and an inductive term (the triangular current ripple set by inductance and frequency). Raising the switching frequency lets you use a smaller inductor for the same current ripple, which shrinks the physical loop area and the radiated field — good for a board that sits next to an analog front-end. But the ripple amplitude itself doesn't automatically improve: at higher frequency the capacitive ripple term can dominate and the ESR/ESL of the output cap becomes the limiting factor, so you may need more or better capacitors, not fewer.

The second axis is where the switching noise lands relative to the analog signal. If the front-end has a defined passband (say a biopotential or pressure signal up to a few hundred Hz), I want the fundamental and its harmonics to fall well outside that band and, ideally, outside the ADC's sampling aperture so they don't alias down. A frequency in the hundreds of kHz to low MHz range is usually a reasonable compromise: high enough to keep the inductor small and the ripple manageable, low enough that efficiency and EMI are still controllable, and far enough above the signal band that a modest post-filter and the downstream LDO's PSRR can knock it down.

The third axis is EMI and efficiency. Higher frequency means higher switching losses and more radiated energy at higher harmonics, which complicates CISPR-style compliance and makes layout and shielding more critical. On a medical device that has to pass emissions testing, I'd rather not push frequency higher than the ripple and size targets actually require.

Practically, I'd shortlist two or three candidate frequencies, then verify with a bench measurement: scope the output ripple with a proper tip-and-barrel probe, measure the conducted and radiated spectrum near the analog section, and check the noise floor at the ADC input with the converter running versus bypassed. If the switching spurs show up in the measurement band, I'd either move the frequency, add a post-filter (ferrite plus cap, or an LC), or rely on a downstream LDO with good high-frequency PSRR — and confirm the LDO actually attenuates at the switching frequency rather than just at DC, since PSRR falls off with frequency.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a post-filter or a downstream LDO is the better way to clean up the switching ripple before the analog front-end? What layout practices would you use to keep the switching loop's field away from the analog section?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a P-channel MOSFET and a P-channel-plus-charge-pump (or an integrated load switch) for reverse-polarity protection on a battery input, and what failure modes would you check?

**Answer:** The core trade-off is on-resistance and gate-drive headroom versus simplicity and part count. A plain P-channel MOSFET in the high-side path is the simplest reverse-polarity scheme: source to the battery, drain to the load, gate pulled to ground through a resistor so the device is on in normal polarity and off when the battery is reversed. The catch is that the gate-source voltage available to enhance the FET is limited by the battery voltage itself. With a single Li-ion cell at 3.0–4.2V, a logic-level P-FET can work, but as the cell discharges toward 3.0V the available Vgs shrinks and Rds(on) rises, so conduction loss and heat go up exactly when the battery is weakest. That's the failure mode I'd check first: does the FET stay adequately enhanced across the full battery range, including the low end and cold temperature where threshold voltage shifts?

A P-channel plus charge pump (or an integrated load switch that includes one) solves the headroom problem by generating a gate voltage below the source, so the FET stays fully enhanced even at low battery voltage. The cost is extra complexity, a small quiescent current draw from the charge pump, and the pump's own switching noise — which matters on a battery-powered medical device that may be in a low-power sleep state for long periods. So the second failure mode is standby current: does the charge pump or load switch leak more than the sleep budget allows?

Other failure modes I'd check across both options: reverse leakage when the battery is inserted backwards (the FET body diode can conduct in the wrong direction depending on topology, so I'd confirm the body diode orientation actually blocks reverse current); inrush and SOA during hot-plug or battery insertion, where the input capacitance charges through the FET and the safe operating area has to cover that surge; and thermal behavior under continuous load at worst-case Rds(on). For an integrated load switch I'd also check its current-limit and thermal-shutdown behavior, since those can be either helpful protection or an unexpected nuisance trip depending on the load profile.

My decision rule: if the battery voltage is comfortably above the FET's enhancement requirement across the whole range and standby current is tight, the plain P-FET wins on simplicity and quiescent draw. If the rail is low, the load current is significant, or I need guaranteed low Rds(on) at end-of-discharge, the charge-pump or integrated switch is worth the complexity — provided its standby current fits the budget.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify reverse-polarity behavior on the bench without risking the rest of the board? What would make you add a series Schottky instead of a MOSFET, and what would you give up?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting the bulk and decoupling capacitor network for a microcontroller that has both a high-current digital core and a sensitive on-chip analog reference, and how would you decide how much capacitance is "enough"?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as two separate problems that share a ground plane, because the digital core and the analog reference have opposite needs: the core wants a low-impedance reservoir for fast current transients, and the reference wants a quiet, stable supply with minimal high-frequency disturbance.

For the digital core, the decoupling is about supplying transient current close to the pins so the current loop stays small. I'd follow the datasheet's recommended values as a starting point, then think in terms of impedance versus frequency: a mix of small ceramic capacitors (low ESL, effective at high frequency) placed right at the power pins, backed by a larger bulk capacitor that handles lower-frequency transient energy and stabilizes the rail. "Enough" is determined by the transient current the core draws and the allowable rail droop — I'd estimate the charge the core needs during a clock-edge burst, divide by the tolerable voltage dip, and check that the local capacitance plus the regulator's response covers it. Then I'd verify on the bench by measuring the rail with a short-ground-tip probe during worst-case code execution, not just at idle.

For the analog reference, the priority flips: I want the reference pin to see as little digital switching noise as possible. That usually means a dedicated RC or ferrite-plus-capacitor filter right at the reference pin, a local capacitor sized to the reference's recommended load capacitance (references are often sensitive to the exact value and ESR — too much or too little can affect stability or settling), and physical separation from the digital decoupling so the two don't share a noisy return path. I'd also check whether the reference has a specified output bypass requirement and honor it rather than guessing.

The "how much is enough" question I'd answer with a combination of datasheet guidance, a first-order charge/energy calculation for the transients, and then measurement. Over-decoupling isn't free: extra capacitors add cost, board area, inrush at power-up, and can create resonant peaks with the regulator or with ferrites if the network isn't damped. So I'd rather start from the datasheet, verify with a scope and a spectrum analyzer at the pins, and add capacitance only where the measurement shows a droop or a noise problem — not blanket-stuff the board.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you check for a resonant peak between a ferrite bead and the decoupling capacitors on a rail? What would you look for on the scope to tell you the bulk capacitor is doing its job versus the ceramics?

## Q4: Walk me through how you would debug a circuit where an op-amp's output is correct at DC but shows a slow, large-amplitude drift over minutes when the board is warmed by nearby power components.

**Answer:** A slow drift that only appears once the board warms points to a thermal effect, so my first move is to separate "the op-amp itself is drifting" from "something feeding the op-amp is drifting." I'd start by confirming the time constant: is the drift tracking the thermal time constant of the board (tens of seconds to minutes), or is it faster? That tells me whether I'm chasing a genuine thermal gradient or something like a capacitor soaking charge.

Then I'd localize it. I'd use a heat gun or a cold spray, or simply a finger on individual components, to see which part's temperature correlates with the drift — the op-amp, its feedback resistors, the input source, or a nearby regulator. If warming the op-amp alone reproduces the drift, I'd look at its offset voltage temperature coefficient and input bias current drift. If warming the feedback network reproduces it, I'd suspect resistor tempco mismatch or a thermocouple effect at a solder joint. If warming a nearby power component reproduces it, I'm probably seeing thermal EMF or a supply rail shifting.

The candidates I'd work through: input offset voltage drift (specified as µV/°C — over a 20–30°C rise this can be tens or hundreds of µV, which may be large relative to the signal); input bias current drift interacting with source impedance (bias current can double every ~10°C, so a high source impedance turns a small bias drift into a real voltage error); resistor tempco and mismatch in the feedback divider; thermoelectric EMF at dissimilar-metal junctions, which is small per junction but can matter at high gain; and supply-rail drift if the op-amp has finite PSRR and the nearby power components are heating the regulator. I'd also rule out a capacitor-related effect — dielectric absorption or leakage that changes with temperature — by checking whether the drift is monotonic with temperature or has a soak-and-recover signature.

To confirm, I'd measure the op-amp's inputs and output simultaneously while logging temperature, and compare the observed output drift against the predicted drift from the datasheet tempco numbers. If the measured drift matches the offset-voltage tempco prediction, it's the op-amp and I'd consider a lower-drift part or a chopper-stabilized amplifier. If it matches bias-current-times-source-impedance, I'd reduce the source impedance or pick a lower-bias-current part. If it's thermoelectric, I'd look at layout — symmetric thermal paths, avoiding junctions between dissimilar metals in the signal path.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish offset-voltage drift from bias-current drift experimentally? What layout or component choices reduce thermoelectric effects in a high-gain front-end?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the thermal engineer argues that your chosen linear regulator for a low-noise analog rail will dissipate too much power inside the sealed enclosure and proposes replacing it with a switching regulator followed by a small LDO. You believe the switching regulator's ripple and switching noise, even after the LDO, could degrade the analog front-end's noise floor. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a real engineering trade-off rather than a position to defend, because both concerns are legitimate — thermal margin in a sealed enclosure is a genuine safety and reliability issue, and analog noise floor is a genuine measurement-performance issue. The right move is to get both concerns quantified so the decision rests on data rather than assertion.

First I'd ask the thermal engineer to walk me through the numbers: what's the actual power dissipation of the linear regulator at worst case, what's the enclosure's thermal resistance, and what junction temperature does that predict against the part's rating and any derating we've committed to? If the margin is genuinely thin, that's a hard constraint I have to respect — I can't argue my way past a thermal violation.

Then I'd put my own concern on the same footing: what's the actual noise floor requirement for the analog front-end, and how much of the budget does the switching regulator's ripple and switching spurs actually consume after the LDO? Rather than assert that "switching noise will degrade the front-end," I'd propose to measure it — build or borrow a bench setup with the switching regulator plus LDO feeding a representative analog load, and measure the noise at the front-end output and the spurs in the band of interest. The LDO's PSRR falls off with frequency, so the real question is whether the residual switching ripple at the switching frequency and its harmonics lands in the measurement band or aliases into it, and whether it's above the front-end's own noise floor.

With both sets of numbers in hand, the options usually become concrete: keep the linear regulator but reduce its dissipation (lower input-to-output differential, a series resistor to drop some voltage before the regulator, or a different rail architecture); accept the switching regulator plus LDO if the measured noise is within budget; or use a switching regulator with a higher switching frequency and a well-chosen LDO whose PSRR is good at that frequency, plus layout and filtering to keep the switching loop away from the analog section. I'd bring the measured data back to the review and let the group choose among the options against the actual requirements, rather than have it be my preference versus the thermal engineer's.

The key is that I'd want the decision documented with the evidence — the thermal calculation and the noise measurement — so that whichever way it goes, the rationale is traceable in the design history file. That's as important for a medical device as getting the "right" answer.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the measured switching noise did land in the analog band, what would be your next options short of abandoning the switching regulator? How would you make sure the thermal and noise budgets stay linked as the design evolves?
