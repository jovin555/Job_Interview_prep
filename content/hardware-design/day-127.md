# hardware-design — Day 127

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a gate driver for a high-side N-channel MOSFET used as a load switch, where the source floats at the battery voltage and the switch must turn on slowly enough to limit inrush current but turn off fast enough to protect the load?

**Answer:** The core problem is that a high-side N-channel MOSFET needs a gate voltage above its source, and the source sits at the battery rail — so the driver has to generate a gate-source voltage that stays comfortably above the threshold (and ideally in the 8–10 V range for low RDS(on)) while the source itself moves. I'd start by deciding between a bootstrap-based driver, a charge-pump driver, and an integrated load-switch IC. A bootstrap driver is simple and cheap but requires switching activity to refresh the bootstrap capacitor, which is a poor fit for a load switch that may stay on for long periods — the cap can droop and the FET can drift into its linear region. A charge pump keeps the gate enhanced indefinitely, which is what you want for a static load switch.

For the turn-on/turn-off asymmetry, I'd separate the two paths: a larger series gate resistor (or a controlled current source in the driver) on the turn-on path to charge the gate slowly and limit inrush into the downstream capacitance, and a small resistor plus a Schottky diode on the turn-off path so the gate discharges quickly during a fault. I'd calculate the inrush from the load capacitance and the acceptable di/dt, then size the turn-on resistor from the gate charge curve — checking the FET's SOA to confirm it survives the inrush event without exceeding its safe operating area, since slow turn-on means the FET spends real time in the linear region dissipating power. On the turn-off side, I'd verify the discharge path can pull the gate down fast enough that the FET exits saturation before the fault current stresses it.

I'd also check the driver's absolute maximum ratings against the battery's full range, including any transient overshoot, and confirm the driver's undervoltage lockout behaves safely — you don't want the FET partially enhanced at low battery. Finally, I'd verify the whole thing on the bench with a current probe on the inrush event and a thermal measurement on the FET during repeated switching.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the FET stays within its safe operating area during a worst-case inrush event, and what would you measure? What happens to your bootstrap or charge-pump scheme if the battery voltage sags below the driver's UVLO during a heavy load transient?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting the switching frequency for a buck converter that must supply a sensitive analog rail, where the output ripple must stay below a few millivolts and the converter sits close to the analog front-end on the same board?

**Answer:** Switching frequency is a multi-way trade, and for a sensitive analog rail the trade is dominated by ripple, EMI coupling, and loop bandwidth rather than raw efficiency. Higher frequency shrinks the inductor and output capacitor for a given ripple, which helps board area and transient response — but it also pushes the switching harmonics up in frequency where they're more likely to couple into the analog front-end through radiated fields or the ground plane, and it increases switching losses. Lower frequency makes the ripple harder to filter and forces a larger inductor, which can hurt transient response and may put the fundamental closer to the analog signal band.

My approach would be to first establish the ripple budget: the output ripple is roughly ΔIL × ESR plus the capacitive term, so at a few millivolts the output capacitor's ESR and ESL often dominate, not the capacitance value. That means I'd pick a frequency where a reasonable ceramic output cap and inductor combination can meet the ripple target without heroic component sizes — typically somewhere in the few-hundred-kHz to ~1 MHz range for a small analog rail, but the exact number comes from the ripple and transient math, not a rule of thumb.

Then I'd layer on the analog-coupling constraint: I'd want the switching fundamental and its low harmonics to sit well away from the analog signal band and from any known susceptibility peaks, and I'd plan the layout so the hot loop is small and localized, with the converter's ground return kept separate from the analog ground until a single-point connection. If the fundamental still lands somewhere problematic, I'd consider synchronizing the converter to an external clock, or adding a post-filter or a downstream LDO to knock down the residual ripple. I'd verify on the bench with a spectrum analyzer on the analog rail and a near-field probe to see what's actually coupling.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a downstream LDO is enough to clean up the switching ripple, or whether you need a passive LC post-filter as well? What layout practices would you use to keep the converter's hot loop from coupling into the analog front-end?

## Q3: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision comes down to how much of the ADC's error budget you're willing to spend on the reference, and whether the supply rail can meet that budget across temperature and time. A dedicated reference IC gives you specified initial accuracy, a guaranteed temperature coefficient, and — critically — low output noise and good long-term drift, all of which are characterized and guaranteed. A filtered supply rail is cheaper and saves a component, but its accuracy and drift are whatever the upstream regulator delivers, and its noise is the regulator's noise plus whatever the filter passes. For a high-resolution converter, the reference error maps almost directly into conversion error, so the reference often ends up being the dominant term in the error budget.

The datasheet parameters I'd actually weigh: initial accuracy (in % or LSB), temperature coefficient (ppm/°C — this usually dominates over a medical device's 0–50 °C range), long-term drift, and output noise (both low-frequency 0.1–10 Hz noise and wideband noise, since the ADC's reference input sees both). I'd also look at the reference's output impedance and its load regulation, because the ADC's reference input draws switched charge during conversion — a reference that can't recover quickly from those charge kicks will show conversion-to-conversion variation. Line regulation matters if the rail feeding the reference isn't well controlled. And I'd check the reference's dropout and quiescent current if it's a battery-powered design.

Then I'd do the arithmetic: convert each error term into LSBs at the target resolution, sum them, and see whether the filtered rail leaves enough margin for the rest of the signal chain. If the rail's drift alone eats most of the budget, the reference IC is justified. I'd also confirm the reference's noise is low enough that it doesn't set the system noise floor, and that its output can be adequately decoupled without destabilizing it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you characterize a reference's noise on the bench, and what would you compare it against? If you chose the filtered rail, what would you do to verify its drift over temperature is acceptable?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?

**Answer:** The correlation with handling and movement points strongly at a mechanical or intermittent-connection root cause, so I'd resist the temptation to chase firmware or component tolerances first. I'd start by reproducing the failure in a controlled way: mount the board in the enclosure, then apply deliberate mechanical stimulus — flexing the board, tapping near connectors, wiggling cables, and pressing on specific areas — while monitoring the failing signal or rail. The goal is to localize which physical action triggers the fault, which narrows the search enormously.

From there I'd work through the usual suspects in order of likelihood: connector and cable interfaces (crimp quality, contact retention, strain relief, whether the cable is being pulled at the connector), solder joints on large or heavy components (BGAs, connectors, inductors) that may have marginal wetting or voids, board flex causing cracked joints or cracked MLCCs, and fasteners or shielding cans that short or press on components when the enclosure is closed. I'd use a current-limited bench supply and a scope with a long record length or a trigger on the fault condition so I can capture what happens at the moment of failure — a rail glitch, a reset, a signal dropout. If I suspect a specific joint, I'd try to confirm with a thermal camera (a marginal joint often runs warmer), a gentle flex test with the scope probing across the suspect net, or X-ray/CT if available.

I'd also check whether the enclosure itself changes the electrical environment — added capacitance to ground, a shield can shorting to a component, or a cable routed differently than on the bench. Once I have a hypothesis, I'd confirm it by fixing the suspected cause and re-running the same mechanical stimulus to see if the failure disappears. The fix might be mechanical (strain relief, potting, a different connector) rather than electrical, which is why it's important not to assume the problem is in the schematic.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cracked solder joint from a cracked ceramic capacitor as the root cause? What design changes would you make to prevent this class of failure in a future revision?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a cost-versus-risk trade that needs to be resolved with data rather than assertion, and I'd want to keep the relationship constructive — the manufacturing engineer's cost concern is legitimate and worth taking seriously. My first move would be to make sure we're comparing the same thing: get the actual cost delta per board, including any downstream test, rework, or yield implications, so the discussion is grounded in real numbers rather than a general impression that one finish is "expensive."

Then I'd lay out the technical risk case specifically. For the BGA, the concern is coplanarity and joint reliability — a finish that isn't flat and uniform can lead to voids, non-wet opens, or head-in-pillow defects that are hard to detect and may only show up as intermittent failures in the field, which for a medical device is a serious reliability and regulatory problem. For the high-resolution ADC, the concern is surface uniformity affecting solder joint consistency and, in some cases, the electrical performance of fine-pitch parts. I'd bring the assembly house into the conversation, because they're the ones who actually have to build it — their process capability and their recommendation carry a lot of weight and can settle the question objectively.

I'd also explore whether there's a middle path: a different finish that meets the flatness requirement at lower cost, or a change to the board design (pad geometry, stencil, reflow profile) that makes a cheaper finish viable without compromising joint quality. If after that review the cheaper finish still carries unacceptable risk, I'd document the decision and the rationale — this is exactly the kind of trade that belongs in the design history file for a Class II device. If the manufacturing engineer still disagrees, I'd escalate to the appropriate technical authority with the data from both sides rather than let it become a personal standoff.

**Possible follow-ups:** What data would you want from the assembly house before making the final call? How would you document this decision so it stands up to a regulatory audit?

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify on the bench that the chosen finish actually delivers the joint quality you expect?
