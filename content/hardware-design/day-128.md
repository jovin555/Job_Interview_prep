# hardware-design — Day 128

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the feedback resistor values for a transimpedance amplifier front-end, and what trade-offs would drive the choice between a high-value and a low-value feedback resistor?

**Answer:** The transimpedance amplifier converts a sensor's photocurrent or leakage current into a voltage, with the transimpedance gain set by the feedback resistor Rf. The core trade-off is noise versus bandwidth and stability. A high-value Rf gives more gain per unit of input current, which improves the signal-to-noise ratio relative to the amplifier's input-referred voltage noise — but it also increases the Johnson noise contribution of the resistor itself (proportional to √Rf), raises the closed-loop bandwidth limitation imposed by the feedback pole formed with the photodiode capacitance and the amplifier's input capacitance, and makes the circuit more sensitive to stray capacitance and bias current errors. A low-value Rf gives wider bandwidth and lower resistor thermal noise in absolute terms, but the signal is smaller and the amplifier's input voltage noise and offset become a larger fraction of the output.

My approach: start from the required output swing and the sensor's full-scale current to set the nominal Rf, then check that the resulting bandwidth (roughly 1/(2π·Rf·Ctotal)) meets the signal bandwidth requirement, where Ctotal includes the photodiode junction capacitance, the amplifier input capacitance, and stray layout capacitance. Then do a noise budget: compare the resistor's thermal noise, the amplifier's input current noise multiplied by Rf, and the input voltage noise — the dominant term depends on the source capacitance and the resistor value. I'd also check stability: the pole formed by Rf and the total input capacitance creates a phase lag, so a small feedback capacitor in parallel with Rf is usually needed to compensate and prevent peaking or oscillation. Finally, I'd check the amplifier's input bias current — with a high Rf, even nanoamps of bias current produce a significant offset, so a CMOS or JFET-input amplifier is often preferred for high-Rf designs. The choice is really a system-level optimization, not a single-component decision.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you calculate the value of the feedback capacitor that compensates the input pole, and what would you measure on the bench to confirm the circuit is stable? What happens to the noise budget if the sensor's own capacitance is large and varies with the measurand?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting the switching frequency for a buck converter that must supply a sensitive analog rail, where the output ripple must stay below a few millivolts and the converter sits close to the analog front-end on the same board?

**Answer:** Switching frequency is a multi-way trade-off between ripple, efficiency, solution size, and EMI. Higher frequency shrinks the inductor and output capacitor for a given ripple, which helps meet a tight ripple spec in a small area — but it increases switching losses, can reduce efficiency, and pushes the fundamental and its harmonics into frequency bands that are harder to filter and more likely to couple into a sensitive analog front-end. Lower frequency improves efficiency and moves the switching fundamental lower, but requires a larger inductor and more output capacitance to hold ripple down, and the lower fundamental can fall closer to the signal band of interest.

For a sensitive analog rail, my reasoning would be: first, estimate the ripple requirement and work out the LC product needed at candidate frequencies — the ripple current in the inductor scales inversely with frequency for a given inductance, so a higher frequency lets me use a smaller inductor and still meet ripple. Second, consider where the switching harmonics land relative to the analog signal bandwidth and any known interference-sensitive frequencies; I'd want the fundamental and low harmonics well away from the signal band and ideally above it, so a passive filter can attenuate them effectively. Third, consider EMI: a higher switching frequency with fast edges can radiate more, and the layout and shielding become more critical. Fourth, consider the converter's own control loop — many integrated regulators have a minimum on-time or a maximum duty-cycle limitation that constrains the usable frequency range at the required input/output ratio.

In practice I'd shortlist two or three frequencies, simulate or bench-test the ripple and the conducted emissions at each, and look at the actual noise coupling into the analog front-end rather than just the rail ripple — the rail can meet spec while still injecting noise through ground or through the supply pins of the analog devices. Synchronizing the converter to an external clock or choosing a frequency that avoids beating with other switching sources on the board is often part of the answer.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the residual ripple at the analog rail is acceptable — what would you measure at the ADC input rather than at the rail? What layout practices reduce the coupling of switching noise into a nearby analog front-end?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the gate voltage and load current both look right but the device is hot, the first thing I'd do is separate conduction loss from switching loss and from thermal-path problems. Conduction loss is I²·Rds(on), so I'd check the actual Rds(on) at the device's junction temperature — Rds(on) rises significantly with temperature, and a part chosen at 25°C may be dissipating much more than expected once it's warm. I'd also verify the gate drive is actually reaching the gate: a "correct" voltage measured at the driver output can be very different from the voltage at the gate pin if there's a series gate resistor, a long trace, or a driver that can't source enough current during the switching transition. A MOSFET that's only partially enhanced operates in the linear region and dissipates far more than the datasheet's on-resistance would suggest.

Next I'd look at switching loss. Even a "resistive load" switched at a modest frequency can have significant loss if the rise and fall times are slow — for example, if the gate charge is large relative to the driver's current, or if there's no gate resistor and the Miller plateau is prolonged. I'd scope the gate-source voltage and the drain-source voltage simultaneously to see the actual switching transition times and check for ringing or slow edges. I'd also check whether the load is truly resistive at the switching frequency — a load with any inductance will produce voltage spikes at turn-off that can push the device into avalanche or increase loss.

Then the thermal path: I'd verify the thermal resistance from junction to ambient, check the PCB copper area under the drain tab, look for thermal vias, and confirm the device isn't sitting in a hot spot near other dissipating components. A part that's electrically fine can still run hot if the layout gives it nowhere to dump heat. Finally, I'd check for oscillation — a MOSFET can oscillate at high frequency if the gate circuit is poorly damped, and that shows up as heat without an obvious current signature. The systematic approach is to measure Vgs, Vds, Id, and case temperature together, then attribute the loss to conduction, switching, or thermal resistance before changing anything.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish gate ringing from slow switching on a scope, and what would you change in each case? If the loss turns out to be dominated by Rds(on) at temperature, how would you go about selecting a better device?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?

**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware-based POST is to verify that the analog signal chain is alive and within tolerance before the firmware trusts its readings — and critically, to do so in a way that doesn't depend on the ADC or the firmware being correct, since those are exactly the things that could fail. So the design should inject known stimuli and check the response with independent comparators or monitors, not just read back through the same ADC path.

Concretely, I'd want the POST to verify several things. First, rail integrity: independent rail monitors (comparators or supervisor ICs) that confirm each supply is within its window, including the analog reference. Second, reference integrity: a check that the voltage reference is producing its expected value, again with an independent comparator rather than the ADC. Third, signal-chain continuity: inject a known test signal — a divided-down reference, a precision current into the front-end, or a switchable calibration source — at the input of the analog chain and verify that the output of the chain reaches an expected window, using a comparator at the chain output. This catches an open sensor, a dead amplifier, or a broken trace. Fourth, offset and gain sanity: a two-point check (e.g., zero-scale and a known mid-scale stimulus) that confirms the chain's transfer function is within a coarse window, which catches gross gain or offset failures even if it doesn't replace a full calibration.

The key design principle is independence: the POST's pass/fail decision should be made by hardware that shares as little as possible with the normal measurement path. That means separate comparators, a separate reference divider, and a latch or status register that the firmware reads but cannot override. I'd also want the POST to be fail-safe — if a check can't be performed (e.g., a switch fails to close), the default should be a fault indication, not a silent pass. And I'd want the test stimuli to be disconnected or bypassed during normal operation so they don't load the signal chain or inject noise.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you keep the POST's test signal injection from adding noise or leakage to the signal chain during normal operation? How would you verify that the POST itself is working — i.e., that it would actually catch a fault if one occurred?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a decision that needs to be made on evidence rather than on cost alone, and I'd want to bring the manufacturing engineer into the reasoning rather than just defending my choice. First, I'd make sure I understand their cost concern precisely — is it the finish cost itself, the yield impact, the supplier availability, or something about the assembly process? Sometimes the cost argument is really about a downstream problem I haven't considered.

Then I'd lay out the technical requirements that drove the original choice. For a high-density BGA, the finish affects solder joint reliability and voiding — a finish with poor coplanarity or uneven thickness can cause opens or weak joints that are hard to inspect and may fail in the field. For a high-resolution ADC, the concern is surface uniformity and, depending on the finish, potential effects on high-frequency performance and long-term reliability of fine-pitch joints. I'd want to quantify what "cheaper" actually buys and what risk it introduces: what's the cost delta per board, and what's the expected yield or field-failure impact if the cheaper finish causes joint defects? For a Class II medical device, a field failure has regulatory and patient-safety consequences that dwarf a per-board cost saving, so the risk-adjusted comparison usually favors the more robust finish unless the cheaper option can be shown to meet the same reliability bar.

I'd also look for a middle path: is there a finish that's cheaper than my original choice but still meets the BGA and ADC requirements? Or can the cost be recovered elsewhere — panelization, supplier negotiation, or a different board house? The goal is to reach a decision the whole team owns, documented with the rationale, rather than to win the argument. If after that review the evidence still supports the original finish, I'd escalate the decision with the data to the project lead so it's made at the right level, and if the cheaper finish is chosen, I'd want the reliability and test plan to explicitly cover the risks it introduces.

**Possible follow-ups:** What data would you want from the assembly house or a reliability test to settle whether the cheaper finish is acceptable? If the decision went against you, how would you make sure the risk was documented and covered in verification?

**Possible follow-ups (Q5):** How would you frame the cost-versus-risk trade-off for a project manager who is focused on the schedule and budget? What would you do if the manufacturing engineer's proposal came with a supplier quote showing a large saving and the project was already over budget?
