# hardware-design — Day 86

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?
**Answer:** Start by translating the system requirement into a frequency-tolerance budget over the full operating temperature range, then decide whether a standard crystal, a TCXO, or an overtone/AT-cut variant is needed. The key parameters are the crystal's frequency tolerance at 25°C, its temperature coefficient (often expressed as a parabolic curve around the turnover point), aging, and the load-capacitance sensitivity — because the effective load capacitance seen by the crystal shifts the oscillation frequency. I'd match the crystal's specified load capacitance to the actual PCB and MCU pin capacitance, then choose the two load caps so their series combination plus stray capacitance equals the specified value. On the bench I'd verify startup margin across temperature (cold-start at the low end is the usual failure), measure the actual oscillation frequency with a frequency counter or a scope with a good timebase, and check drive level against the crystal's maximum rating to avoid over-driving and long-term degradation. For tight accuracy I'd also confirm the MCU's internal load-cap trim, if available, and characterize the pullability so firmware can compensate if needed.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a startup-margin problem from a load-capacitance mismatch when the oscillator works at room temperature but not cold? What would you check if the measured frequency is correct at 25°C but drifts more than the crystal's spec over temperature?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a precision voltage reference circuit for a 16-bit SAR ADC that must maintain accuracy within ±1 LSB over a 0–50°C temperature range, given a 3.3V supply and a 2.5V reference voltage?
**Answer:** At 16 bits with a 2.5V reference, 1 LSB is about 38 µV, so the reference's initial accuracy, temperature drift, and noise all have to fit inside that budget with margin. I'd start by picking a reference whose initial tolerance plus drift over 0–50°C stays well under half an LSB, then look at its output noise in the ADC's bandwidth — low-frequency 1/f noise and wideband noise both matter because the reference directly modulates the conversion result. The reference needs a clean supply: I'd feed it from a low-noise LDO or an RC/ferrite-filtered rail, and add a bulk plus a small film cap close to the reference pin to absorb transient load current from the ADC's sampling. Layout matters — the reference ground should be a quiet analog ground, and the reference trace should be short and away from switching nodes. I'd also consider whether the ADC's internal reference buffer is adequate or whether an external buffer is needed to drive the switched-capacitor load without droop. Finally, I'd verify by measuring the reference's drift and noise over temperature and confirming the ADC's code spread stays within the target.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you separate reference noise from ADC noise when both contribute to the observed code spread? What would you do if the reference's specified drift is marginal but the measured drift over your temperature range is acceptable?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The point of a hardware POST is to verify the analog signal chain without relying on the ADC or the application firmware, so a failure can't be masked by the very components being tested. I'd design it to check three things: that each supply rail is within tolerance (using comparators or a supervisor with window detection), that the precision reference is present and at the correct value (a comparator against a divided version of a known-good reference, or a window comparator), and that the signal chain has continuity — for example, injecting a known test signal at the front end and confirming a response appears at a test point, or checking that the sensor excitation current is flowing. The results would be latched into a status register or a hardware fault line that the firmware reads at startup, and any failure would prevent the device from entering its therapeutic mode. I'd want the POST to be independent of the ADC so a dead ADC doesn't hide a dead front end, and I'd want the checks to be deterministic and fast enough not to delay startup unacceptably. The design also needs to be verifiable — I'd document the expected pass/fail conditions and test them by injecting faults.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you avoid false failures from normal startup transients or supply ramp timing? How would you decide which checks belong in hardware versus firmware?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?
**Answer:** Intermittent, motion-correlated failures usually point to a mechanical or connection issue rather than a purely electrical one, so I'd start by narrowing down whether it's a physical connection, a grounding/shielding change, or a thermal effect from the enclosure. First I'd try to reproduce it deliberately — flex the board, tap the enclosure, and use a vibration or handling fixture while monitoring the failing signal. If it reproduces, I'd look for the usual suspects: a connector or board-to-board interface with marginal contact, a cold or cracked solder joint, a component that's mechanically stressed by the enclosure, or a cable that's pinched or routed against a sharp edge. I'd also check whether the enclosure changes the grounding or shielding — for example, a metal enclosure can shift the antenna's tuning or couple noise differently. If it's thermal, I'd use a thermal camera or a hot-air pencil to localize. Once I have a suspect, I'd confirm with a targeted measurement (scope on the suspect node while flexing) and then fix it at the source — rework the joint, add strain relief, or change the mechanical design — rather than adding a filter to hide it.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cold solder joint from a connector contact issue without destructive teardown? What would you check if the failure only appears after the device has been running for a while?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements question rather than a preference, because the right answer depends on the actual use case, not on who feels more strongly. First I'd ask the reliability engineer to share the mating-cycle assumption they're using and where it comes from — is it based on a field-replacement scenario, a service procedure, or a worst-case user behavior? Then I'd check the device's intended use and the risk file: if the cable is genuinely single-connect and the instructions for use say so, the lower-cycle connector may be justified; if field replacement is plausible, the reliability engineer has a point. I'd also look at whether the connector's other parameters (contact resistance, insertion force, sealing, patient-contact safety) are affected by the choice, since a higher-cycle connector might also change the mechanical interface. If we can't resolve it from requirements alone, I'd propose a compromise: keep the current connector but add a verification test that simulates the realistic mating-cycle count, or qualify the higher-cycle connector as a second source. The goal is to make the decision traceable to the risk analysis and the intended use, not to win the argument.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document the decision so it's defensible in a design history file? What would you do if the higher-cycle connector also required a change to the enclosure tooling late in the project?
