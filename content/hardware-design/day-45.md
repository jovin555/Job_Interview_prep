# hardware-design — Day 45

## Q1: How would you approach designing a power supply architecture for a medical device that contains both a high-resolution analog front-end (requiring a noise floor below 50 µV RMS) and a motor driver that can draw 1A peaks?

**Answer:** The fundamental challenge here is that you have two loads with diametrically opposed power requirements sharing one energy source. The motor needs high instantaneous current with fast slew rates, while the analog front-end needs a clean, stable supply with extremely low noise. I would start by separating the power domains as early as possible — physically and electrically. The motor driver should be fed from its own rail, ideally directly from the battery or main supply through a dedicated path, while the analog section gets its own low-noise regulator chain. The key decision is whether to use a single switching regulator followed by post-regulation, or separate regulators entirely. For the analog rail, I'd typically use a switching pre-regulator followed by an LDO with high PSRR, because the switching stage provides efficiency while the LDO cleans up the ripple and switching noise. The LDO's PSRR needs to be examined across the switching frequency and its harmonics, not just at DC. For the motor driver, I'd use a separate regulator or direct battery connection with proper bulk decoupling close to the motor driver to handle the 1A peaks locally. The ground architecture is critical — I'd use a star ground or split ground plane with a single point of connection between the analog and digital/motor grounds, being careful about where return currents flow. I'd also consider adding a small series ferrite bead or inductor in the analog supply path to provide additional high-frequency isolation. The layout would keep the motor driver and its high-current loop physically separated from the analog section, and I'd verify the design with both time-domain measurements (looking at noise during motor actuation) and frequency-domain analysis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a split ground plane and a solid ground plane with careful component placement? What specific LDO parameters would you evaluate to ensure adequate noise rejection from the switching pre-regulator?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a precision analog front-end's output shows a periodic disturbance at approximately 1–10 Hz, even when the input is shorted to ground, and the disturbance amplitude varies with the power supply voltage?

**Answer:** A low-frequency periodic disturbance that persists with the input shorted points to something in the power or reference path rather than the signal path itself. The fact that amplitude varies with supply voltage is a strong clue — it suggests the disturbance is coupling through the supply or reference, or that there's a thermal loop involved. I'd start by powering the circuit from a clean bench supply instead of the device's normal power source to see if the disturbance disappears — that would implicate the power supply design. If it persists, I'd look at the voltage reference next, since a reference with inadequate decoupling or a marginal stability margin can oscillate or breathe at low frequencies. I'd also suspect thermoelectric effects: if there's a component dissipating varying power — perhaps a regulator or driver that cycles on and off — it could create a low-frequency thermal cycle that modulates offset voltages through the temperature coefficient of the op-amps or reference. I'd use a thermal camera or probe component temperatures to check for this. Another possibility is that the disturbance is aliased from a higher frequency — if the measurement system samples at a rate that aliases a switching frequency or its sidebands down to 1–10 Hz. I'd check with a spectrum analyzer on the supply rail and the output simultaneously, looking for correlated peaks. I'd also verify the ground connections — a poor ground connection somewhere can create a low-frequency loop that picks up environmental signals or couples through the supply. The systematic approach is to isolate stages: short the output of each stage to its input reference and see where the disturbance first appears, then focus on that stage's supply, reference, and grounding.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a disturbance that's truly at 1–10 Hz versus one that's being aliased from a higher frequency? What test equipment would you use to make this determination?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting the inductor for a boost converter that must deliver 5V at 500mA from a single Li-ion cell (3.0–4.2V), where the load has fast current transients?

**Answer:** The inductor selection starts with understanding the converter's operating mode and the load's transient requirements. For a boost converter, the inductor's value determines the ripple current, which directly affects both the output ripple and the converter's ability to respond to load transients. I'd first calculate the minimum inductance needed to keep the converter in continuous conduction mode (CCM) at the minimum load current, since operating in discontinuous mode at light loads can cause poor transient response and increased output ripple. The inductance value is a trade-off: larger inductance gives lower ripple current and better efficiency but slower transient response and larger physical size; smaller inductance responds faster to transients but has higher ripple current, which stresses the switch, diode, and output capacitor. For fast load transients, I'd lean toward a smaller inductance with higher ripple current, compensated by a larger output capacitor to absorb the transient energy. The critical parameter is the saturation current rating — the inductor must not saturate at the peak current, which is the average input current plus half the ripple current. At minimum battery voltage (3.0V), the input current will be highest, so that's the worst case for saturation. I'd derate the saturation current by at least 20–30% to account for temperature effects and manufacturing tolerance. I'd also look at the inductor's DC resistance (DCR) for efficiency, and its self-resonant frequency (SRF) — it should be well above the switching frequency. The core material matters too: for a boost converter at typical switching frequencies (500 kHz–2 MHz), a ferrite core is usually appropriate, but I'd check for core loss at the operating frequency and ripple current. Finally, I'd verify the inductor's behavior under transient conditions — a fast load step can cause the inductor current to momentarily exceed the steady-state peak, so the saturation margin needs to cover this.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would the inductor selection change if the converter had to operate in discontinuous conduction mode at light loads? What's the relationship between inductor value and the output capacitor value for meeting a specific transient response requirement?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a precision current source for driving a medical-grade temperature sensor (e.g., an RTD or thermistor) that must maintain accuracy within ±0.1°C over a 0–50°C range?

**Answer:** The first decision is the sensor type — an RTD (typically Pt100 or Pt1000) is more linear and stable than a thermistor, but requires more complex excitation. For a ±0.1°C requirement, I'd likely use a Pt1000 RTD with a precision current source, since the higher resistance minimizes lead-wire errors and the self-heating is lower than with a Pt100. The current source itself needs to be extremely stable — for a Pt1000, a 1°C change corresponds to roughly 3.85Ω, so at a typical excitation current of 1mA, that's about 3.85mV per degree. To achieve ±0.1°C accuracy, the current source must be stable to about 0.04% over temperature and time, which rules out simple discrete designs without precision references. I'd use a precision voltage reference driving a precision op-amp configured as a current source, with a low-temperature-coefficient sense resistor. The sense resistor's temperature coefficient is often the limiting factor — a ±5 ppm/°C resistor would give about ±0.025% drift over a 50°C range, which is borderline. I'd select a resistor with ±0.1% tolerance and ±2 ppm/°C or better. The op-amp needs low offset voltage and low offset drift — a chopper-stabilized amplifier would be ideal since it can achieve sub-µV offset and near-zero drift. The voltage reference needs similar precision — a bandgap reference with ±0.05% initial accuracy and low drift, or better yet, a buried-zener reference if the power budget allows. I'd also consider the self-heating of the RTD itself: at 1mA excitation, a Pt1000 dissipates 1mW, which in still air could cause a small temperature rise — I'd need to account for this in the accuracy budget. The measurement would use a ratiometric approach where possible: if the ADC's reference is derived from the same current source, many errors cancel. Finally, I'd add a calibration step at manufacturing to null out residual offset and gain errors, and I'd document the complete error budget showing how each component contributes to the total ±0.1°C requirement.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you choose between a 2-wire, 3-wire, or 4-wire RTD connection for this application? How would you account for self-heating of the RTD in your accuracy budget?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review, a colleague from the software team argues that the device's fault detection can be handled entirely in firmware — monitoring sensor values through the existing ADC and shutting down the system via a GPIO — eliminating the need for a dedicated hardware comparator circuit. The colleague argues this will reduce cost, save board space, and allow more flexible threshold adjustment. You believe the hardware approach is necessary because the protection must work even if the firmware hangs or the ADC fails. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** This is a safety-critical design decision, so I'd approach it methodically rather than just asserting my position. First, I'd acknowledge the legitimate points in the colleague's argument — firmware-based detection does offer flexibility and cost savings, and those are valid engineering considerations. Then I'd reframe the discussion around the device's safety requirements rather than personal preference. The key question is: what's the failure mode we're protecting against, and what's the consequence if the protection doesn't activate? For a medical device, the safety case typically requires that protection functions be independent of the functions being protected — if the firmware is responsible for both normal operation and fault detection, a single firmware failure defeats both. I'd ask the colleague to walk through the failure scenarios: what happens if the firmware hangs while the motor is running? What if the ADC's reference drifts and the readings become inaccurate? What if there's a single-event upset in the processor? The hardware comparator provides a defense-in-depth layer that operates independently of the firmware's health. I'd also reference the relevant safety standards — IEC 60601 and ISO 14971 typically require that safety functions be designed with appropriate independence and that the risk analysis considers common-cause failures. Rather than making it a win-lose argument, I'd propose a path forward: we could document the risk analysis showing why hardware protection is necessary, or we could evaluate whether a hybrid approach makes sense — perhaps firmware detection for non-critical faults with hardware protection reserved for the highest-risk scenarios. If the colleague's cost concerns are driving the discussion, I'd offer to work together to find a lower-cost hardware implementation — perhaps a simpler comparator circuit or a different component choice — rather than dismissing the concern outright. The goal is to reach a decision based on safety requirements and risk analysis, not on who argues more persuasively.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you go about documenting the risk analysis that justifies the hardware approach? What if the project manager pressures you to accept the firmware-only solution to meet a schedule deadline?
