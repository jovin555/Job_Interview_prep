# hardware-design — Day 64

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?
**Answer:** Start by translating the system's timing budget into a frequency-stability requirement in ppm over the full operating temperature range, then compare that against the crystal's frequency-vs-temperature characteristic — not just its room-temperature tolerance. A standard AT-cut crystal follows a cubic curve with a turnover point; if the turnover temperature sits near the middle of the operating range, the total excursion is minimized, whereas a crystal whose turnover point is at the edge of the range will drift much more. For tight budgets, a TCXO or an ovenized part may be the only way to meet the spec, and that's a cost/board-area trade-off to surface early. Beyond stability, I'd check load capacitance matching — the specified CL must match the actual parasitic plus discrete capacitance on the board, because a mismatch pulls the frequency and shifts the turnover point. I'd also verify drive level is within the crystal's rated range (too little can cause startup failure, too much causes aging and possible fracture), and confirm the ESR is compatible with the oscillator's negative-resistance margin. On the bench, I'd measure startup time and startup margin across temperature (cold start is usually the worst case), sweep the supply voltage, and use a frequency counter or a phase-noise analyzer to confirm the actual frequency and jitter against the datasheet. I'd also verify the oscillator's negative resistance by injecting a variable resistance in series with the crystal and finding the point where oscillation stops — that gives a margin number rather than a pass/fail.

## Q2: How would you approach designing a snubber network for a switching node in a converter, and how would you decide whether it's actually needed?
**Answer:** The first question is whether the switching node actually needs damping. I'd start by scoping the node with a low-inductance probe and looking at the ringing frequency and amplitude — if the overshoot is within the MOSFET's and diode's voltage ratings with margin, and the radiated/conducted EMI is within budget, a snubber may be unnecessary and just burns efficiency. If ringing is a problem, the classic approach is an RC snubber across the switch node (or across the diode for a boost). The design method: measure the ringing frequency with the snubber capacitor temporarily added, since the added capacitance shifts the frequency and lets you back out the parasitic inductance and capacitance. From there, choose the snubber capacitor so the ringing frequency drops by roughly a factor of two, and choose the resistor to critically damp — approximately equal to the characteristic impedance of the parasitic LC. The trade-offs are real: the resistor dissipates power every switching cycle (P ≈ C·V²·f), so at high frequency and high voltage the efficiency hit can be significant, and the capacitor must have low ESL to be effective. Alternatives worth considering first: slowing the gate drive slightly (adding gate resistance), improving the layout to reduce the parasitic loop inductance, or using a soft-recovery diode. A snubber is often the last resort after layout and gate-drive optimization, because it's a band-aid over a parasitic that better layout would have reduced.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a snubber problem from a gate-drive problem when you see ringing? What's the risk of an oversized snubber capacitor?

## Q3: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?
**Answer:** The core distinction is that a comparator is designed to be open-loop and saturate cleanly, while an op-amp is designed for closed-loop linear operation and will behave poorly if you try to use it as a comparator — slow recovery from saturation, possible phase inversion, and unpredictable output levels. For a protection threshold, I'd almost always reach for a dedicated comparator, and specifically one with the right characteristics for the job: propagation delay (a protection circuit may need sub-microsecond response), input offset voltage and its drift over temperature (which sets the threshold accuracy), input bias current (which matters if the source impedance is high), and hysteresis (built-in or added externally to prevent chatter near the threshold). I'd also look at the output stage — open-drain vs push-pull — because that determines how it interfaces to the latch or the downstream logic, and whether it can be pulled to a safe level if the supply drops. An op-amp might be considered if the same amplifier is already in the signal chain and the threshold is very loose, but that's a compromise, not a design choice. For a safety-relevant threshold, I'd also consider a comparator with an integrated reference, or a window comparator built from two comparators, and I'd verify the threshold accuracy over temperature on the bench rather than trusting the datasheet alone.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you add hysteresis to a comparator without degrading threshold accuracy? What failure modes of a comparator would you want to guard against in a protection circuit?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?
**Answer:** Audible noise from an inductor means the switching frequency, or a subharmonic of it, has dropped into the audible band — roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The most common cause is that the regulator has entered a pulse-skipping or burst mode at light load to maintain efficiency, and the burst repetition rate is in the audible range. That's not a fault per se, but it's a user-perceived problem in a medical device, and it can also indicate the loop is marginally stable. My first step would be to scope the switch node and the inductor current at the actual light-load condition and look at the burst frequency and duty pattern. If it's burst mode, the options are: disable burst mode if the controller allows it (at the cost of light-load efficiency), increase the minimum load so the regulator stays in continuous conduction, or change the inductor to one with a higher self-resonant frequency and better mechanical damping. If the noise is a subharmonic oscillation, that points to a compensation problem — the loop is marginally stable and the regulator is skipping pulses in an irregular pattern. In that case I'd check the compensation network against the datasheet's recommended values and the actual output capacitor ESR, and consider adding a small amount of slope compensation or adjusting the feedback network. I'd also check for mechanical resonance — sometimes the inductor's own body resonates with the PCB, and a different mounting or a dab of conformal coating can silence it. The key is to distinguish "annoying but stable" from "marginally stable and about to become a real problem."
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure loop stability on the bench without a network analyzer? What's the trade-off between disabling burst mode and light-load efficiency?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements question first, not a component argument. The disagreement hinges on an assumption about how the device is actually used in the field — is the cable connected once at installation, or is it routinely disconnected for cleaning, transport, or replacement? Rather than debating the connector, I'd want to get the actual use case documented: talk to the clinical or field service team, check the instructions for use, and look at whether the device is expected to be moved between patients or between rooms. If the use case genuinely is "connect once," the reliability engineer's concern may be based on a different assumption, and the right move is to align on the requirement. If the use case is ambiguous or the field data is unclear, I'd lean toward the more conservative connector, because the cost of a field failure — especially in a medical device — is much higher than the cost of the connector, and the reliability engineer is raising a legitimate risk. I'd also look for a middle path: a connector with a higher cycle rating that isn't the most expensive option, or a design change that makes the cable less likely to be disconnected (a strain relief, a captive cable, a locking mechanism). The goal is to make the decision on evidence and risk, not on whose component preference wins, and to document the rationale so it's traceable in the design history file.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you resolve this if the field data genuinely doesn't exist yet? How would you document the decision so it holds up in a design review or audit?
