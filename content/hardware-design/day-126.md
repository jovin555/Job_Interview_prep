# hardware-design — Day 126

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the input capacitor for a switching regulator, and what parameters would you check to make sure it actually does its job across the full operating range?

**Answer:** The input capacitor's job is to supply the high-frequency pulse current the converter draws during the switch on-time, so the input source only sees a smoothed average current. I'd start by calculating the RMS ripple current the capacitor must carry — for a buck it's roughly I_out·√(D(1−D)), which peaks near 50% duty cycle — and pick a capacitor whose ripple-current rating at the actual operating temperature exceeds that with margin. Temperature derates ripple current significantly, so I'd check the datasheet's temperature correction factor rather than the headline 25°C number. Next, effective capacitance: many ceramic dielectrics (X5R/X7R) lose a large fraction of their nominal value under DC bias, so I'd use the vendor's bias-derating curves at the actual rail voltage, not the nameplate value. I'd also look at ESR and ESL, because the capacitor's impedance at the switching frequency and its self-resonant point determine how well it actually shunts the ripple — above self-resonance it looks inductive, so a small parallel capacitor closer to the IC often helps. Finally, I'd consider the physical loop: the input cap must be placed tight against the high-side switch and ground return to minimize loop inductance, because that parasitic inductance sets the switching-node ringing and radiated EMI. On the bench I'd verify by measuring input ripple voltage and, more importantly, the switching-node waveform for excessive ringing, and check the capacitor's case temperature under full load.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between one large ceramic and several smaller ones in parallel? What changes if the input comes through a long cable rather than a local supply?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision comes down to the ADC's error budget and how much of it the reference is allowed to consume. A filtered supply rail is attractive for cost and board area, but its absolute accuracy, temperature drift, and noise are all inherited from the regulator and the filter — and a passive filter only attenuates noise above its corner, so low-frequency drift and 1/f noise pass straight through. For a high-resolution converter, I'd first work out the LSB size and translate the reference's initial accuracy, tempco, and output noise into LSBs or ppm, then compare against the fraction of the budget I can allocate. The parameters I'd actually read are: initial accuracy at 25°C, temperature coefficient (and whether it's specified as a box method or a slope), long-term drift, output noise in µV RMS over the relevant bandwidth (and low-frequency 1/f noise, which matters for DC-type measurements), and output impedance / load regulation, since the reference has to hold up against the ADC's switched-capacitor sampling current. I'd also check the reference's own PSRR if it's fed from a noisy rail, and whether it needs a specific output capacitor for stability. If the measurement is ratiometric — the sensor excitation comes from the same reference — a filtered rail can actually be the better choice because ratiometric operation cancels reference error. Otherwise, for a tight error budget, a dedicated reference with a specified tempco and low noise is usually the defensible choice.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the reference's noise contribution on the bench? What layout precautions matter for a voltage reference feeding a high-resolution ADC?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the current is right and the gate voltage is right, the heat is coming from either conduction loss higher than expected or switching loss I haven't accounted for — so I'd separate those two first. For conduction, I'd measure the actual drain-source voltage across the device while it's on and compute dissipation as V_DS × I; that gives real R_DS(on) in-circuit rather than trusting the datasheet. A common cause of elevated R_DS(on) is insufficient gate drive at the actual gate — a 10V reading on a meter doesn't prove the gate is fully enhanced during switching, and if the driver is weak or there's a large gate resistor, the device may be spending significant time in the linear region. I'd scope the gate-source and drain-source waveforms to check rise/fall times and whether the device is fully on before current ramps. For switching loss, I'd look at the switching frequency and whether the load is actually purely resistive — wiring inductance can cause the drain voltage to spike at turn-off, and a snubber or better layout may be needed. I'd also check thermal path: a small package on a poor copper pour can run hot even at modest dissipation, so I'd compare calculated junction temperature against the actual case temperature to see if the thermal resistance is the problem. Finally, I'd rule out shoot-through or body-diode conduction if there's any inductive component in the load.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish gate-drive weakness from a thermal-path problem with bench measurements alone? What would you check first if the drain waveform shows ringing at turn-off?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a ferrite bead for a power rail that supplies both a sensitive analog sensor drawing a small steady current and a digital microcontroller drawing larger current with fast transient spikes?

**Answer:** The tension is that a ferrite bead helps isolate the analog sensor from the digital transients, but its impedance and DC resistance can also cause the digital load's own supply to sag during those same transients. I'd start by characterizing the two loads: the analog sensor's current is small and steady, so it can tolerate a bead with meaningful impedance; the microcontroller's transient spikes mean the bead must not present so much impedance that the rail droops below the MCU's minimum during a spike. So I'd look at the bead's impedance-versus-frequency curve, not just the single rated impedance at 100 MHz — what matters is the impedance in the band where the digital noise actually lives (often tens to hundreds of MHz for fast edges), plus the DC resistance, which sets the IR drop and must be small relative to the allowed rail tolerance at the peak current. I'd also check the bead's rated current against the MCU's peak, with derating for temperature, and its saturation behavior — ferrite beads can saturate and lose impedance if the DC bias is high. The placement matters as much as the part: the bead goes in series with the digital branch, with local decoupling on the MCU side of the bead, so the bead and the local capacitors form a filter that keeps the transient current local rather than drawing it through the shared rail. On the bench I'd verify by measuring the analog rail's noise with the MCU active versus idle, and checking the MCU rail's transient droop with a scope probe at the pin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a ferrite bead and an LC filter for this isolation? What would you check if the bead's impedance collapses under the MCU's DC bias?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a trade-off to be resolved with evidence rather than a position to defend. First I'd make sure I understand the manufacturing engineer's actual cost driver — is it the finish itself, the process complexity, or yield — because the cheapest finish on paper isn't cheap if it drives rework or field failures. Then I'd lay out the technical requirements concretely: the BGA's pitch and pad count, the flatness and coplanarity the assembly process needs, the solder-joint reliability expectations over the product's life, and whether the ADC's performance is actually sensitive to the surface finish or whether that's my assumption. I'd bring the assembly house into the conversation, since they can speak to yield data with each finish on comparable boards, and I'd ask the manufacturing engineer to cost out the full picture — finish, process, inspection, and expected fallout — rather than just the per-board material delta. If the data supports the cheaper finish for this board, I should be willing to change my mind; if it doesn't, I'd present the reliability and yield risk in terms the whole team can weigh, and let the decision be documented with the rationale. The key is that the decision gets made on shared data, not on whose preference is louder, and that whatever we choose is recorded so it's defensible later.

**Possible follow-ups:** What data would you want from the assembly house before agreeing to a change? How would you handle it if the cheaper finish passed initial builds but showed marginal joints only after thermal cycling?
