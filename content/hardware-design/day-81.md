# hardware-design — Day 81

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor value and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start by treating shunt value as a trade-off between signal amplitude and self-heating. A larger shunt gives a bigger differential voltage for the amplifier to resolve, which helps with offset and noise, but dissipation scales as I²R, so at 1A a 100 mΩ shunt burns 100 mW while a 10 mΩ shunt burns only 10 mW. The right value is the largest one that keeps dissipation and self-heating (and therefore resistance drift) within budget, while still producing a differential voltage comfortably above the amplifier's input offset and noise floor. For a ±2% accuracy target over temperature, the dominant error terms are usually shunt tolerance and its temperature coefficient, amplifier input offset voltage and its drift, gain error, and CMRR — so I'd budget each term and pick a shunt with a low tempco (e.g., a dedicated current-sense resistor rather than a generic thick-film part) and an amplifier whose offset drift is small relative to the full-scale signal.

For topology, a bidirectional measurement needs either a differential amplifier referenced to a mid-rail bias, or a dedicated bidirectional current-sense amplifier that outputs a voltage centered on a reference (often Vref/2). I'd choose a current-sense amplifier with an integrated matched resistor network rather than building a discrete difference amplifier, because the matched network gives far better CMRR and gain accuracy over temperature — critical when the shunt sits at a voltage that swings with the motor's PWM. I'd also consider high-side vs low-side sensing: high-side keeps the ground reference clean and detects faults to ground, but requires the amplifier to handle a high common-mode voltage, while low-side is simpler but breaks the ground path. For a motor driver, high-side or in-line sensing is usually preferred for fault detection. Finally, I'd verify the amplifier's common-mode input range covers the full bus voltage plus transients, and check that its bandwidth is adequate for the PWM frequency so the output settles between switching edges.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you lay out the shunt and the amplifier's input traces to preserve CMRR at the PWM switching frequency?
- What would change in your selection if the motor current were unidirectional instead of bidirectional?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware POST is to confirm that the analog signal chain is alive and within tolerance before the firmware trusts any measurement — and to do so using means that don't depend on the very ADC and firmware being validated. I'd structure it around a few independent checks. First, rail monitoring: comparators or a supervisor IC that assert a fault if any critical supply is out of window, so the firmware can't run on a marginal rail. Second, reference verification: a window comparator or ADC readback that confirms the precision reference is within tolerance, since every downstream measurement is ratiometric to it. Third, a signal-chain continuity check: injecting a known stimulus — a switched reference voltage or a precision divider — at the front-end input and confirming the output lands in an expected band. That catches an open sensor, a shorted input, or a dead amplifier stage.

The key design principle is independence: the checks should use hardware paths that don't share failure modes with the normal measurement path. For example, a comparator-based window check on the reference doesn't rely on the ADC, and a separate stimulus source doesn't rely on the sensor. I'd also make the POST results latch into a status register that the firmware reads, rather than having the firmware poll an ADC channel and decide — that way a hung or corrupted firmware can't mask a hardware fault. Where a fault must prevent therapy, the POST output should gate the enable path directly in hardware, not just inform the firmware. Finally, I'd define what "pass" means in terms of the device's accuracy budget, and document the coverage — what the POST does and does not catch — so the risk file reflects the residual gaps.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you avoid the POST stimulus itself injecting noise or offset into the measurement path during normal operation?
- How would you verify that the POST circuitry is itself working, rather than silently passing?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where an op-amp's output is correct at DC but shows a slow, large-amplitude drift over minutes when the board is warmed by nearby power components?
**Answer:** A slow drift over minutes that correlates with board warm-up points to a thermal effect rather than a noise or stability problem. I'd start by confirming the correlation: use a thermal camera or a thermocouple to map which components heat up and how the drift tracks temperature, and try to reproduce it by applying gentle local heat (hot air at low flow, or a heat gun on low) to individual components while watching the output. If the drift follows a specific part, that narrows it quickly.

The likely culprits are: input offset voltage drift of the op-amp itself (especially if it's a bipolar-input part with significant offset tempco), input bias current drift interacting with source impedance (a high-impedance node turns bias-current drift into voltage drift), thermocouple effects at dissimilar-metal junctions in the signal path, or a resistor whose tempco is larger than expected. I'd also check whether the feedback network or the gain-setting resistors are drifting — a resistor with a poor tempco in a high-gain stage multiplies its drift. Another possibility is a nearby regulator or power device heating the op-amp and shifting its operating point, or a PCB layout where the op-amp sits in a thermal gradient between two heat sources, creating a thermocouple across its input pins.

To isolate, I'd measure the op-amp's inputs directly (with a low-thermal-EMF probe technique) to see whether the drift originates at the input or is introduced by the op-amp, and I'd swap in a low-drift, low-bias-current part or a chopper-stabilized amplifier to see if the drift disappears. If it's a layout/thermal-gradient issue, the fix is physical: move the sensitive part away from heat sources, add thermal relief or a copper pour to equalize temperature across the input pins, or use matched resistor networks with tight tempco tracking. The fix should be verified over the full operating temperature range, not just at the bench.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish offset-voltage drift from bias-current drift as the root cause?
- What layout techniques reduce thermocouple effects at an op-amp's input pins?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting between a crowbar protection scheme and a clamping scheme for a low-voltage rail that feeds sensitive analog circuitry, and what would drive the decision?
**Answer:** The choice comes down to what the protection must do when an overvoltage event occurs and what the downstream circuitry can tolerate. A clamping scheme — typically a TVS diode, Zener, or active clamp — limits the rail to a safe voltage but lets the rail continue to operate, so the analog circuitry keeps running (possibly with degraded accuracy) during the event. A crowbar scheme deliberately short-circuits the rail to ground (via an SCR or a latching switch) when the voltage exceeds a threshold, forcing the upstream supply or fuse to trip and removing power entirely. The crowbar is more definitive — it guarantees the sensitive parts see no overvoltage — but it takes the whole rail down and requires a fuse or resettable element to clear the fault.

The decision drivers are: the energy and duration of the expected overvoltage event, the absolute maximum ratings of the analog parts, and whether the system can tolerate a rail shutdown. If the analog front-end has a hard absolute-maximum that a clamp can't guarantee (for example, a precision ADC whose input must never exceed its supply), a crowbar may be the safer choice because it removes the overvoltage rather than just limiting it. If the event is a brief transient and the parts can survive a clamped level, a TVS clamp is simpler, doesn't interrupt operation, and doesn't require a fuse to reset. I'd also consider the failure mode: a crowbar that fails to trip leaves the rail unprotected, while a clamp that fails open does the same — so I'd want the protection to be verifiable and, where safety-critical, redundant. For a medical device, I'd tie the choice back to the risk analysis: what's the consequence of the analog rail seeing an overvoltage, and what's the consequence of the rail dropping out mid-therapy?

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you size the crowbar's trip threshold relative to the analog parts' absolute maximum ratings?
- What are the failure modes of a TVS clamp, and how would you detect a failed protection device?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a question of use-case definition rather than a straight difference of opinion, because the two positions rest on different assumptions about how the device is actually used. The first step is to get the assumptions on the table: is the cable genuinely single-connect, or does clinical practice involve disconnecting and reconnecting — for cleaning, for patient transfer, for reprocessing? If the intended-use and the field reality diverge, the reliability engineer's concern is legitimate and the design should follow the field reality, not the optimistic assumption.

I'd propose resolving it with data rather than argument. That means checking the intended-use documentation and, if it's ambiguous, talking to clinical or service stakeholders about how the cable is actually handled. If the answer is genuinely "connected once," I'd document that assumption explicitly in the design inputs and the risk file, and note the connector's rated cycles against the expected number of mate/unmate events with margin. If there's any realistic scenario of repeated mating — even infrequent field replacement — I'd take the reliability engineer's point seriously and either specify the higher-cycle connector or add a mitigation (a strain relief, a serviceable connector, or a clear instruction that the cable is not field-replaceable). The key is that the decision gets recorded with its rationale, so it's traceable and reviewable later. I'd also want to understand the cost delta and whether it's material to the project — if it's small, the conservative choice is usually the right one for a medical device.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you document the connector's duty-cycle assumption so it's traceable in the design history file?
- If the higher-cycle connector were significantly more expensive, how would you frame the trade-off to the project lead?
