# hardware-design — Day 111

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?

**Answer:** Start by translating the system's timing budget into a frequency-tolerance requirement in ppm, then split that budget between the crystal's own stability and the rest of the clock path (load capacitors, oscillator circuit, and any PLL multiplication). For a wide-temperature application, the crystal's frequency-versus-temperature characteristic is the dominant term, so I'd compare candidate parts on their specified stability over the full operating range rather than the room-temperature tolerance, which is often the only number people look at. I'd also check the crystal's load-capacitance spec against what the MCU's oscillator is designed to drive, its equivalent series resistance (ESR) versus the oscillator's maximum drive capability, and its drive-level rating — overdriving a small crystal can cause aging or fracture, while underdriving risks marginal startup. On the bench I'd verify startup margin across temperature, especially at the cold extreme where ESR rises and loop gain falls, by observing startup time and whether oscillation begins cleanly or takes many cycles. I'd measure actual frequency error at several temperatures against a reference, check that the negative-resistance margin of the oscillator circuit is adequate (typically several times the crystal's ESR), and confirm the drive level with a current probe or by measuring voltage across the crystal and computing dissipated power. Finally I'd look at whether the chosen load capacitors are NP0/C0G so their own drift doesn't eat into the budget.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative-resistance margin without a dedicated test fixture? What would you do if the crystal meets its spec at temperature but startup is marginal only at cold?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor value and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?

**Answer:** The shunt value and the amplifier gain are a coupled trade-off, so I'd start from the ADC's full-scale input and the accuracy target. A larger shunt gives more signal at low currents and better signal-to-noise, but dissipates more power and creates more self-heating, which itself becomes an error source because the shunt's temperature coefficient shifts its resistance. A smaller shunt wastes less power but pushes the amplifier's offset and gain error to dominate the budget. For a 1A full-scale with a ±2% accuracy target over temperature, I'd pick a shunt that keeps dissipation comfortably low — on the order of tens of milliwatts at full scale — and then choose an amplifier with low offset voltage and low offset drift, because at small shunt voltages the offset term is often the largest single contributor. For bidirectional current I'd use a dedicated bidirectional current-sense amplifier or a difference amplifier with a defined reference bias, so the output sits mid-scale at zero current and swings both ways. I'd budget the error terms explicitly: shunt tolerance, shunt TCR, amplifier offset and drift, gain error and drift, and ADC reference error, then check that the RSS of those terms stays inside ±2% across the temperature range. I'd also consider whether high-side or low-side sensing fits the application — high-side preserves the ground reference and catches faults to ground, low-side is simpler and cheaper but breaks the load's ground return.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the common-mode voltage range if the motor is driven from a higher supply than the amplifier's own rail? What layout precautions matter for a shunt-based sense path?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting between a crowbar protection scheme and a clamping scheme for a low-voltage rail that feeds sensitive analog circuitry, and what would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The two schemes solve different problems, so the decision comes down to what failure mode I'm protecting against and what the downstream circuitry can tolerate. A clamping scheme — a TVS or zener that conducts and holds the rail near a safe voltage — is non-destructive and self-recovering: it absorbs the transient energy and lets the rail keep operating, which suits expected, bounded events like ESD or a modest inductive kick. A crowbar deliberately short-circuits the rail through a thyristor or similar device once a threshold is crossed, collapsing the voltage hard and fast, and it typically requires the upstream supply to current-limit or a fuse to open so the crowbar releases. That makes it appropriate for a fault that would otherwise persist and damage the analog front-end, but it's a latching, disruptive event — the device goes down. For a sensitive analog rail I'd weigh the energy and duration of the credible overvoltage, whether the analog parts can survive the clamp's let-through voltage, and whether a brief rail collapse is acceptable operationally. I'd also check coordination: the clamp must trip below the analog parts' absolute maximum but above any legitimate transient, and the crowbar's trigger must be fast enough to beat the analog devices' damage curve. In a medical device the consequence of a false trip versus a missed fault also matters, so I'd want the scheme's behavior to be predictable and testable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the coordination between the clamp and the downstream absolute-maximum ratings on the bench? What failure modes would you check for the crowbar device itself?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?

**Answer:** The correlation with handling points to a mechanical or intermittent-electrical cause rather than a design-margin issue, so I'd treat it as a hunt for a marginal connection or a strain-sensitive joint. First I'd try to reproduce it outside the enclosure by flexing the board, tapping components, and wiggling connectors and cables while monitoring the failing signal — that tells me whether the fault is board-level or assembly-level. I'd look hard at the usual suspects: connector and cable interfaces where the enclosure's routing puts strain on a crimp or a solder joint, board-to-board headers, and any component whose solder joint could be cracked by flexing. I'd also consider whether the enclosure changes the thermal or EMI environment — a metal enclosure can shift grounding and shielding behavior, and a tight fit can press on a component or a trace. Continuous monitoring with a scope on the suspect node, set to trigger on the fault, is more useful than probing after the fact. If I can't localize it by inspection, I'd use techniques like freeze spray or gentle heat to see if temperature interacts with the fault, and I'd check for cold solder joints and insufficient solder on fine-pitch parts under magnification. The fix usually comes down to strain relief, connector retention, or a layout change that removes mechanical stress from a critical joint.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cracked solder joint from a connector contact issue without destructive analysis? What design changes would you make to prevent this class of failure in the next revision?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB stackup (8-layer, with two dedicated ground planes) is unnecessarily expensive and proposes a 4-layer stackup instead. You believe the 8-layer design is necessary for signal integrity and EMI compliance, particularly for the high-resolution ADC and wireless transmitter on the board. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a technical trade-off to be resolved with evidence rather than opinion, and I'd want the manufacturing engineer's cost concern taken seriously because it's real. My approach would be to first understand exactly what the cost delta is and where it comes from, then lay out what the 8-layer stackup is buying: dedicated ground planes for a clean analog return, controlled impedance for the high-speed and RF traces, and physical separation between the sensitive ADC front-end and the switching and radio sections. I'd ask whether the 4-layer proposal can actually deliver those — often it can't, because you lose the plane pair and the routing density forces the analog and digital returns to share copper. Rather than just asserting that, I'd propose a concrete comparison: a quick signal-integrity and EMI assessment of the 4-layer option, or a review of the return-path and crosstalk implications, so the decision rests on data. I'd also look for a middle path — a 6-layer stackup sometimes recovers most of the benefit at lower cost — and bring that to the table. Throughout I'd keep the conversation framed around the device's compliance and measurement requirements, since for a Class II medical product the cost of an EMI or accuracy failure downstream dwarfs the board cost, and I'd want the decision documented with the rationale so it's defensible in the design history file.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the comparison so it's fair to both options? If the team still disagrees after the analysis, how would you escalate or decide?
