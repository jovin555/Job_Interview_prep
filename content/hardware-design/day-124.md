# hardware-design — Day 124

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a gate resistor for a MOSFET used as a high-side load switch, where the switch must limit inrush current into a large capacitive load but also turn off quickly enough to protect the load during a fault?

**Answer:** The gate resistor is really two separate design problems — turn-on and turn-off — and I'd treat them independently rather than picking one value and hoping it works for both.

For turn-on, the goal is to control the inrush into the capacitive load. The load capacitance and the MOSFET's transconductance set how fast the output rises for a given gate voltage ramp; a larger gate resistor slows the gate charge delivery and therefore slows the drain current rise, which limits the inrush. I'd estimate the allowable inrush from the upstream supply's current limit and the load's tolerance, then work backward to a gate charge time using the gate charge curve (Qg vs Vgs) from the datasheet — not just Ciss, because the plateau region dominates. I'd also check that the MOSFET stays inside its safe operating area during the ramp: slow turn-on means the device spends more time in the linear region dissipating power, so the SOA curve and the thermal impedance for that pulse width become the real constraint, not just the resistor value.

For turn-off, I want the gate discharged fast so the switch can interrupt a fault current quickly. That usually means a separate, much smaller turn-off path — a diode across the gate resistor, or a dedicated gate driver with asymmetric source/sink capability — so turn-on is slow and turn-off is fast. If I'm using a discrete resistor divider or a single resistor, I'd accept that turn-off is compromised and check whether the fault response time still meets the protection requirement.

I'd also account for the gate resistor's effect on ringing: too small a value with a long gate trace can excite parasitic inductance and cause gate-source ringing, so I'd verify with a scope probe at the gate and check Vgs stays within the absolute maximum. Finally, I'd confirm the resistor's power rating — during a slow turn-on into a large cap, the gate resistor sees real energy.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the inrush limiting actually works without a current probe rated for the surge? What changes if the load capacitance is unknown or varies across units?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a SAR ADC and a delta-sigma ADC for digitizing a slowly varying physiological signal that needs high resolution, and what datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** For a slowly varying physiological signal — say a temperature, pressure, or biopotential channel with content well under a few hundred hertz — both architectures can work, so the decision usually comes down to resolution, noise, and the system around the converter rather than raw sample rate.

The delta-sigma's strength is that it oversamples and noise-shapes, so you get very high resolution in the band of interest almost for free, with an inherent anti-aliasing benefit from the digital filter. If the signal is genuinely slow and I need 18–24 bits of effective resolution, a delta-sigma is often the lower-effort path — I don't need a steep analog anti-alias filter ahead of it, and the digital filter handles the out-of-band noise. The trade-offs are latency (the digital filter's group delay and settling time matter if I'm multiplexing channels), and the fact that the modulator's clock and the digital filter can couple noise back into the analog front-end if layout isn't careful.

The SAR's strength is speed and simplicity: no latency penalty, easy multiplexing between channels, and a well-understood interface. But at high resolution the SAR's noise floor and the reference requirements become the limiting factor — I'd be looking hard at ENOB, input-referred noise, and whether the reference is quiet enough to actually realize 16 bits. A SAR also needs a proper anti-alias filter ahead of it, and the sample-and-hold's kickback means the driving amplifier has to settle fast.

The datasheet parameters I'd actually weigh: ENOB and SNR in the signal bandwidth (not just resolution in bits), input-referred noise density, INL/DNL if linearity matters, the reference input's noise and PSRR, and for the delta-sigma the output data rate versus noise trade-off and the filter's settling behavior. I'd also look at the input structure — differential vs single-ended, input bias current, and whether the part has an integrated PGA or filter that simplifies the front-end.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the signal has a small DC offset riding on a large common-mode, which architecture handles that more gracefully? How would you verify the effective resolution on the bench rather than trusting the datasheet?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?

**Answer:** Audible noise from an inductor means the converter is producing mechanical excitation in the audio band — roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz — and at light load that almost always points to the converter entering a pulse-skipping or burst mode, where the switching frequency drops into the audible range or the burst repetition rate lands there.

My first step is to characterize the behavior rather than guess: scope the switch node and the inductor current at the light load where it whines, and look at the repetition rate of the bursts. If the converter is in a skip/burst mode, the burst frequency is often set by the load, the output cap, and the hysteresis band, and it can easily fall in the audible range. I'd confirm by loading the converter up out of skip mode and seeing whether the noise disappears — that isolates the mechanism.

Once confirmed, the fixes are architectural and component-level. Architecturally, some converters let you disable skip mode or force continuous conduction at light load, at the cost of efficiency — that's a trade I'd weigh against the product's standby power budget. Component-wise, the inductor's construction matters enormously: a potted or molded inductor with tight windings is much quieter than an open drum-core part, and the same inductance value from a different family can go from audible to silent. I'd also check whether the burst frequency can be pushed above the audio band by adjusting the output capacitance or the converter's mode settings, and whether the PCB mounting is amplifying the vibration — sometimes the inductor is acting as a speaker through the board.

I'd also rule out other sources before blaming the inductor: ceramic capacitor piezoelectric singing is a common false positive, and it's worth confirming the sound is actually coming from the inductor with a stethoscope or by probing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to accept the noise if the product is in a noisy environment anyway? What would you check if the whine appears only at a specific input voltage?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a TVS diode for protecting a medical device's external connector that carries both power and a sensitive analog signal, and what parameters matter most?

**Answer:** The connector carries two very different threats on the same interface, so I'd treat the power pin and the analog pin as separate protection problems even if they share a connector.

For the power pin, the TVS has to survive the surge energy defined by the applicable standard — for a medical device that's typically IEC 61000-4-2 for ESD and often 61000-4-5 for surge — so the first parameters are standoff voltage (above the maximum normal operating voltage with margin for tolerance and transients), breakdown voltage, and peak pulse power/current with the specified waveform. I'd check the clamping voltage at the expected surge current against the absolute maximum of everything downstream, because a TVS that survives the surge but lets the clamp rise above the downstream IC's rating hasn't protected anything.

For the analog pin, the constraints are tighter and often conflicting. The TVS capacitance sits directly across a sensitive signal, so a high-capacitance part will roll off bandwidth and, worse, can introduce distortion and leakage that corrupts a precision measurement. I'd look for a low-capacitance TVS — often a diode-array or a specialized low-cap part — and check its capacitance at the actual signal bias, since capacitance varies with voltage. Leakage current matters too: on a high-impedance analog input, even nanoamps of TVS leakage become an offset error. And I'd confirm the working voltage leaves enough headroom that the TVS doesn't conduct on the normal signal swing.

Across both, I'd verify the TVS is placed so the surge current has a short, low-inductance path to ground before it reaches the protected circuitry, and that the ground return doesn't share impedance with the analog reference. The datasheet parameters that actually drive the decision are standoff/breakdown/clamping voltages, peak pulse rating with waveform, capacitance, and leakage.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a connector where the same pin can be either power or signal depending on the accessory? What would you do if no single TVS meets both the surge and capacitance requirements?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a decision that needs to be made on evidence rather than on who feels more strongly, and I'd try to reframe the conversation from "expensive vs. cheap" to "what does each finish actually deliver for this board, and what's the cost of getting it wrong."

First I'd make sure I understand the manufacturing engineer's concern precisely — is it the finish cost itself, the added process steps, the supplier lead time, or yield? Those have different answers. Then I'd lay out the technical requirements concretely: the BGA pitch and pad count, the flatness and coplanarity the assembly needs, the solder joint reliability expectations, and whether the ADC's performance is actually sensitive to the finish (for example, whether there's any concern about intermetallics, whiskers, or surface finish affecting high-frequency behavior). I'd want to separate what's a genuine requirement from what's my preference.

Then I'd bring data. If there's assembly yield history or a supplier's recommendation, that's more persuasive than assertion. I'd propose a concrete comparison: cost delta per board, the risk and cost of a re-spin or field failure if joints are marginal, and whether a middle-ground finish exists that satisfies both concerns. If the cheaper finish genuinely meets the assembly and reliability requirements, I should be open to changing my mind — the goal is the right board, not my original choice.

If after that we still disagree, I'd escalate the decision with the trade-offs documented rather than let it stall, and I'd want the decision recorded with its rationale so it's traceable later. The key is that the manufacturing engineer and I are both trying to ship a reliable product; the disagreement is about risk tolerance, and that's a conversation to have explicitly.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure a small build to settle the question empirically? What would you do if the cheaper finish passed the build but you still had concerns about long-term reliability?
