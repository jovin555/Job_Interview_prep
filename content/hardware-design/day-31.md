# hardware-design — Day 31

## Q1: How would you approach designing a precision voltage reference circuit for a 16-bit SAR ADC that must maintain accuracy within ±1 LSB over a 0–50°C temperature range, given a 3.3V supply and a 2.5V reference voltage?

**Answer:** The first step is to work out the actual error budget. For a 16-bit ADC with a 2.5V reference, 1 LSB is roughly 38 µV. That's the total budget for everything — reference initial accuracy, temperature drift, noise, and any loading effects. A dedicated reference IC with ±0.05% initial accuracy and 5 ppm/°C drift would contribute about 1.25 mV from initial tolerance alone, which already exceeds the 1 LSB budget. So I'd need to think carefully about what "within ±1 LSB" actually means in context — is it relative to the reference itself, or absolute accuracy? If absolute, I'd need a much tighter reference or a calibration step. For the circuit itself, I'd use a low-dropout reference with a Kelvin-connected output, a low-ESR output capacitor sized per the datasheet for stability, and a clean ground return that doesn't share current with digital circuitry. I'd also add a small RC filter on the reference output if the ADC's internal reference buffer has fast current transients during conversion. Temperature drift is the harder constraint — I'd look for a reference with a specified drift curve rather than just a single ppm/°C number, and I'd verify the drift over the actual 0–50°C range, not just at 25°C. Finally, I'd consider whether the ADC has a separate reference input pin that allows the reference to be filtered independently from the supply.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the reference's temperature drift during board-level testing? What if the budget can't be met with a single reference — how would you handle the trade-off?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load is getting hot (case temperature 85°C) even though the gate drive voltage appears correct (10V) and the load current measures 2A?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd question is whether the MOSFET is actually fully enhanced. A gate voltage of 10V might be above the threshold voltage, but it may not be enough to achieve the specified RDS(on) if the device is a logic-level MOSFET with a different gate drive requirement, or if the gate drive is current-limited and the gate isn't fully charged. I'd check the gate-source waveform with a scope — not just the DC level — to see if there's a slow rise time or ringing that indicates a gate drive issue. I'd also check the drain-source voltage while the load is on. If VDS is, say, 1V at 2A, that's 2W of dissipation, which would explain the temperature. If VDS is low but the device is still hot, I'd look at switching losses — is the MOSFET being switched at high frequency, and is the gate drive fast enough to minimize time in the linear region? I'd also verify the thermal path: is the copper area under the device adequate, and is the thermal pad properly soldered? A cold solder joint under the thermal pad is a common cause of unexpectedly high case temperature. Finally, I'd check whether the load is truly resistive or has an inductive component that could cause voltage spikes and additional dissipation during switching.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the issue is RDS(on) vs. switching losses? What measurements would you take to isolate the cause?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting a ferrite bead for a power rail that supplies both a sensitive analog sensor (drawing 10mA) and a digital microcontroller (drawing 50mA with 10mA transient spikes at 1 MHz)?

**Answer:** The key is to understand that a ferrite bead is frequency-dependent resistance, not a simple filter. For this application, I'd first characterize the noise sources: the microcontroller's 10mA spikes at 1 MHz are the primary concern, and the analog sensor needs a clean supply. I'd look at the ferrite bead's impedance-versus-frequency curve and select one that has high impedance at 1 MHz and above, while keeping DC resistance low enough that the voltage drop at 60mA total doesn't push the rail out of tolerance. A bead with, say, 100Ω impedance at 100 MHz might only have 10–20Ω at 1 MHz, so I'd need to check the actual curve. I'd also consider the DC bias effect — ferrite beads lose impedance as DC current increases, so I'd verify the impedance at the actual operating current, not just at zero bias. Placement matters too: the bead should be close to the microcontroller to isolate its switching noise, and the analog sensor should be on the other side with its own decoupling. I'd also add a bulk capacitor on the microcontroller side and a low-ESR ceramic on the analog side. If the 1 MHz spike is particularly problematic, a bead alone may not be enough — I might add a small LC filter instead, or use a bead with a parallel capacitor to create a pi-filter.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify that the selected ferrite bead actually solves the noise problem during testing? What if the voltage drop across the bead is too high at full load?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a comparator-based overcurrent detection circuit for a medical device's motor driver output, where the threshold must be accurate to within ±5% over temperature and the response time must be under 10 µs?

**Answer:** I'd start by defining the sensing element. A low-value sense resistor in the motor return path is the most straightforward approach, but I need to account for its tolerance and temperature coefficient. A 1% resistor with 50 ppm/°C drift would contribute roughly 0.25% error over a 50°C range, which is acceptable within the ±5% budget. The comparator itself introduces offset voltage and hysteresis — I'd select a comparator with low offset (under 1 mV) and use a small amount of positive feedback for hysteresis to prevent chatter at the threshold. The reference voltage for the threshold needs to be stable over temperature; a resistor divider from the supply rail would drift with the supply, so I'd use a dedicated reference or a precision divider with low-TCR resistors. For the 10 µs response time, the comparator's propagation delay is usually well under 1 µs, but the real bottleneck is often the sensing circuit's settling time — the RC time constant formed by the sense resistor and any filter capacitance. I'd keep filtering minimal, just enough to reject motor noise without slowing the response. The output stage needs to drive the shutdown path directly — ideally a latch that holds the fault condition until a deliberate reset, since the motor current may drop below threshold after the fault is detected. I'd also add a test point or a way to verify the threshold during manufacturing, since ±5% accuracy over temperature is hard to guarantee by design alone.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the trade-off between noise immunity (filtering) and response time? How would you verify the ±5% threshold accuracy during production testing?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review, the firmware lead argues that the ADC sampling rate should be increased from 100 Hz to 1 kHz to improve measurement resolution through oversampling. You believe the higher rate will alias 50/60 Hz noise into the passband and require a much steeper anti-aliasing filter, increasing component count and board area. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as an engineering trade-off to be resolved with data, not as a win-lose argument. First, I'd acknowledge the firmware lead's goal — improving resolution through oversampling is a legitimate technique, and it can work if the noise is truly random and uncorrelated. But the key question is whether the noise in this system is random or correlated. If there's 50/60 Hz mains pickup, oversampling won't help because the noise is deterministic and will alias. I'd propose a quick experiment: capture a few seconds of data at both sampling rates and look at the FFT. If the noise is broadband and random, oversampling might work. If there are discrete peaks at 50/60 Hz or harmonics, we need a different approach. I'd also quantify the filter trade-off: going from 100 Hz to 1 kHz sampling with a 100 Hz signal bandwidth means the anti-aliasing filter's transition band changes from about 900 Hz to 9.9 kHz — that's actually a much easier filter requirement, not harder. The real issue is the opposite: at 1 kHz sampling, any noise above 500 Hz aliases, whereas at 100 Hz sampling, noise above 50 Hz aliases. So the higher rate actually relaxes the anti-aliasing filter. I'd also consider the power impact — sampling at 1 kHz uses more power, which matters for a battery-powered device. My approach would be to frame this as a shared problem: let's define the noise requirements, measure the actual noise environment, and then decide together based on data rather than assumptions.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the experiment shows that oversampling does improve resolution, but the power cost is significant? How would you decide whether the trade-off is worth it? How would you document the decision for the design history file?
