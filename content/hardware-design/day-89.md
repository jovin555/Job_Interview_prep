## Q1: How would you approach selecting the feedback resistor values for a transimpedance amplifier front-end, and what trade-offs would drive the choice between a high-value and a low-value feedback resistor?

**Answer:** The transimpedance amplifier converts a photodiode's current into a voltage, with the transimpedance gain set by the feedback resistor Rf. The core trade-off is noise versus bandwidth and stability. A larger Rf gives higher signal gain, which improves the signal-to-noise ratio relative to the amplifier's input voltage noise — but it also increases the Johnson noise contribution of the resistor itself (proportional to √Rf), reduces the closed-loop bandwidth (since the feedback pole moves lower as Rf increases with the photodiode capacitance), and interacts with the photodiode's junction capacitance to create a phase margin problem. The photodiode capacitance and the amplifier's input capacitance form a pole in the feedback loop, and with a large Rf that pole can erode phase margin and cause peaking or oscillation. The standard fix is a small feedback capacitor Cf in parallel with Rf to introduce a zero that compensates the input pole — the value is chosen so the loop is critically damped, typically Cf ≈ √(Cin / (2π·Rf·GBW)) as a starting point, then tuned on the bench. So the selection process is: start from the required transimpedance gain and bandwidth, pick Rf to meet gain while checking that the resulting bandwidth (set by Rf and the total input capacitance) still meets the signal bandwidth requirement, then add Cf for stability, and verify the noise budget — the input-referred current noise from the amplifier's voltage noise divided by the total input impedance, plus the resistor's own current noise, must stay below the photodiode's shot noise floor if you want to preserve linearity at low light levels. A low-value Rf is easier to stabilize and has wider bandwidth but throws away signal swing and makes the amplifier's voltage noise dominate; a high-value Rf maximizes sensitivity but demands careful compensation and a low-capacitance photodiode and layout. In practice I'd also consider a T-network or a two-stage approach if the required gain is very high, because a single very large Rf becomes impractical for stability and for the resistor's own parasitic capacitance.

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage-mode and a current-mode control architecture for a buck converter that must supply a fast-transient digital load while also feeding a sensitive analog rail through a downstream LDO?

**Answer:** The two architectures differ in what they sense and how they compensate. Voltage-mode control senses the output voltage and compares it to a ramp; it has a well-understood, single-loop compensation but a double pole from the LC output filter that requires a type-III compensator, and its response to load transients is limited by the inductor current slew — it can't react until the output voltage has already moved. Current-mode control senses the inductor current (or a proxy) and uses it as the inner loop, which effectively removes the LC double pole and replaces it with a single dominant pole plus a sampling-related pole at half the switching frequency. That makes compensation simpler (type-II is usually enough) and gives cycle-by-cycle current limiting and better line rejection. For a fast-transient digital load, current-mode generally wins because the inner current loop responds to load steps faster and the converter can be pushed to higher crossover without instability. The catch is that current-mode needs slope compensation above 50% duty cycle to avoid subharmonic oscillation, and it's more sensitive to noise on the current-sense signal — which matters here because the same board also carries a sensitive analog rail. The downstream LDO is actually a helpful buffer: it gives the analog rail good PSRR at low frequency and rejects the switching ripple that the buck's output can't fully suppress, so the buck's job is to be stable and reasonably quiet, and the LDO handles the final cleanliness. Given that, I'd lean current-mode for the transient response and simpler compensation, add adequate slope compensation, keep the current-sense trace short and away from the analog section, and let the LDO's PSRR handle the residual ripple. If the switching noise coupling into the current-sense signal proved problematic, voltage-mode with a well-damped type-III compensator is the fallback — it's more immune to sense noise but slower.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide how much slope compensation to add, and what symptom tells you it's insufficient? What would you check in the LDO's PSRR curve to confirm it actually rejects the buck's switching frequency?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?

**Answer:** The correlation with handling and enclosure installation points to a mechanical or electromechanical cause rather than a pure electrical design flaw — so I'd treat it as an intermittent-connection hunt and try to make the failure reproducible before changing anything. First, I'd try to characterize the failure: does it correlate with a specific orientation, a specific kind of motion (flex, shock, vibration), or a specific point of pressure on the enclosure? I'd instrument the device — add a data logger or a firmware counter that records resets, brownouts, and communication errors with timestamps, so I can tell whether the failure is a reset, a communication dropout, or an analog measurement glitch. Then I'd look at the usual suspects in order of likelihood: connector and cable seating (board-to-board, flex, and external connectors are the classic culprits — a connector that's marginal on the bench can open or intermittently short once the harness is dressed and the enclosure closes), solder joints on large or heavy components (inductors, connectors, crystals) that see mechanical stress, board flex causing cracked joints or intermittent traces, and shielding or grounding that changes when the board is screwed down or the enclosure is closed. I'd use a combination of gentle flexing with a scope on the suspect nets, a thermal or vibration test to provoke it, and if needed a current-probe or a logic analyzer on the supply rails to catch brownouts. The key discipline is to find the mechanism before "fixing" it — reflowing everything or adding capacitors without identifying the root cause just moves the problem. Once I have a suspect, I'd confirm by reproducing the failure with a deliberate provocation (e.g., flexing a specific board region) and then verifying the fix eliminates it under the same provocation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a mechanical intermittent from a thermal one if both handling and warm-up seem to trigger it? What design changes would you make to a board to make it more robust against enclosure-induced flex?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a decoupling capacitor network for a high-resolution ADC that shares a board with a switching regulator and a digital section?

**Answer:** I'd think about it in layers, because each capacitor in the network serves a different frequency band and a different job. At the ADC's supply pins, the first priority is a small, low-ESL ceramic — typically 100 nF — placed as close as physically possible to the pin with a short, wide trace and a via right at the pad, because its job is to supply the high-frequency transient current the ADC draws during conversion and to keep the supply impedance low at the ADC's sampling and modulator frequencies. That capacitor's effectiveness is dominated by its mounting inductance, not its capacitance value, so placement and via geometry matter more than the nominal value. Behind that, a larger ceramic (1–10 µF) provides the mid-frequency bulk and dampens the interaction between the small capacitor and the upstream rail. Then at the board level, a bulk capacitor near the regulator output handles the lower-frequency load steps. The complication here is the switching regulator and the digital section: the regulator's switching ripple and the digital section's transient currents both propagate along the supply planes, so I'd want the ADC's supply to be fed through a ferrite bead or a small series impedance that isolates it from the noisy rail, with the decoupling network on the ADC side of that bead. I'd also pay attention to the reference pin — the ADC's reference decoupling is often more critical than its supply decoupling for accuracy, and it needs its own clean, well-placed capacitor. Finally, I'd verify on the bench: measure the supply ripple at the ADC pin with a scope and a short-ground-tip probe, and check the ADC's output noise with a quiet input to confirm the decoupling is actually doing its job, rather than assuming the BOM values are sufficient.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a ferrite bead in the ADC supply is helping or actually causing a resonance with the decoupling capacitors? What would you look for in the ADC's datasheet to know its transient current demand during conversion?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the software lead argues that the device's power-on self-test can be simplified by removing the hardware checks — rail monitors, reference checks — and relying on firmware to verify the system by reading the ADC and checking that values are in range. The software lead argues this reduces component count and board area. You believe the hardware checks are necessary because they verify the analog signal chain independently of the ADC and the firmware. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd start by making sure we're actually disagreeing about the same thing, because "self-test" can mean different things to hardware and software. The software lead's proposal verifies that the system produces plausible numbers; my concern is that it can't distinguish "the analog chain is healthy" from "the ADC and firmware are consistently wrong in a way that still looks plausible." If the ADC's reference has drifted, or the front-end gain has shifted, the firmware reading values in range may pass while the measurement is silently out of spec — and in a medical device that's exactly the failure mode a self-test should catch. So I'd frame the conversation around what each check is independently verifying: a rail monitor confirms the supply is actually in tolerance without trusting the ADC; a reference check confirms the ADC's reference is where it should be; and the firmware's in-range check confirms the digital path and the overall signal chain are behaving. They're complementary, not redundant. I'd also take the cost and board-area concern seriously rather than dismissing it — a rail monitor and a reference check can be a small comparator or supervisor IC plus a couple of passives, and I'd work with the software lead to see whether some checks can share components or be consolidated without losing independence. The key principle I'd hold to is that at least the critical checks must not depend on the same element they're supposed to be verifying; if the ADC is the thing under test, the check can't run through the ADC. I'd propose we take the question to the risk analysis — map each proposed check to the failure mode it detects and the harm it mitigates — and let that drive which checks stay in hardware. That turns it from a hardware-versus-software argument into a shared decision about risk coverage, which is a much more productive conversation and usually gets both sides to a defensible answer.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide which checks are critical enough to require hardware independence versus which can reasonably live in firmware? If the team ultimately keeps only the firmware checks, what residual risk would you document and how would you flag it?
