# hardware-design — Day 113

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a gate resistor for a MOSFET used as a load switch, and what trade-offs would drive the value you choose?

**Answer:** The gate resistor sits between the driver and the MOSFET gate, and its value is a compromise between switching speed and the stress that fast switching creates. A small resistor charges the gate quickly, minimizing time spent in the linear region where the MOSFET dissipates the most power — important for a load switch that may be turned on into a capacitive load or a stalled motor. But fast turn-on produces a high di/dt, which couples into the input supply through the source inductance and can cause ringing on the gate and drain, potentially exceeding the gate's absolute maximum or the drain's SOA. A larger resistor slows the transition, reducing ringing and conducted/radiated emissions, but increases switching loss and can extend the inrush current duration.

My approach: start from the MOSFET's total gate charge and the driver's source/sink current to estimate the raw switching time, then add series resistance to bring the edge rate to what the layout and the load can tolerate. I'd check the gate loop inductance — the resistor only damps the loop if it's placed close to the gate with a short return path — and verify the resulting Vgs waveform on the bench for overshoot and ringing. For a load switch specifically, I'd also consider whether a separate turn-on and turn-off path (a diode in parallel with the resistor) is warranted, so turn-off can be fast for protection while turn-on stays slow to limit inrush. Finally, I'd confirm the resistor's power rating during the switching transient and check that the gate voltage stays within the datasheet's absolute maximum across temperature.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you detect that the gate resistor is causing ringing rather than the layout, and what would you change first? What role does the MOSFET's input capacitance versus its reverse-transfer capacitance play in the switching loss you're trying to control?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision hinges on how much of the ADC's error budget the reference is allowed to consume. A dedicated reference IC gives you a specified initial accuracy, a temperature coefficient, and — critically — a specified long-term drift and output noise, all of which are guaranteed over the operating range. A filtered supply rail is cheaper and saves a component, but its accuracy and drift are whatever the regulator delivers, and its noise is whatever the filter leaves behind; neither is characterized as a reference, so you can't put a number on it in the error budget.

The parameters I'd actually compare: initial tolerance (as a fraction of an LSB at the target resolution), temperature coefficient in ppm/°C multiplied by the operating range, output voltage noise density integrated over the ADC's bandwidth (this directly adds to the conversion noise), long-term drift, and the reference's output impedance and load regulation — because the ADC's reference input draws a switched current during conversion, and a reference that can't recover from that charge injection will cause gain errors that vary with code. I'd also check the reference's dropout and quiescent current if the rail is battery-powered, and whether it needs a bypass capacitor sized for stability.

For a 16-bit converter, the reference error budget is often a meaningful fraction of one LSB, so a dedicated reference is usually the right call unless the measurement requirements are genuinely loose. The filtered rail can work when the ADC's own internal reference is adequate or when the system is calibrated against a known standard at manufacturing, but then you're relying on calibration to absorb drift you can't otherwise bound.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you quantify the reference's contribution to the ADC's effective number of bits? What would you check on the bench to confirm the reference is settling adequately between conversions?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?

**Answer:** Temperature-dependent behavior with a fixed load points at a parameter that drifts with temperature — either in the regulator's control loop, a passive component, or a connection. I'd start by separating the regulator from the rest of the board: measure the input voltage at the regulator's input pin, not at the supply, because a connector or trace resistance that rises with temperature can cause the input to sag under the same load. Then I'd look at the switching waveform itself — is the ripple increase at the switching frequency (suggesting the output capacitor's ESR has risen, or the inductor has gone into saturation earlier), or is it low-frequency instability (suggesting the loop's phase margin has degraded)?

Common culprits: the output capacitor's ESR and capacitance both drift with temperature, and if the loop compensation was marginal at room temperature, the reduced damping at high temperature can push it into oscillation. The inductor's saturation current falls as temperature rises, so a design that was just barely adequate at 25°C can start saturating at 85°C, producing current-limit behavior and dropout. The feedback divider's resistors drift, shifting the setpoint. And the regulator's internal reference and error amplifier have their own temperature coefficients.

I'd use a thermal chamber or a heat gun with a thermocouple to map the failure against temperature, then swap or re-characterize the suspect component — measure the output capacitor's ESR at temperature, check the inductor current waveform for the flat-topping that indicates saturation, and verify the loop's phase margin with a network analyzer or a load-step response at temperature. The fix is usually to re-derive the compensation with the worst-case component values, or to specify components whose parameters are stable over the range.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish output-capacitor ESR drift from inductor saturation as the cause, using only a scope? What would you change in the compensation network if the phase margin is marginal only at high temperature?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?

**Answer:** The point of a hardware POST is to verify the analog signal chain using a known, on-board stimulus and independent monitoring, so that a failure is caught even if the firmware or the ADC is misbehaving. I'd design it around a few checks: a reference-voltage monitor that confirms the ADC's reference is within tolerance before any conversion is trusted; a rail monitor on each analog supply that confirms the rails are up and within window; and a signal-path check that injects a known stimulus — a divided-down reference, a precision current into a known resistor, or a switchable calibration source — at the front-end input and verifies the response through a comparator or a dedicated monitor channel, not through the main ADC alone.

The independence matters: if the only thing checking the signal chain is the ADC and the firmware reading it, then a stuck ADC or a hung firmware passes the test. So I'd want the POST to have its own comparator or window detector on the analog output, and its own sequencer or state machine that can flag a failure to a hardware fault line. I'd also want the POST to be verifiable — a way to inject a fault deliberately during design verification and confirm the POST catches it — and to fail safe, meaning a failed POST holds the device in a safe state rather than allowing it to proceed.

The trade-offs are board area, cost, and test time. I'd scope the POST to the checks that matter for safety and for the measurements the device depends on, and document the coverage explicitly so the risk analysis can reference it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify during design validation that the POST actually catches a failed reference rather than passing on a marginal one? What would you do if the POST's own stimulus source drifts over the device's life?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the firmware lead argues that the ADC sampling rate should be increased from 100 Hz to 1 kHz to improve measurement resolution through oversampling. You believe the higher rate will alias 50/60 Hz noise into the passband and require a much steeper anti-aliasing filter, increasing component count and board area. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a technical disagreement to resolve with data rather than a position to defend. First I'd make sure I understand the firmware lead's actual goal — if the concern is resolution, oversampling can genuinely help, but only if the noise being averaged is white and the signal is band-limited before the converter. The risk I'm worried about is that raising the sample rate without moving the anti-aliasing corner lets mains hum and its harmonics fold down into the band of interest, and then no amount of averaging recovers the signal.

I'd propose we quantify it: model or measure the noise spectrum at the ADC input with the current front-end, then compute what aliasing would occur at 1 kHz sampling with the existing filter, and what filter would be needed to prevent it. If the numbers show the existing filter is adequate at 1 kHz, the firmware lead is right and I'd support the change. If they show a steeper filter is needed, we'd compare the cost of that filter against the benefit of the higher rate — and consider whether a middle ground, like a modest rate increase paired with a simple second-order filter, meets both goals.

The key is to keep the discussion on the shared requirement — measurement accuracy — rather than on whose approach is preferred, and to bring the risk analysis and the error budget into the conversation so the decision is traceable. If we still disagree after the data, I'd escalate to the systems lead with the analysis in hand rather than let it stall the schedule.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the data showed the higher rate was fine but the firmware lead's oversampling implementation introduced its own latency problem? How would you document the resolution of this disagreement for the design history file?
