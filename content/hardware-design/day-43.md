## Q1: How would you approach designing a power supply architecture for a medical device that contains both a high-resolution analog front-end and a motor driver, where the motor can draw 1A peaks and the analog section requires a noise floor below 50 µV RMS?

**Answer:** The fundamental challenge here is that you have two loads with conflicting requirements sharing a common power source. The motor draws large, fast current transients that can create voltage dips and switching noise, while the analog front-end needs a clean, stable supply to maintain its noise floor. I would start by separating the power domains as early as possible — ideally at the battery or input connector level. The motor driver should have its own dedicated supply path, and the analog section should be fed from a separate regulator chain. The key decision is where to place the separation: if the motor and analog circuits share a common input source, I would use a two-stage approach for the analog rail — first a switching regulator to step down efficiently, then a low-noise LDO after it to provide the final clean rail. The LDO's PSRR becomes critical here, especially at the motor's switching and commutation frequencies. I would also pay careful attention to the ground topology. A star ground or split ground plane with a single point of connection between the motor return path and the analog ground is essential to prevent motor currents from flowing through the analog ground reference. The layout should keep the motor's high-current loop short and physically separated from the analog circuitry. For the noise floor requirement, I would calculate the budget across the entire signal chain — the LDO's output noise, the reference noise, and the ADC's own noise — and verify that the power supply contribution is well below the 50 µV RMS target. Finally, I would add bulk capacitance at the motor driver input to handle the 1A peaks locally, preventing those transients from propagating back to the shared input rail.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between using separate batteries versus a single battery with multiple regulators? What specific LDO parameters would you evaluate to ensure adequate PSRR at the motor's switching frequencies?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a precision analog front-end's output shows a periodic disturbance at approximately 1–10 Hz, even when the input is shorted to ground, and the disturbance amplitude varies with the power supply voltage?

**Answer:** A periodic disturbance in the 1–10 Hz range with the input shorted points to something internal to the circuit rather than an external signal coupling in. The fact that the amplitude varies with the supply voltage is a strong clue — it suggests the disturbance is riding on the power rail itself. I would start by probing the power supply rail with a scope set to AC coupling and a long time base to see if the same 1–10 Hz periodicity appears there. If it does, the source could be a control loop oscillation in a regulator — for example, an LDO or switching converter that's marginally stable and oscillating at a low frequency. This can happen when the output capacitor's ESR is too low or too high for the regulator's compensation, or when the load current is near the boundary of the regulator's operating modes. I would also check whether the disturbance correlates with any periodic activity in the system — for instance, a microcontroller waking periodically to take measurements, or a wireless radio transmitting on a slow duty cycle. If the disturbance is thermal in nature, it could be a self-heating effect where a component's characteristics drift cyclically. Another possibility is a ground loop or thermocouple effect at a connection point, though that would typically be more random than periodic. I would use a spectrum analyzer or FFT on the scope to characterize the exact frequency and look for harmonics, which can help identify the source. I would also try powering the circuit from a clean bench supply to see if the disturbance disappears — that would confirm the issue is in the power path rather than the analog circuitry itself.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the disturbance disappears when powered from a clean bench supply, how would you isolate whether the problem is in the regulator circuit or upstream in the input power? What specific measurements would you take to characterize the disturbance frequency and amplitude precisely?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting between a SAR ADC and a sigma-delta ADC for a medical device that measures a slowly varying physiological signal (e.g., temperature or pressure) with high resolution, and what are the key trade-offs you'd consider?

**Answer:** For a slowly varying physiological signal, both architectures can work, but the choice depends on the specific requirements. SAR ADCs offer several advantages: they're inherently sample-by-sample converters with no latency, they have a well-defined transfer function that's easy to characterize, and they consume power proportional to sampling rate — which is attractive for battery-powered devices. Their main limitation is that achieving high resolution (above 16 bits) requires very careful layout and a clean reference, and the input bandwidth must be limited with an anti-aliasing filter before the converter. Sigma-delta ADCs, on the other hand, use oversampling and noise shaping to push quantization noise out of the band of interest, which allows them to achieve very high resolution (20–24 bits) with relatively relaxed analog front-end requirements. They're also inherently better at rejecting aliasing because the modulator samples at a much higher rate than the output data rate. However, sigma-delta converters have a latency — the digital filter introduces a delay between the analog input and the digital output — which matters if the signal is used in a real-time control loop. They also consume more power because the modulator runs continuously at the oversampled rate. For a temperature or pressure sensor that changes slowly, I would lean toward a sigma-delta ADC if I need very high resolution and the signal is truly low-bandwidth, because the oversampling and noise shaping relax the anti-aliasing filter requirements and the digital filter can be configured to reject 50/60 Hz mains interference. If I need deterministic sampling with low latency, or if power consumption at low sample rates is critical, a SAR ADC might be the better choice. I would also consider the system-level implications: the SAR ADC requires a precise external reference and a well-designed anti-aliasing filter, while the sigma-delta ADC often integrates the reference and offers programmable filter settings.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would the presence of 50/60 Hz mains interference affect your choice? What specific datasheet parameters would you compare between the two options to make the final decision?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based latch circuit for a medical device that must maintain a fault condition (e.g., over-temperature or overcurrent) even after the triggering event has cleared, while ensuring the latch can be reset only through a deliberate, safe action?

**Answer:** The core requirement here is that a fault condition must be remembered and acted upon even after the triggering event goes away — the system must not automatically restart into a potentially unsafe state. I would design this using a comparator to detect the fault condition, feeding into a latch circuit that holds the fault state. The classic approach is a thyristor-style latch using two transistors, or a comparator with positive feedback (hysteresis) that creates a latching behavior. A more modern approach uses a dedicated latch IC or a comparator with an enable pin. The key design decisions are: the threshold accuracy of the fault detection, the response time, and the reset mechanism. For the reset, I would require a deliberate action — typically a physical reset button that the operator must press, or a reset signal from a supervisory circuit that only asserts after the fault condition has been cleared and a safe state has been confirmed. The latch should also drive a visual indicator (LED) and potentially an audible alarm so the operator knows a fault has occurred. In a medical device, the reset path must be designed so that it cannot happen automatically — for example, the reset button should require a sustained press (debounced) and should only be effective when the fault condition is no longer present. I would also consider whether the latch should be powered from a separate rail that remains active even if the main power is interrupted, so the fault state is remembered. However, this raises the question of whether the device should restart in a fault state after power cycling — in some cases, a power-on reset that clears the latch and requires the system to go through a safe startup sequence is acceptable, provided the startup sequence includes a self-test that would re-detect the fault. The critical safety analysis is to determine what failure modes require a latched shutdown versus what can be automatically recovered.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the scenario where the device loses power while in a latched fault state — should it restart in the fault state or clear the latch? How would you verify that the latch circuit itself is fail-safe?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review, the firmware lead proposes replacing the hardware-based over-temperature protection circuit (a comparator and latch that shuts down the motor driver) with a firmware-based solution that monitors temperature via the ADC and shuts down the motor through a GPIO. The firmware lead argues this will save board space, reduce cost, and allow more flexible threshold adjustment. You believe the hardware approach is necessary because the protection must work even if the firmware hangs or the ADC fails. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** This is a classic safety architecture disagreement, and my approach would be to focus on the engineering analysis rather than the personal disagreement. First, I would acknowledge the firmware lead's valid points — a firmware-based approach does offer flexibility in threshold adjustment and could save cost and board space. Then I would reframe the discussion around the safety requirements. In a medical device, the over-temperature protection is a safety function, and the question is whether it needs to be independent of the main processor. I would reference the relevant standard — IEC 60601 requires that safety functions be designed so that a single fault condition doesn't lead to a hazardous situation. If the firmware hangs or the ADC fails, and the protection is implemented in firmware, then a single fault (firmware hang) defeats the protection. A hardware comparator and latch provides an independent layer of protection that doesn't depend on the processor executing correctly. I would propose a risk analysis to formally evaluate both approaches — looking at the failure modes of each and the resulting risk. I would also suggest a middle ground: keep the hardware comparator as the primary safety protection, but allow the firmware to adjust the threshold through a DAC or digital potentiometer if flexibility is truly needed. This gives the firmware lead the flexibility they want while maintaining the independence of the protection. If we still disagree after the analysis, I would escalate to the project's safety officer or risk management team to make a formal decision based on the risk assessment. The key is to keep the discussion focused on patient safety and regulatory requirements, not on who wins the argument.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the risk analysis to formally compare the two approaches? If the project schedule is tight and the firmware lead insists on their approach, how would you prioritize the safety analysis against the schedule pressure?
