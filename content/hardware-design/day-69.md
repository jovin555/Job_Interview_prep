# hardware-design — Day 69

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start by deciding where the shunt sits in the circuit, because that choice drives the amplifier topology. A high-side shunt keeps the motor's return path referenced to system ground, which is usually preferable for safety and for avoiding ground disturbance, but it forces the amplifier to handle a large common-mode voltage that swings with the supply — so a dedicated current-sense amplifier with high common-mode rejection and a wide common-mode input range is typically the right part rather than a generic op-amp difference stage. A low-side shunt is simpler and cheaper because the common-mode is near ground, but it lifts the motor's return above ground, which can disturb other ground-referenced circuitry and complicates fault detection if the load can be disconnected.

For the shunt value itself, size it from the accuracy and power budget together. The full-scale voltage across the shunt sets the signal level the amplifier must resolve, and a larger shunt gives a better signal-to-noise ratio but dissipates more power as I²R. At 1A, even a 100 mΩ shunt burns 100 mW at full scale, which may be unacceptable in a small enclosure, so there's a real trade between resolution and self-heating. Self-heating matters twice over: it wastes power, and it shifts the shunt's own resistance via its temperature coefficient, which directly eats into the ±2% budget. A low-TCR shunt — a few tens of ppm/°C rather than hundreds — keeps that drift small.

For bidirectional measurement, the amplifier needs a reference or offset so that negative current produces a valid output within the single-supply range, typically by biasing the output to mid-rail. Then I'd build an error budget: shunt tolerance and TCR, amplifier gain error and gain drift, offset voltage and its drift, and CMRR error from the common-mode swing. If the sum is marginal, I'd either tighten the dominant term or add a calibration step. Layout also matters — a Kelvin (four-terminal) connection to the shunt keeps the high-current path out of the sense lines, and the sense traces should be matched and kept away from switching nodes.

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?
**Answer:** The core distinction is that a comparator is designed to be operated open-loop and to saturate cleanly at its output, while an op-amp is designed for closed-loop linear operation and is only incidentally usable as a comparator. For a protection threshold, the decision usually comes down to speed, output interface, and hysteresis behavior.

A dedicated comparator is generally the better choice when the output must drive a logic input or a latch directly, when the decision must be fast and well-defined, and when I want built-in hysteresis or a specified propagation delay. Comparators are characterized for exactly this — input offset, propagation delay, output logic levels — and many have open-drain outputs that interface cleanly to a pull-up or to a wired-OR fault bus. An op-amp used as a comparator can work, but its saturation recovery time is often poorly specified, its output swing may not reach valid logic levels, and it can behave unpredictably if the inputs are driven far apart or if it's slow to come out of saturation. That's a poor foundation for a safety-relevant threshold.

The other thing I'd design in deliberately is hysteresis. A bare threshold will chatter when the input sits near the trip point, especially with noise on the sensed signal, and chatter on a protection line is dangerous — it can cause repeated resets or an indeterminate state. Positive feedback around the comparator, or a comparator with built-in hysteresis, sets clean trip and release points. I'd also think about the failure mode: what does the output do if the comparator loses power, or if the sense line opens? For a protection function, the design should fail toward the safe state, which sometimes means choosing the output polarity and pull-up arrangement so that a lost signal reads as "fault" rather than "okay."

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?
**Answer:** Audible noise from an inductor means the regulator is producing periodic current pulses in the audible band, roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz, and the inductor's windings or core are physically moving in response. The output being within spec doesn't mean the behavior is acceptable — it's a symptom worth understanding, and in a medical device it can also indicate the regulator is entering an unintended operating mode.

The first thing I'd check is whether the regulator is in a pulse-skipping or burst mode at light load. Many switching regulators drop into a low-power mode to maintain efficiency when the load is small, and in that mode they deliver bursts of pulses separated by idle periods. If the burst repetition rate lands in the audible range, the inductor sings. The fix is usually to either keep the regulator in forced-continuous-conduction mode (accepting lower light-load efficiency), adjust the mode-selection threshold, or change the load conditions so it doesn't enter that mode. I'd confirm this by scoping the switch node and looking at the burst envelope, not just the output ripple.

If it's not burst mode, I'd look at loop stability. A marginally stable loop can produce low-frequency oscillation or subharmonic behavior that modulates the switching, and that modulation can be audible. I'd check the compensation network and the output capacitor's ESR, since both affect the loop. I'd also consider whether the inductor itself is mechanically loose or poorly potted — some inductors are simply noisier than others, and a shielded, potted part can be much quieter. Finally, I'd verify the switching frequency itself isn't being divided down or dithered in a way that creates an audible component. The approach is to identify the mechanism first — burst mode, loop instability, or mechanical — because each has a different fix, and changing components blindly wastes time.

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware-based self-test is to verify that the analog signal chain is actually functional before the device is trusted to make measurements, and to do so in a way that doesn't depend on the very firmware and ADC that might be faulty. So I'd start by asking what could fail silently: a broken sensor connection, a dead amplifier, a stuck reference, a shorted input, or a rail that's out of tolerance. The self-test should be able to distinguish "the chain is working" from "the chain is producing plausible-looking but wrong data."

A common approach is to inject a known stimulus into the front-end and check that the output responds as expected. That could be a precision reference voltage switched into the amplifier input through an analog switch, or a known current injected into a sensor node, with the response compared against a window. The key is that the stimulus and the comparison should be as independent of the normal signal path as practical — if the test uses the same ADC and firmware that the measurement uses, a fault in either could mask a real problem. Where possible, I'd use a separate comparator or a hardware window detector to check the response, so the pass/fail decision doesn't rely solely on the main processor.

I'd also want to verify the supply rails and the voltage reference independently, since a drifted reference corrupts every measurement downstream. Rail monitors and a reference check give an early indication that the chain's foundation is sound. The self-test should run at power-on and ideally be re-runnable, and its result should be latched or reported in a way that the system can act on. Finally, I'd think about what the self-test does on failure — it should drive the device to a safe, clearly-indicated state rather than allowing measurements to proceed with a known-bad chain.

- How would you avoid the self-test itself becoming a single point of failure?
- What's the difference between a self-test that verifies the signal chain and one that verifies the measurement accuracy?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** The disagreement is really about an assumption, not about the connector itself: is the cable connected once and left in place, or is it expected to be replaced in the field? If we disagree on that, we'll never agree on the connector, so the first thing I'd do is surface the assumption and get it resolved with evidence rather than opinion. That means looking at the intended use, the instructions for use, and any field-service or replacement strategy. If the device is genuinely single-connection, the reliability engineer's concern may be overstated; if field replacement is plausible — even if not the primary use case — then the concern is legitimate and I'd want to understand how many cycles are actually expected.

I'd approach it collaboratively rather than defensively. The reliability engineer is raising a real risk, and my job is to make sure we're solving the right problem. I'd ask what cycle count they're assuming and where that number comes from, and I'd share my understanding of the use case. If we can't resolve it from the requirements, that's a signal that the requirements themselves are ambiguous and need to be clarified — which is a better outcome than either of us winning the argument.

If it turns out field replacement is a real scenario, I'd evaluate the higher-cycle connector on its merits: cost, board space, mating force, and whether it introduces any new risks. If the cost is justified by the use case, I'd support the change. If the use case genuinely doesn't require it, I'd document the rationale and the assumption so the decision is traceable. Either way, the decision should be recorded with its reasoning, because in a regulated environment the "why" matters as much as the "what."

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you document a decision like this so it's defensible in a design history file?
- If the requirements are ambiguous, how would you go about getting them clarified without stalling the project?
