# hardware-design — Day 110

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a MOSFET for a load switch that must block reverse current when the device is powered off, and what failure modes would you check beyond the datasheet's on-resistance?

**Answer:** The starting point is recognizing that "block reverse current when off" is a body-diode problem, not an RDS(on) problem. In a standard N- or P-channel MOSFET, the intrinsic body diode conducts from source to drain (for an N-channel) regardless of gate state, so a single FET used as a load switch will leak current backward through that diode whenever the output is held above the input. The first architectural decision is therefore whether to use a back-to-back arrangement — two FETs in series with their body diodes opposing — or a single FET oriented so the body diode blocks in the direction that matters, accepting that the FET can only block in one polarity. For a battery-powered device where the concern is the load holding up the rail after the switch is commanded off, back-to-back is usually the honest answer, and the cost is roughly double the RDS(on) and conduction loss.

Beyond RDS(on), I'd check: the body diode's reverse leakage over temperature (it roughly doubles every 10 °C and can dominate standby budget); the gate threshold voltage range and its temperature coefficient, because a logic-level FET driven near threshold at cold temperature may not fully enhance; the safe operating area for the inrush event at turn-on into a capacitive load; and the SOA derating for repetitive pulses. I'd also check the gate charge, since a slow turn-on to limit inrush means the FET spends time in the linear region and the SOA chart, not the RDS(on) spec, governs survival. Finally, I'd verify the off-state drain-source leakage and confirm the gate pull-down or pull-up is defined when the control signal is floating — an undefined gate is a classic path to unintended conduction.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the back-to-back pair's total RDS(on) against a thermal budget in a sealed enclosure? What would you check on the bench to confirm the switch actually blocks in the off state at temperature extremes?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage-mode and a current-mode control architecture for a buck converter that must supply a fast-transient digital load while also feeding a sensitive analog rail through a downstream LDO?

**Answer:** I'd frame the decision around what each architecture is good at and what the downstream LDO actually buys me. Current-mode control gives me a well-behaved single-pole response in the useful range, cycle-by-cycle current limiting, and better line rejection, which matters when the input is a battery whose voltage moves. It also makes the compensation easier to stabilize across a wide input range. The trade-offs are the need for slope compensation above 50% duty, sensitivity to noise on the current-sense signal, and a minimum on-time that can limit the achievable duty at high switching frequency. Voltage-mode control is simpler in the sense that it doesn't need current sensing, handles very low duty cycles cleanly, and is less perturbed by switching noise on the sense node — but it's a second-order system, needs a more careful Type III compensation, and has no inherent cycle-by-cycle current limit.

For this specific case — fast digital transients plus a sensitive analog rail behind an LDO — I'd lean current-mode for the fast transient response and the built-in current limit, then rely on the LDO's PSRR to clean up the residual ripple at the analog rail. The key insight is that the LDO's rejection falls off with frequency, so I'd want the converter's switching frequency and its loop crossover placed where the LDO still has meaningful PSRR, and I'd verify the composite ripple at the analog rail rather than assuming the LDO solves everything. I'd also confirm the converter's transient droop stays within the LDO's headroom, because a droop that pushes the LDO out of regulation defeats the whole arrangement.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the crossover frequency of the converter relative to the LDO's PSRR curve? What would you check on the bench to confirm the composite supply noise at the analog rail meets the budget?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting the switching frequency for a boost converter that must generate a 24V rail from a 5V input at 300mA, where the board is space-constrained and the rail feeds a sensitive analog load?

**Answer:** The duty cycle here is high — roughly 1 − 5/24, so around 79% — which immediately constrains the choice. Higher switching frequency shrinks the inductor and output capacitor, which helps the space constraint, but it also raises switching losses, increases the impact of the minimum on-time and the inductor's AC resistance, and pushes the fundamental and its harmonics into a region where they're harder to filter and more likely to couple into the analog load. Lower frequency allows a larger inductor with lower DCR and better efficiency, but the inductor and output cap grow, and the ripple current in the inductor grows too, which affects output ripple and the boost's stability margin at high duty.

My approach would be to start from the ripple current target — typically 30–40% of the DC inductor current — and see what inductance that implies at a few candidate frequencies, then check whether that inductor fits the board and whether its saturation current covers the peak. I'd also check the converter's minimum off-time, because at 79% duty a high switching frequency can leave too little off-time for the high-side switch to reset, forcing duty-cycle jitter or pulse-skipping. For a sensitive analog load, I'd bias toward the lower end of the practical range and add a post-filter or a downstream LDO, and I'd plan to characterize the output ripple and any low-frequency beat with the analog front-end's sampling before committing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you evaluate whether the boost's output ripple is dominated by the inductor ripple current or by the output capacitor's ESR? What would you do if the chosen frequency produced an audible beat with the analog sampling rate?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?

**Answer:** Audible noise from an inductor means the converter is producing mechanical excitation in the 20 Hz–20 kHz band, which at light load almost always points to the converter entering a pulse-skipping or burst mode — the loop is dropping into discontinuous operation, and the burst repetition rate lands in the audible range. The output can still be within spec because the ripple during each burst is small and the average is regulated; the problem is the burst frequency, not the regulation.

My first step would be to confirm the mechanism: scope the switch node and the inductor current at light load and look for grouped pulses with a low repetition rate, and correlate the repetition rate with the pitch. Then I'd check whether the converter has a forced-PWM or a mode-select pin and whether the design is inadvertently allowing the light-load efficiency mode. If the audible mode is inherent, options are: force PWM (accepting the efficiency hit at light load), raise the burst frequency by adjusting the compensation or the minimum off-time if the part allows it, or change the inductor to one with a higher self-resonant frequency and better mechanical damping — some inductors are simply noisier than others at the same ripple because of winding and core construction. I'd also check for loose mounting or a mechanically resonant board, since the same ripple on a better-damped inductor may be inaudible. The fix should be verified at the actual light-load currents the device will see, not just at no load.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether forcing PWM is acceptable given the device's standby power budget? What inductor parameters would you compare to reduce the acoustic signature without changing the electrical design?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a decision that needs to be made on evidence rather than on assertion from either side, and I'd want the manufacturing engineer to feel that their cost concern is being taken seriously rather than dismissed. My approach would be to separate the two claims: cost and technical risk. On cost, I'd ask for the actual delta at the volumes we're planning, because "too expensive" often turns out to be a small per-board number that's dwarfed by the cost of a reflow yield problem or a field return. On technical risk, I'd lay out specifically what the finish affects — solder joint reliability on fine-pitch BGA, coplanarity and wetting on the ADC's pads, and any concern about the finish's effect on the high-frequency performance of the analog section — and I'd propose a concrete way to test the cheaper option rather than arguing about it abstractly.

The constructive path is usually a build-and-test: run a small pilot lot with the cheaper finish, do the cross-section and X-ray inspection on the BGA, and run the ADC through its accuracy characterization on those boards. If the cheaper finish passes, I'd be the first to adopt it — the goal is a reliable product at a good cost, not defending my original choice. If it fails, we have data rather than opinion. I'd also bring in the quality and regulatory perspective, since for a Class II device a finish change is a change to the design history file and needs to be assessed for its effect on the validated process. The key is to keep the disagreement about the evidence and the risk tolerance, and to make sure the decision and its rationale are documented either way.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the pilot-lot evaluation so the result is defensible to a regulator? What would you do if the manufacturing engineer's cost pressure came with a schedule deadline that didn't allow for a pilot lot?
