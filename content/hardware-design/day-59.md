# hardware-design — Day 59

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a load switch versus a discrete MOSFET-plus-gate-driver for a battery-powered medical device that needs to power-gate a peripheral rail, and what would drive the decision?
**Answer:** The decision comes down to how much integration the design can tolerate versus how much control and transparency it needs. An integrated load switch bundles the pass FET, gate drive, and often reverse-current blocking, slew-rate control, thermal shutdown, and fault flag into one part — that reduces BOM count, board area, and the number of things that can be mis-specified, which matters in a portable device where every square millimeter and every microamp of quiescent current counts. A discrete MOSFET plus driver gives more freedom: you can choose an FET with exactly the Rds(on), SOA, and thermal path you need, tune the gate drive for a specific inrush profile, and place the pass element where the thermal copper is. The trade-off is that you now own the gate-drive design, the inrush limiting, the reverse-current path, and the fault behavior — all of which are easy to get subtly wrong.

My selection criteria would be: (1) current level and Rds(on) budget — at low currents an integrated switch usually wins on both size and quiescent current; at higher currents the FET's thermal path starts to dominate and a discrete part with a good copper pour may be better; (2) whether I need controlled slew rate for inrush limiting — many integrated switches have this built in, whereas a discrete design needs an RC gate network or a dedicated driver; (3) whether reverse current must be blocked — if the peripheral can back-feed the rail when the main supply drops, an integrated switch with true reverse blocking saves a separate ideal-diode circuit; (4) fault reporting — if the system needs to know a rail failed to come up, an integrated switch's fault flag is a clean signal, whereas a discrete design needs extra sensing; (5) quiescent current in the off state, which is often the deciding factor in a device that sleeps for long periods. For a medical device I'd also check that the chosen part's fault behavior is deterministic and documented, since "what happens when this rail faults" is a question that will come up in risk analysis.

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?
**Answer:** Audible noise from an inductor means the switching frequency — or a harmonic of it — has landed in the audible band, or the regulator has entered a pulse-skipping or burst mode whose repetition rate is in the audible range. The first thing I'd do is confirm which: put a current probe or a small series sense resistor on the inductor and look at the switching node and inductor current on a scope with the bandwidth limited, because at light load many regulators drop into PFM or burst mode, and the burst repetition rate is often a few hundred hertz to a few kilohertz — squarely audible. If that's the cause, the "fix" is usually a mode or frequency change rather than a component change: some regulators let you force PWM at light load (at the cost of efficiency), some let you shift the switching frequency above 20 kHz, and some have a burst-mode threshold you can adjust.

If it's not burst mode, I'd look at whether the inductor is mechanically resonating — some unshielded or loosely wound inductors sing when the ripple current has a strong AC component, and the fix is a different construction (shielded, molded, or potted) rather than an electrical change. I'd also check for subharmonic oscillation or instability that's producing a low-frequency envelope on top of the switching waveform, which can happen if the compensation is marginal or the ESR of the output capacitor is very low. And I'd check whether the noise is actually coming from a ceramic capacitor — MLCCs are piezoelectric and can sing just as loudly as an inductor, which is a common red herring. The diagnostic sequence is: scope the switching node and inductor current, identify the dominant frequency, then decide whether it's a mode issue, a component-construction issue, or a stability issue.

**Possible follow-ups:** If forcing PWM at light load fixes the noise but costs you standby efficiency, how would you weigh that trade-off in a battery-powered device? / How would you tell the difference between an inductor singing and a ceramic capacitor singing?

## Q3: How would you approach characterizing the power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR) of an analog front-end, and how would you use that measurement to decide whether the supply noise is acceptable?
**Answer:** PSRR is the ratio of a disturbance injected on the supply to the resulting disturbance at the output, usually expressed in dB, and it's frequency-dependent — it typically degrades as frequency rises because the amplifier's loop gain falls off. To characterize it, I'd inject a known AC signal onto the supply rail (either with a signal generator through a bias tee, or with a network analyzer's source) and measure the output amplitude at each frequency, sweeping from DC up to well past the bandwidth of interest. The key is to inject at the point where the real noise will appear — at the regulator output, not at the bench supply — because the impedance of the intervening network matters. I'd also measure it with the front-end in its actual gain configuration, since PSRR is often specified at unity gain and degrades at higher gain.

Once I have the PSRR curve, I'd combine it with the actual supply noise spectrum: if the regulator's switching ripple is, say, 10 mV at 500 kHz and the front-end's PSRR at 500 kHz is 40 dB, the output ripple contribution is about 100 µV — and I can compare that against the noise budget for the measurement. If it's too high, the options are to improve the PSRR (better amplifier, more loop gain, or a post-regulator LDO with good high-frequency rejection), to reduce the supply noise at the source (better filtering, a different switching frequency, or a linear pre-regulator for the analog rail), or to add a passive filter between the regulator and the front-end. The measurement is what tells you which of those is actually needed, rather than guessing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure PSRR if you don't have a network analyzer, and what are the limitations of that approach? / Why does PSRR typically degrade at higher frequencies, and what does that imply for a switching regulator's ripple?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a reverse-polarity protection circuit for a medical device that draws up to 2A peak, where the voltage drop must be minimized and no reverse leakage is acceptable?
**Answer:** The classic options are a series Schottky diode, a P-channel MOSFET in the high-side path, or an N-channel MOSFET with a charge pump, and the choice is driven by the drop and leakage requirements. A Schottky is simple and blocks reverse current completely, but at 2A the forward drop is typically 0.3–0.5V, which is a lot of wasted power and heat in a battery-powered device — and it eats into the headroom for the downstream regulator. A P-channel MOSFET in the high-side path has a much lower drop (set by Rds(on) × I), but you have to be careful about the gate drive: the gate must be pulled below the source to turn it on, and when the input is reversed the body diode and the gate network must not allow current to flow. A P-FET also has a body diode that conducts in the forward direction, so the "blocking" is really the channel being off — you need to make sure the gate is held at the source potential (or above) when the input is reversed, which usually means a resistor from gate to source plus a Zener or a small signal FET to clamp it.

For a 2A peak load with a tight drop budget, I'd lean toward a P-channel MOSFET with a carefully designed gate network, or an N-channel MOSFET with a charge pump if the drop needs to be even lower and the extra complexity is justified. The "no reverse leakage" requirement is the one that rules out a simple body-diode-only arrangement — I'd verify on the bench that with the input reversed, the current into the source is below the leakage spec, and I'd check it at temperature, because MOSFET leakage rises with temperature. I'd also consider an integrated ideal-diode controller, which handles the gate drive and the reverse-blocking logic in one part and is often the cleanest answer when the drop and leakage requirements are both tight.

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the software lead argues that the device's power-on self-test can be simplified by removing the hardware checks (rail monitors, reference checks) and relying on firmware to verify the system by reading the ADC and checking that values are in range. The software lead argues this reduces component count and board area. You believe the hardware checks are necessary because they verify the analog signal chain independently of the ADC and the firmware. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** I'd start by separating the two things the self-test is actually doing: verifying that the system is safe to operate, and verifying that the measurement chain is functioning. Those have different requirements, and conflating them is usually where the disagreement comes from. A firmware-only check that reads the ADC and confirms the value is in range can only tell you that the ADC is producing a plausible number — it can't distinguish between a correct measurement and a stuck or offset reading, and it can't detect a failure in the reference or the analog front-end if the failure happens to produce an in-range value. A hardware check — a rail monitor, a reference comparison, a known test signal injected into the front-end — verifies the chain independently of the ADC and the firmware, which is exactly what you want for a safety-related function.

I'd frame the conversation around the risk analysis rather than around component count: what failure modes does the self-test need to detect, and what's the detection capability of each approach for those failure modes? If the risk analysis says a stuck ADC or a drifted reference is a hazardous failure, then a firmware-only check that can't detect it is not adequate, regardless of how much board area it saves. I'd also point out that the hardware checks don't have to be expensive — a rail monitor is a small comparator or a supervisor IC, and a reference check can be as simple as a switched divider that the ADC reads against a known value. If the software lead's concern is genuinely board area, there may be a middle ground: keep the hardware checks that cover the hazardous failure modes and drop the ones that are redundant with firmware checks that are themselves adequate. The goal is to make the decision on the basis of what the self-test needs to detect, not on the basis of which team proposed it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document the outcome of this discussion so it's traceable in the design history file? / If the risk analysis shows the hardware check is required, how would you handle the schedule impact of adding it back in?
