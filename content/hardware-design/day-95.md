# hardware-design — Day 95

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?

**Answer:** Start by translating the system's timing budget into a frequency-tolerance requirement over the full operating temperature range, then split that budget between the crystal's own stability and the oscillator circuit's contributions. For a plain crystal, the frequency-vs-temperature curve is a cubic that's worst at the temperature extremes, so a part specified only at 25°C tells you almost nothing — you need the tolerance over the actual range, plus aging and initial accuracy. If the requirement is tight, the options are a TCXO, a crystal with a well-characterized temperature curve plus firmware compensation, or relaxing the requirement by deriving critical timing from a different source. Load capacitance matters: the specified CL must match the effective capacitance seen by the crystal (including PCB parasitics and pin capacitance), or the frequency will be pulled off nominal. Drive level should be within the crystal's rating — too much causes aging and even fracture, too little can hurt startup. On the bench I'd verify startup margin across temperature and supply voltage extremes, not just nominal: measure negative resistance (or drive level) with the crystal loaded, check startup time from cold, and confirm the oscillator doesn't fail to start at the cold corner where drive is weakest. I'd also check frequency against a reference at several temperatures and confirm the pull matches the datasheet curve, and look at the waveform for clipping or excessive drive.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative resistance margin without specialized equipment? What would you do if the cold-corner startup margin came out marginal but the part is already qualified?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision hinges on what the function actually needs to do. A comparator is designed for open-loop, saturated operation: it has high gain, fast response, and often built-in hysteresis, and its output is intended to drive a logic level. An op-amp used open-loop will also saturate, but its output swing, speed, and logic compatibility are not guaranteed, and it may be unstable or slow to recover from saturation — so using one as a threshold detector is a compromise unless the op-amp is specifically characterized for it. For a protection function, the key parameters are propagation delay (how fast the output responds after the input crosses the threshold), threshold accuracy over temperature (input offset voltage and its drift), hysteresis (to prevent chatter when the input sits near the threshold), and output type (push-pull vs open-drain, and whether it can drive the downstream logic or latch directly). I'd also consider whether the threshold needs to be adjustable, whether the input can go outside the supply rails (some comparators have rail-to-rail or beyond-the-rails inputs), and whether the part needs to keep working when the main supply is marginal. For a safety-relevant protection path, I'd favor a comparator with defined hysteresis and a specified propagation delay, and I'd verify the threshold and response time over temperature on the bench rather than trusting typical curves.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you add hysteresis to a comparator-based threshold detector, and what would you watch for? What failure modes of a comparator would concern you most in a protection path?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting the input filter for a switching regulator, and how would you decide whether a simple capacitor is enough versus needing an LC or π-filter?

**Answer:** Start from what the filter is actually protecting against and what it must not degrade. A switching regulator draws a pulsed current at the switching frequency and its harmonics, and that current flows through the input source impedance, producing ripple that can couple into sensitive analog circuitry or conduct out along the input cable. A single input capacitor handles the high-frequency component well if it's placed close to the regulator's input pins with a short, low-inductance return path — its job is to supply the transient current locally so it doesn't have to come from the source. But a capacitor alone doesn't attenuate lower-frequency ripple much, and it can interact with source inductance to form a resonance. If the conducted emissions or the ripple at the input need real attenuation, an LC filter adds a roll-off, and a π-filter (capacitor–inductor–capacitor) gives more attenuation at the cost of more parts and a second resonance to damp. The decision comes down to the required attenuation at the switching frequency and its harmonics, the source impedance, and the load's sensitivity. I'd also check the filter's damping — an undamped LC can ring and even amplify at resonance — and make sure the inductor's saturation current and DC resistance are adequate, and that the capacitor's ESR and ripple current rating are suitable. On the bench I'd measure input ripple and conducted emissions with and without the filter, and check for resonance peaking under load transients.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you damp an LC input filter to avoid resonance peaking? What would you check if adding the filter made the regulator unstable?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?

**Answer:** The correlation with handling and movement points toward a mechanical or connection-related cause rather than a pure electrical design flaw, so I'd start by trying to reproduce the failure deterministically — flexing the board, tapping the enclosure, wiggling connectors and cables, and varying temperature — while monitoring the circuit's behavior. I'd instrument the suspect nodes so I can see what changes at the moment of failure: supply rails, reference voltages, and the signal path, ideally with a scope set to trigger on the fault. Common culprits are intermittent connector contacts, solder joints (especially on connectors and large components), board flex cracking a trace or joint, a component whose value shifts with temperature or mechanical stress, or a cable that's being strained in the assembled position. I'd also check whether the enclosure changes the electrical environment — added capacitance or grounding paths, or a shield that's now connected differently — and whether the failure is actually electrical or a firmware response to a marginal signal. The key discipline is to change one variable at a time and confirm the fault follows it, rather than shotgunning fixes. Once I have a candidate, I'd verify by deliberately reproducing the condition and confirming the fix removes it across repeated trials.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cold solder joint from a cracked trace without destructive inspection? What would you do if the fault only appears after the unit has been assembled and can't be instrumented in place?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the thermal engineer argues that your chosen linear regulator for a low-noise analog rail will dissipate too much power inside the sealed enclosure and proposes replacing it with a switching regulator followed by a small LDO. You believe the switching regulator's ripple and switching noise, even after the LDO, could degrade the analog front-end's noise floor. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a shared engineering problem rather than a contest of positions, because both concerns are legitimate — thermal margin in a sealed enclosure is real, and so is the risk of raising the analog noise floor. First I'd get the numbers on the table: the actual power dissipation and the resulting temperature rise inside the enclosure versus the thermal limits, and the analog front-end's noise budget versus what a switching regulator plus LDO would realistically contribute. That means looking at the LDO's PSRR at the switching frequency and its harmonics, not just at DC, and considering whether the switching frequency and its harmonics land where the front-end is sensitive. Then I'd look for options that satisfy both constraints: a lower-dropout linear regulator to cut dissipation, a switching regulator with a higher switching frequency and good layout to push noise out of band, additional filtering between the switching stage and the LDO, or a separate quiet rail for the most sensitive circuitry. If the disagreement persists, I'd propose a bench test — build or simulate both configurations and measure the actual noise floor and thermal rise — so the decision is made on evidence rather than opinion. I'd document the trade-off and the rationale in the design record, and if the thermal concern wins, make sure the noise impact is characterized and accepted deliberately rather than assumed away.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the bench test to make the comparison fair and conclusive? If the data showed the switching solution met the noise budget but with little margin, how would you decide whether to accept it?
