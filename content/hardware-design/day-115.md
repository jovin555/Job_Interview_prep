# hardware-design — Day 115

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the output capacitor for a buck converter that feeds a load with periodic high-current bursts, where the output voltage must stay within a tight tolerance during the transient and the capacitor also has to satisfy the loop stability requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as two separate but coupled requirements — transient response and loop stability — and size the capacitor to satisfy both, then verify the choice in the loop.

For the transient requirement, the output capacitor is the energy reservoir that supplies the load during the interval before the converter's control loop can respond. The relevant parameters are the capacitance value, the ESR, and the ESL. During a load step, the voltage deviation has three components: an immediate IR drop across ESR and ESL, a capacitive droop as the cap discharges before the inductor current ramps up, and the recovery overshoot/undershoot as the loop settles. I'd estimate the worst-case load step (the burst amplitude and duration), the converter's control-loop bandwidth, and the inductor value, then calculate the minimum capacitance needed to hold the droop within tolerance. For a burst that lasts several milliseconds, the cap may have to supply the entire burst charge, so the calculation becomes C ≥ I_burst × t_burst / ΔV_allowed — that often dominates over the loop-bandwidth-driven value.

For stability, the output capacitor's ESR and capacitance set the location of the output pole and the ESR zero, which interact with the compensation network. A very low-ESR ceramic cap can push the ESR zero to a high frequency and leave the loop with insufficient phase margin, especially with a voltage-mode control scheme. I'd check the modulator's transfer function with the chosen cap and confirm the crossover frequency and phase margin with the actual compensation values, not just the datasheet's typical application circuit.

Practical selection: I'd pick a ceramic capacitor (or a bank of them) with low ESR and ESL for the high-frequency transient, and add a bulk electrolytic or polymer cap if the burst energy is large. I'd derate for DC bias — ceramic capacitance drops significantly at rated voltage, so the effective capacitance at the operating rail may be far below the nominal value — and for temperature. I'd also check the capacitor's ripple current rating against the inductor ripple plus the burst current. Finally, I'd verify on the bench with a load-step test at the worst-case burst profile and confirm both the transient deviation and the loop's phase margin (via a bode plot or a step-response ringing check).

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether the burst should be handled by the output capacitor alone or by adding a small local bulk capacitor at the load?
- What would you check on the bench to confirm the capacitor's effective capacitance at the operating voltage is what you assumed in the design?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision comes down to how much of the ADC's error budget the reference is allowed to consume, and whether a filtered supply rail can meet that budget over the full operating range.

A dedicated reference IC gives you a specified initial accuracy, a specified temperature coefficient, and a specified long-term drift — all of which are guaranteed by the datasheet over the operating range. A filtered supply rail (say, an LDO output with an RC or LC filter) inherits the regulator's accuracy and drift, which are typically far worse: an LDO might be ±1–2% initial and have a temperature coefficient in the tens to hundreds of ppm/°C, whereas a good reference is ±0.05–0.1% initial and a few ppm/°C. For a 16-bit ADC, 1 LSB is about 15 ppm of full scale, so a reference with 100 ppm/°C drift will blow the error budget across a 0–50°C range unless the design calibrates it out.

The datasheet parameters that actually drive the decision: initial accuracy (in % or ppm), temperature coefficient (ppm/°C, and whether it's specified as a box or a slope), long-term drift (ppm/√1000h), output noise (µV RMS over the relevant bandwidth — this matters because reference noise appears directly in the ADC's conversion result), and PSRR of the reference (how much supply ripple couples through). For a filtered rail, the equivalent parameters are the regulator's line and load regulation, its output noise, and the filter's attenuation at the frequencies that matter.

There's also a practical consideration: a reference IC needs headroom — its dropout voltage plus the ADC's reference input current — so the supply rail must be high enough above the reference voltage. And the reference's output must be stable with the ADC's switched-capacitor reference input, which draws transient current at the conversion rate; that often requires a bypass capacitor and sometimes a buffer.

My approach: I'd start from the ADC's error budget, allocate a fraction (say, a third) to the reference, and see whether a filtered rail can meet it across temperature and tolerance. If it can, the rail is cheaper and simpler. If not — which is usually the case for 16-bit or higher — I'd use a reference IC and specify its noise and drift tightly. I'd also check whether the ADC has an internal reference and whether its specifications are guaranteed over the operating range, since internal references are often marginal at high resolution.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you quantify the reference's noise contribution to the ADC's overall noise floor, and what would you compare it against?
- What would you check on the bench to confirm the reference is actually meeting its datasheet drift over temperature?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the current and gate voltage are both as expected but the device is hot, the power dissipation is higher than the design assumed, or the thermal path is worse than assumed. I'd separate those two possibilities first.

On the dissipation side: conduction loss is I² × R_DS(on), and R_DS(on) rises with junction temperature — a MOSFET specified at 25°C might have two to three times that resistance at 100°C junction. So I'd check whether the datasheet R_DS(on) I used was the 25°C value rather than the hot value, and whether the actual junction temperature is consistent with the measured case temperature and the thermal resistance. I'd also check whether the MOSFET is fully enhanced: a gate voltage that "looks correct" on a multimeter may be lower than expected at the gate-source terminals under switching, or the gate drive may be insufficient to reach the low-R_DS(on) region — check V_GS(th) and the R_DS(on) vs V_GS curve, not just the nominal drive voltage. If the load is switched rather than continuously on, switching losses (gate charge, C_oss, crossover) may dominate, and those depend on switching frequency and gate resistor.

On the thermal side: I'd verify the actual thermal resistance from junction to ambient — the package's θ_JA, the copper area under the drain pad, the number of thermal vias, and whether the board is in still air or an enclosure. A part that's fine on an open bench can overheat inside an enclosure. I'd also check for solder voids under the thermal pad, which raise the junction-to-case resistance significantly.

Debugging sequence: measure V_DS(on) directly across the device while it's on and carrying the 2A — that gives the actual conduction loss without relying on the datasheet. Measure the case temperature with a thermocouple or thermal camera, and back-calculate the junction temperature from the package's θ_JC and the measured power. Compare against the design's thermal budget. If the junction temperature is within the part's rating but the case is hot, the issue is thermal path; if the junction is over rating, the issue is dissipation. Then check gate drive with a scope at the gate-source pins (not the driver output), look for ringing or slow edges, and confirm the part is the intended one and not a substitute with higher R_DS(on).

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish conduction loss from switching loss if the load is PWM-driven rather than continuously on?
- What layout changes would you make to improve the thermal path if the junction temperature is marginal?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a ferrite bead for a power rail that supplies both a sensitive analog sensor drawing a small steady current and a digital microcontroller drawing larger current with fast transient spikes?

**Answer:** The ferrite bead's job here is to isolate the digital transient currents from the analog supply without dropping too much DC voltage or resonating with the capacitors around it. The key is to treat it as a frequency-dependent impedance, not a resistor, and to check both its impedance curve and its DC resistance.

First, the DC requirement: the bead carries the total DC current of both loads, and its DCR causes a DC drop and dissipation. I'd pick a bead whose rated current comfortably exceeds the total load, with a DCR low enough that the drop is a small fraction of the rail tolerance. The analog sensor's supply is sensitive to DC drop, so this matters.

Second, the impedance: a ferrite bead's impedance rises with frequency and peaks somewhere in the tens to hundreds of MHz, then falls. I'd look at the impedance at the frequencies where the digital load's transients actually have energy — the microcontroller's clock and its harmonics, plus the switching edges. A bead with high impedance at those frequencies attenuates the transient current flowing toward the analog side. But the bead's impedance at low frequency is low, so it doesn't help with low-frequency load variation; that's the regulator's job.

Third, the resonance: the bead's inductance resonates with the decoupling capacitors on either side. If the analog-side capacitor and the bead form a high-Q resonant tank, a transient can excite ringing that's worse than no bead at all. I'd check the bead's inductance (from its impedance curve at low frequency) against the capacitance on the analog side, calculate the resonant frequency, and add damping — a small series resistor or a lossy capacitor — if the Q is high. Some beads are specifically designed with lossy characteristics to avoid this.

Fourth, placement: the bead goes between the digital and analog sections, with the analog decoupling capacitors on the analog side of the bead, so the bead and the analog caps form the filter. If the analog caps are on the digital side, the bead does nothing for the analog load.

I'd verify on the bench by measuring the analog rail's noise with the digital load active versus idle, and by injecting a transient and looking at the analog-side response. If the bead resonates, I'd see ringing at the resonant frequency; if it's too resistive, I'd see excessive DC drop.

- How would you decide between a ferrite bead and an LC filter for this isolation, and what would drive the choice?
- What would you check on the bench to confirm the bead isn't resonating with the decoupling capacitors?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a technical trade-off to be resolved with data, not a positional argument, and I'd want to understand the manufacturing engineer's cost concern precisely before defending my choice.

First, I'd ask what the actual cost delta is and where it comes from — the finish itself, the process steps, the yield impact, or the supplier's pricing. Sometimes the cost difference is smaller than assumed, or it's concentrated in a step that can be optimized. I'd also ask whether the cheaper finish has been used successfully on similar boards in the organization, because that's useful evidence either way.

Then I'd lay out the technical requirements that drove my choice. For a high-density BGA, the finish affects solder joint reliability: the pad must be flat and uniform so the paste deposits and reflows consistently, and the finish must be compatible with the alloy and the reflow profile. For a high-resolution ADC, the concern is surface uniformity and, in some cases, the finish's effect on high-frequency loss and on the reliability of fine-pitch joints. I'd bring the specific requirements — BGA pitch, pad size, alloy, reflow profile, and any reliability or qualification standards the board must meet — and show how the cheaper finish performs against them, using the supplier's data and any prior qualification evidence.

If the cheaper finish can meet the requirements with a process change — a different paste, a modified reflow profile, additional inspection — I'd be open to it, because the goal is a reliable board at reasonable cost, not a particular finish. If it can't, I'd explain the specific failure mode it risks (voiding under the BGA, inconsistent joints, reliability under thermal cycling) and propose a path forward: a qualification build with the cheaper finish, or a compromise such as using the cheaper finish on the non-critical areas if the process allows selective finishing.

The key is to keep the discussion on requirements and evidence, involve quality and reliability if the decision affects qualification, and make sure the final choice is documented with the rationale so it's defensible in the design history file. I'd also want the manufacturing engineer to feel heard — their cost concern is legitimate, and the outcome should reflect a real evaluation, not a veto.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the cheaper finish passes a qualification build but the reliability engineer is still concerned about long-term field performance, how would you resolve that?
- How would you document the decision so it's defensible in a design review or audit?
