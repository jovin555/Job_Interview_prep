# hardware-design — Day 54

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a 1A motor current bidirectionally, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start by choosing the sense element value from a power-versus-signal trade-off. A larger shunt gives more signal for a given current, improving SNR and reducing the relative impact of the amplifier's offset, but it dissipates more heat and drops more voltage. For a 1A bidirectional load, a shunt in the low-milliohm range keeps dissipation modest while still producing a usable differential signal; the exact value should be picked so the full-scale drop is large enough that the amplifier's input offset and gain error stay inside the ±2% budget, but small enough that the voltage burden on the motor supply is negligible. Then choose the topology: a high-side sense keeps the ground reference intact and detects faults to ground, but requires an amplifier with high common-mode input range and good CMRR; a low-side sense is simpler and cheaper but breaks the ground path and can't detect a short to ground. For bidirectional current, the amplifier must have a reference pin so the output can be biased mid-rail and swing both ways. Finally, budget the error terms: shunt tolerance and its temperature coefficient, amplifier offset voltage and drift, gain error, and CMRR error from common-mode variation. A precision, low-drift shunt plus a zero-drift current-sense amplifier is usually the combination that holds ±2% across temperature without calibration. Verify on the bench by sweeping current in both directions at temperature extremes and comparing against a calibrated reference meter.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you lay out the shunt and amplifier to preserve CMRR and reject switching noise from the motor? What would change if the motor supply were 48V instead of a low-voltage rail?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting and placing a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?
**Answer:** The first decision is the resonator type and its specification. A standard quartz crystal has a temperature characteristic defined by its cut; an AT-cut crystal gives a predictable parabolic frequency-versus-temperature curve, while a tuning-fork crystal is used for low-frequency real-time clocks but has a different, larger curvature. If the accuracy requirement over temperature is tight, a plain crystal may not be enough and a TCXO or an oven-controlled part may be needed — the decision should come from the error budget, not habit. Next, match the crystal's specified load capacitance to the actual load the oscillator circuit presents: the two external load caps in series with the stray capacitance must equal the crystal's rated load capacitance, or the frequency will be pulled off nominal. Then check the drive level: too much drive ages or damages the crystal, too little prevents reliable startup. On the bench, verify startup margin across the full temperature range and at the supply voltage extremes, not just at room temperature — measure the negative resistance or use the manufacturer's recommended startup test. Confirm the frequency against a reference at hot and cold corners, and check that the oscillator isn't being disturbed by nearby switching supplies or by the load the MCU presents. If jitter matters for the application, measure phase noise or period jitter with a suitable instrument.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure oscillator negative resistance without specialized equipment? What layout practices reduce stray capacitance and coupling into the oscillator pins?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?
**Answer:** Treat it as a temperature-dependent parameter shift somewhere in the loop or the power path, and separate the possibilities systematically. First, measure the actual ripple and the switching waveform at the hot condition, not just the output voltage — increased ripple with a stable load often points to a change in the inductor, the output capacitor, or the compensation. Electrolytic capacitors lose capacitance and gain ESR as they cool or heat depending on chemistry, and ceramic capacitors lose effective capacitance with temperature and DC bias, so the output filter may be behaving differently than at room temperature. Check the inductor's saturation current against the peak current at the hot corner: saturation current falls with temperature in many ferrites, so an inductor that was fine cold may be entering saturation hot, which raises ripple and can cause the loop to misbehave. Then look at the controller: many regulators have internal references, error amplifiers, and current-limit thresholds with temperature coefficients, and the current-limit threshold in particular often drops with temperature, which could explain the dropout if the load is near the limit. Also verify the feedback divider's temperature coefficient and the compensation network's component drift. The practical approach is to instrument the board, run it in a thermal chamber or with a heat gun while monitoring the switch node, inductor current, and output, and compare the waveforms at the two temperatures to localize which element changed. Once localized, either replace the marginal component with one specified over the full temperature range or adjust the design margin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish inductor saturation from a compensation problem using only the switch-node waveform? What datasheet parameters would you re-check first?

## Q4: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?
**Answer:** The core difference is that a comparator is designed to be operated open-loop and to saturate cleanly, while an op-amp is designed for closed-loop linear operation and is only incidentally usable as a comparator. For a protection threshold, the comparator is usually the right choice because its output is a defined logic level, its propagation delay is specified, and it often includes hysteresis or is easy to add hysteresis around. An op-amp used as a comparator can be slow to come out of saturation, may have phase inversion or latch-up behavior when the inputs are overdriven, and its output swing may not be compatible with the logic it drives. The decision drivers are: required response time (comparator propagation delay versus op-amp slew and recovery), input offset and drift (both have these, but comparators are specified for threshold accuracy), output interface (push-pull, open-drain, logic level), and whether hysteresis is needed to prevent chatter near the threshold. For a safety-related protection function, the comparator's defined behavior under overdrive and its specified delay are important because the protection must act predictably. If the threshold must be adjustable or the signal must also be amplified, an op-amp stage followed by a comparator is a common combination. In all cases, add hysteresis deliberately and verify the threshold over temperature, since the reference and the input divider both drift.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you add hysteresis to a comparator without compromising threshold accuracy? What failure modes would you check for in a comparator-based protection circuit?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the mechanical engineer proposes moving a temperature sensor from the PCB to a location closer to the patient-contact surface, arguing it will give a more accurate reading of the actual patient temperature. You're concerned that the new location is electrically noisy and mechanically difficult to route, and that the sensor's own self-heating and the thermal mass of the new location may actually degrade accuracy. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** Start by separating the two questions being conflated: where the sensor should physically be to measure what we care about, and what it takes to get a clean, accurate signal from that location. The mechanical engineer's underlying goal — measuring closer to the patient — is legitimate and worth taking seriously, so the discussion should be framed around the measurement requirement rather than around whose proposal wins. Ask for the thermal model or the reasoning behind the claim that the new location is more accurate: what is the thermal path from the patient to the sensor in each case, what is the time constant, and how much self-heating does the sensor produce at its operating current? Those are answerable questions, and the answers may support the move, may show it doesn't help, or may show it helps only if the sensor is driven at lower current or with a different excitation scheme. On the electrical side, quantify the noise concern rather than asserting it: what is the noise environment at the new location, and can it be mitigated with shielding, a twisted pair, a differential measurement, or a remote sensor with a digital interface? On the mechanical side, ask whether the routing and mounting concerns can be resolved with a small connector or a flex cable. The goal is to converge on a design that meets the measurement requirement, possibly by moving the sensor but adding mitigation, or by keeping it on the PCB but improving the thermal coupling. If the disagreement persists, propose a bench or prototype experiment that measures both options against a reference, and let the data decide. Document the decision and the rationale in the design history file, since this is exactly the kind of trade-off a reviewer will ask about later.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the experiment to compare the two sensor locations fairly? If the data is ambiguous, how would you decide?
