# hardware-design — Day 120

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the output capacitor for a buck converter that must supply a load with periodic high-current bursts, where the output voltage must stay within a tight tolerance during the transient and the capacitor also has to satisfy the loop stability requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as two separate but coupled requirements — transient droop and loop stability — and size the capacitor to satisfy both, then verify the choice against the real load profile.

For the transient requirement, the output capacitor has to supply the load step current while the inductor current ramps up. The relevant parameters are the capacitance value, the ESR, and the ESL. The initial voltage droop is dominated by the ESL and ESR — the capacitor's equivalent series inductance and resistance — so a low-ESR ceramic in a small package close to the load is usually better than a large electrolytic, even if the electrolytic has more bulk capacitance. The bulk droop after the initial spike is set by the capacitance and the inductor's slew rate: ΔV ≈ (ΔI × Δt) / C, where Δt is roughly the time for the inductor current to catch up. I'd calculate the worst-case load step, estimate the inductor's response time, and pick a capacitance that keeps the droop within the tolerance with margin.

For loop stability, the output capacitor is part of the converter's compensation network. The ESR zero and the LC double pole set the loop's phase margin, and too little capacitance or too much ESR can push the crossover frequency or phase margin out of spec. I'd check the converter's datasheet stability guidance — many parts specify a minimum output capacitance and a range of ESR — and simulate or bench-test the loop with a load-step response and a bode plot if possible.

The practical approach is to start with the datasheet's recommended output capacitance, then add margin for the transient requirement, then verify on the bench with a real load step (a MOSFET switched with a function generator is a common way to emulate the burst) and check both the droop and the recovery. I'd also check the capacitor's DC bias derating — a ceramic's effective capacitance drops significantly at its rated voltage, so the nominal value is not what you get at the operating point. And I'd check the capacitor's ripple current rating against the converter's ripple current, since a capacitor that's undersized for ripple current will heat up and degrade.

- What would you check on the bench to confirm the capacitor is actually doing its job during the load burst?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision comes down to how much of the ADC's error budget the reference is allowed to consume, and whether a filtered supply rail can meet that budget across the operating conditions.

A dedicated voltage reference IC gives you a specified initial accuracy, a specified temperature coefficient, and a specified long-term drift — all of which are guaranteed by the datasheet over the operating range. A filtered supply rail, by contrast, inherits the supply's accuracy and drift, plus whatever noise the filter doesn't remove. For a high-resolution ADC — say 16 bits or more — the reference's contribution to the overall error is often the dominant term, because the ADC's resolution is only as good as the reference it's compared against. A 1 LSB error at 16 bits with a 2.5V reference is about 38 µV, so the reference's initial accuracy and drift have to be well inside that.

The datasheet parameters that actually drive the decision are: initial accuracy (in % or mV), temperature coefficient (ppm/°C), long-term drift (ppm/1000 hours), output noise (µV RMS over the relevant bandwidth), and line/load regulation. For a filtered supply rail, you'd also need to know the supply's own accuracy and drift, and the filter's attenuation at the frequencies that matter — which is usually where the filtered-rail approach falls short, because a passive filter's attenuation at low frequency is limited and the supply's drift is not removed at all.

The other consideration is the reference's output impedance and its ability to drive the ADC's reference input, which draws a switched current during conversion. A reference with a slow settling time or high output impedance can cause gain errors that vary with the ADC's conversion rate. That's why many high-resolution ADCs specify a reference bypass capacitor and a maximum source impedance.

In practice, for a high-resolution medical-grade ADC I'd default to a dedicated reference IC unless the accuracy requirement is loose enough that a filtered rail is demonstrably sufficient — and I'd prove that with a noise and drift budget, not assume it. For lower-resolution ADCs or less critical measurements, the filtered rail can be a reasonable cost and board-area saving.

- How would you verify the reference's noise contribution on the bench without a dedicated low-noise measurement setup?
- What would you check if the ADC's readings drift over temperature even though the reference's datasheet drift is within spec?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the gate voltage and load current both look right but the MOSFET is hot, the dissipation is higher than the simple I²R × RDS(on) calculation predicts, so I'd work through the possible causes systematically.

First, I'd confirm the actual RDS(on) at the operating conditions. RDS(on) rises with junction temperature — often by 50% or more from 25°C to 125°C — so a part that looks fine at room temperature can dissipate significantly more when hot. I'd check the datasheet's RDS(on) vs. temperature curve and estimate the junction temperature from the case temperature and thermal resistance. If the calculated dissipation at the actual junction temperature matches the observed heating, the part may simply be undersized for the application.

Second, I'd check whether the MOSFET is fully enhanced. A gate voltage that "looks correct" on a multimeter may not be correct dynamically — the gate might be driven through a high-impedance path that charges slowly, or the gate drive might not be referenced correctly, leaving the MOSFET in the linear region during part of the switching cycle. I'd look at the gate voltage with an oscilloscope, not a multimeter, and check the rise and fall times and whether the gate is ringing or drooping.

Third, I'd check for switching losses. If the load is switched at any meaningful frequency, the transition through the linear region dissipates energy on every edge, and that loss can dominate at higher frequencies. I'd measure the drain-source voltage and drain current simultaneously during the switching transition and estimate the switching loss.

Fourth, I'd check for shoot-through or body-diode conduction — if there's a synchronous rectifier or a capacitive load, the body diode may conduct during part of the cycle and dissipate more than expected.

Finally, I'd check the thermal path. A MOSFET that's dissipating the expected power but has a poor thermal path — insufficient copper, no thermal vias, a small package — will run hot even at correct dissipation. I'd compare the case temperature to the expected junction temperature and check whether the board is actually sinking the heat.

The fix depends on which cause dominates: a lower-RDS(on) part, a better gate driver, a snubber or slower switching edge, or more copper and thermal vias.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish between conduction loss and switching loss on the bench?
- What would you check if the MOSFET runs hot only at certain load currents but not others?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?

**Answer:** The crystal selection starts with the accuracy requirement over temperature, because that's usually the binding constraint. A standard crystal has a frequency tolerance at 25°C and a temperature coefficient that describes how the frequency drifts across the operating range — often specified as a parabolic curve with a turnover point near 25°C. For a wide temperature range, I'd compare the crystal's total accuracy (initial tolerance plus temperature drift plus aging) against the system's timing budget, and if a standard crystal can't meet it, I'd consider a TCXO or an oven-controlled part, or a crystal with a tighter temperature specification.

Beyond accuracy, I'd check the crystal's load capacitance specification and match it to the oscillator circuit's load capacitance — the two external capacitors plus stray capacitance. A mismatch pulls the frequency off nominal and can also affect startup margin. I'd also check the crystal's drive level requirement against the oscillator's drive capability, because overdriving a crystal can damage it or cause frequency shifts, and underdriving it can cause startup failures.

The ESR of the crystal matters for startup margin, especially at cold temperatures where the oscillator's transconductance drops and the crystal's ESR rises. I'd check the datasheet's maximum ESR and compare it to the oscillator's specified maximum, and I'd verify startup margin on the bench across the full temperature range — not just at room temperature.

On the bench, I'd verify: startup time and reliability at the temperature extremes (cold start is usually the worst case), frequency accuracy at several temperatures against a reference, and the drive level (measured with a current probe or inferred from the oscillator's specifications). I'd also check for any spurious oscillation or harmonic content, and confirm the crystal doesn't exhibit any mode-hopping or jump behavior near the turnover temperature.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you measure the crystal's drive level on the bench without a dedicated current probe?
- What would you do if the crystal starts reliably at room temperature but fails to start at cold temperature?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a design-for-manufacturability discussion where both cost and reliability matter, and I'd want to resolve it with data rather than opinion.

First, I'd make sure I understand the manufacturing engineer's concern precisely — is it the finish cost itself, the lead time, the supplier availability, or something else? Sometimes the objection is really about a downstream process constraint, and understanding that changes the conversation.

Then I'd lay out the technical requirements that drove my choice. For a high-density BGA, the finish has to provide a flat, uniform surface for consistent solder joint formation — a finish with uneven thickness or poor coplanarity can cause voids, non-wet opens, or head-in-pillow defects that are hard to detect and expensive to rework. For a high-resolution ADC, the concern is more about surface finish consistency affecting the solder joint's electrical performance and long-term reliability, particularly under thermal cycling. I'd bring the specific requirements — the BGA pitch, the ADC's package, the reliability testing the board has to pass — and explain how the finish choice connects to them.

I'd also ask the manufacturing engineer to quantify the cost difference and to propose a specific alternative, then evaluate that alternative against the same requirements. If the cheaper finish can meet the requirements with evidence — a qualification report, a reliability test result, a supplier's process capability data — then it's worth considering. If it can't, the cost saving isn't real, because the risk of field failures or yield loss outweighs it.

If we can't agree on the technical merits, I'd escalate to a joint decision with the quality and reliability functions involved, and I'd propose a qualification test — build a small batch with the alternative finish and run the relevant reliability tests — so the decision is based on evidence rather than assertion. I'd also document the decision and its rationale in the design history file, since this is a medical device and the choice has to be traceable.

The key is to stay collaborative: the manufacturing engineer is raising a legitimate cost concern, and the right outcome is a finish that meets the reliability requirements at the lowest total cost — not a win for either side.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What reliability tests would you propose to qualify the alternative finish?
- How would you handle the situation if the cheaper finish passed the qualification tests but you still had concerns about long-term field reliability?
