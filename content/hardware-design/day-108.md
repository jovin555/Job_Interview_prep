# hardware-design — Day 108

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the sense resistor value and amplifier gain split for a high-side current-sense path in a battery-powered medical device, where the full-scale current is 3A, the ADC reference is 3.3V, and the measurement must resolve small currents in standby without burning excessive power in the shunt?

**Answer:** The core tension is that a larger shunt gives you more signal at low currents (better resolution against the amplifier's input-referred noise and offset), but dissipates more power at full scale and eats headroom. I'd start by defining the two ends of the measurement range: the full-scale current sets the maximum shunt drop I can tolerate, and the smallest current I need to resolve sets the minimum signal the amplifier-plus-ADC chain must handle with acceptable SNR.

A practical approach: pick the shunt so that the full-scale drop is a small fraction of the available headroom — typically 50–100 mV at full scale for a high-side monitor, which keeps I²R loss modest (at 3A and 50 mV that's 150 mW, so a suitably rated resistor). Then choose the amplifier gain so the full-scale output lands just under the ADC reference, leaving a little margin for tolerance and offset. The gain split between shunt and amplifier is really a noise-and-power trade: pushing more of the "gain" into the shunt costs power, while pushing it into the amplifier costs input-referred noise and offset error.

I'd also check the amplifier's input common-mode range — a high-side sense amp must handle the full battery rail plus the shunt drop, and its CMRR over that range matters more than its headline offset. For standby resolution, I'd compare the smallest current's shunt drop against the amplifier's input offset voltage and noise density integrated over the measurement bandwidth; if the signal is buried, no amount of gain helps. Finally, I'd verify the shunt's temperature coefficient and power rating, and consider a Kelvin (four-wire) connection to keep the sense points out of the high-current path.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a high-side and a low-side sense topology here, given the device is battery-powered? What would you do if the amplifier's offset drift over temperature dominated the standby error budget?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting the switching frequency for a buck converter that must supply a digital rail and a downstream LDO feeding a sensitive analog section, where the converter sits on the same board as the analog front-end?

**Answer:** Switching frequency is a multi-way trade, and when a sensitive analog section shares the board I weight EMI and ripple spectral placement heavily, not just efficiency and size.

Higher frequency shrinks the inductor and output capacitor, which helps size and transient response, and it pushes switching harmonics above the analog front-end's band of interest — but it increases switching losses, gate-drive losses, and radiated/conducted emissions that are harder to contain. Lower frequency improves efficiency and reduces switching noise energy, but needs a bigger inductor and cap, and the fundamental and its harmonics may land closer to the analog bandwidth, where the downstream LDO's PSRR is also degrading with frequency.

My reasoning order: first, identify the analog front-end's signal band and the LDO's PSRR curve versus frequency — the LDO rejects low-frequency ripple well but its rejection rolls off, so a switching fundamental that sits where PSRR is still high is easier to clean up. Second, check where the harmonics fall relative to any sensitive sampling or modulation frequencies to avoid intermodulation or aliasing. Third, confirm the chosen frequency doesn't collide with the device's own radio or other clock domains. Then I'd balance the remaining size/efficiency cost.

I'd also plan the physical mitigation regardless of frequency: tight hot-loop layout, shielded inductor, input filter, and keeping the switch node away from the analog section. The frequency choice and the layout/filtering are a package deal — a good frequency with a bad layout still fails EMC.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the downstream LDO alone is sufficient, or whether you also need an LC filter ahead of it? What bench measurements would confirm the switching harmonics aren't coupling into the analog path?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a boost converter's output is correct at room temperature but the output voltage sags and the converter becomes unstable at low temperature, even though the load is unchanged?

**Answer:** Temperature-dependent instability in a boost converter usually points to a component parameter drifting out of the compensation or current-limit design margin, so I'd work from the control loop outward.

First, I'd characterize the failure precisely: at what temperature does it start, is it a slow sag or an oscillation, what's the frequency of the oscillation, and does it correlate with load or input voltage. Capturing the switch node and the feedback node on a scope while cooling the board (freeze spray, then a chamber for repeatability) tells me whether it's loop instability (sustained oscillation) or a limit/current-limit issue (sag without oscillation).

Then I'd look at the usual temperature-sensitive suspects: the output capacitor's ESR rises and capacitance falls at cold, which shifts the loop's zero and can destabilize a compensation network tuned at room temperature; the inductor's saturation and DCR change; the feedback divider's tempco; and the IC's internal reference and error-amplifier behavior over temperature. The boost's right-half-plane zero also moves with operating point, so margin that's thin at room temp can vanish cold.

I'd verify by measuring the loop (inject a signal, or use the IC's available tools) at temperature, and by substituting a low-ESR capacitor or adjusting compensation to see if the behavior changes. The fix is usually to re-derive compensation with worst-case cold capacitor ESR and confirm margin across the full temperature range, not just to add bulk capacitance blindly.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a loop-stability problem from the converter hitting current limit at cold? What role does the boost's right-half-plane zero play in how much loop bandwidth you can safely use?

## Q4: How would you approach choosing between a linear charger and a switching charger for a Li-ion battery in a portable medical device, and what factors beyond efficiency would drive the decision?

**Answer:** Efficiency is often the least interesting axis here — the decision usually turns on thermal budget, board area, EMI, and the safety/compliance story.

A linear charger is simple, has no switching noise, needs few external components, and is easy to reason about for safety and certification. Its downside is heat: the dissipation is (input voltage − battery voltage) × charge current, and in a sealed medical enclosure that heat has nowhere to go and can also warm nearby temperature-sensitive sensors. A switching charger handles the same power with far less dissipation and tolerates a wider input-to-battery voltage difference, but brings EMI, more components, layout sensitivity, and a more complex loop to validate.

So I'd weigh: how much headroom is there between the input source and the battery (a big gap favors switching); what's the thermal budget inside the enclosure and does self-heating threaten sensor accuracy or patient-contact temperature limits; how much board area is available; and how sensitive is the rest of the system to switching noise. For a device that charges while operating, the charger's noise can couple into the analog front-end, which may push toward linear or toward a well-filtered switching design.

Beyond that, I'd consider the compliance angle: whichever topology, the charge profile, termination, and fault handling must be robust and documented, and using an integrated charger with built-in safety features can simplify the regulatory argument versus a discrete implementation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you quantify whether the linear charger's heat is acceptable inside a sealed enclosure? What would you check on the bench to confirm a switching charger isn't degrading the analog measurements?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the test engineer argues that your planned design-verification test coverage for a new analog front-end is excessive — that a handful of spot checks at room temperature would be "good enough" and that full characterization across temperature and tolerance corners is overkill given the schedule. You believe the corner coverage is necessary because the front-end's error budget is tight and the device is a Class II medical product. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a risk-communication problem rather than a "more testing vs. less testing" argument, because the test engineer's concern about schedule is legitimate and dismissing it won't build alignment.

First, I'd reframe the conversation around the error budget: show the analysis that says how much margin exists at nominal and how quickly it's consumed by component tolerance, temperature drift, and reference variation. If the budget is genuinely tight, the corner testing isn't gold-plating — it's the evidence that the design meets its requirements across the conditions the device will actually see, which is exactly what the regulatory submission and the design history file need to demonstrate. I'd make that concrete rather than asserting it.

Second, I'd look for a way to right-size the plan rather than defend it wholesale. Maybe a structured characterization on a small number of units at the key corners, combined with analysis and simulation to justify interpolating between them, gives the needed confidence with less bench time than a brute-force matrix. That respects the schedule pressure while keeping the coverage that matters.

Third, I'd escalate the risk decision transparently if we can't agree: document the residual risk of reduced coverage and let the appropriate owner — project lead, quality, regulatory — make an informed call, rather than either of us deciding unilaterally. On a Class II device, the traceability from requirement to verification evidence is part of the deliverable, so cutting corners silently isn't an option; making the trade-off visible and owned is.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide which corners are the critical ones to test versus which can be covered by analysis? If leadership overruled you and cut the coverage, how would you document and mitigate the residual risk?
