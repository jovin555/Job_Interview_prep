# hardware-design — Day 102

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the inductor for a buck converter, and what parameters would you check to make sure the design stays robust across the full load range?

**Answer:** I'd start from the operating point rather than the part number. First I'd fix the switching frequency and calculate the required inductance from the ripple-current target — typically 20–40% of the maximum load current — using the volt-second balance across the inductor during the on-time: L = (Vin − Vout) × D / (fsw × ΔIL). That gives a starting value, but the real work is checking the inductor against the corners of the operating envelope.

The parameters I'd verify: saturation current (Isat) must be comfortably above the peak current at maximum load *and* at the worst-case transient, with margin for tolerance and temperature — a saturating inductor turns into a near-short and the current limit may not catch it fast enough. RMS current rating must cover the worst-case thermal condition, not just nominal load. DCR matters for efficiency and for the I²R heating inside a sealed enclosure. Core material and its permeability versus temperature and DC bias — ferrites lose inductance under DC bias, so the datasheet's nominal value can be optimistic at full load. Self-resonant frequency should be well above the switching frequency and its first few harmonics so the part behaves inductively, not capacitively. And shielding: a shielded inductor reduces the radiated field that can couple into a nearby analog front-end.

I'd also check the inductor's tolerance and how the ripple current behaves at minimum load — if the converter enters discontinuous mode, the loop dynamics change, so the compensation has to be stable in both regions. Finally, I'd confirm the part is available in the package size the layout can accommodate and that its height fits the enclosure.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a larger inductance for lower ripple and a smaller one for faster transient response? What would you check on the bench to confirm the inductor isn't saturating under transient load steps?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a SAR ADC and a delta-sigma ADC for a medical device that measures a slowly varying physiological signal with high resolution, and what trade-offs would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The first question is what "high resolution" actually needs to mean — effective bits at the signal bandwidth, not the marketed bit count. For a slowly varying signal like temperature or pressure, the signal bandwidth is low, which plays to the delta-sigma's strengths: oversampling and noise shaping push quantization noise out of band, so you can get very high effective resolution with a simple anti-aliasing filter, and the modulator's inherent averaging helps reject the kind of low-frequency interference you see in a clinical environment.

The trade-offs I'd weigh: throughput and latency — delta-sigma converters have a settling time tied to their digital filter, so if the system multiplexes several channels or needs a fast response to a step, the SAR's single-cycle conversion and short latency are attractive. SAR is also easier to multiplex because there's no filter settling to wait through between channel changes. Power: a SAR typically wins at low sample rates if you can power it down between conversions, while a delta-sigma runs continuously. Complexity: delta-sigma needs a well-behaved reference and careful clocking, and its digital filter introduces group delay that matters if the signal is used in a control loop. SAR needs a good input driver — the sampling capacitor's charge injection means the front-end amplifier has to settle within the acquisition window, which gets harder at high source impedance.

For a slow physiological signal where I care about noise floor and linearity more than speed, I'd lean delta-sigma; if the system needs multi-channel scanning, fast response, or a compact low-power budget, I'd lean SAR. Either way I'd validate with an FFT and an ENOB measurement at the actual signal bandwidth rather than trusting the datasheet's headline number.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the effective resolution of either converter in the actual signal chain? What front-end considerations change when you move from a SAR to a delta-sigma input?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a temperature-dependent parameter shift and work from the outside in. First, confirm the observation is real and repeatable — log the input voltage, output voltage, ripple, and switching node waveform while sweeping temperature in a chamber or with a heat gun, and note the exact temperature where behavior changes. That gives me a threshold to reason about.

Then I'd look at the components whose behavior is most temperature-sensitive. The output capacitor is a prime suspect: electrolytics lose capacitance and gain ESR at low temperature, but at high temperature the ESR can rise and the capacitance can drift, changing the loop's phase margin — increased ripple and occasional instability are classic symptoms. I'd check the capacitor's datasheet curves for capacitance and ESR versus temperature and compare against what the loop was compensated for. The inductor's saturation current and DCR also shift with temperature; if the core is approaching saturation at high temperature, ripple current climbs. The feedback divider's resistors drift, which shifts the output setpoint slightly. The error amplifier's transconductance and the compensation network's components drift too. And the MOSFET's Rds(on) rises with temperature, which changes the conduction losses and can push the converter toward current limit or thermal foldback — a "dropout" that's actually protection engaging.

I'd also check for thermal effects outside the converter: a nearby heat source warming the regulator, a solder joint or connector whose resistance rises with temperature, or a thermal shutdown feature on the IC itself. The way to separate these is to measure the switching node and the feedback node at temperature and see whether the loop is losing margin (ringing, subharmonic oscillation) or whether the converter is hitting a limit (current limit, thermal shutdown). Once I know which, I can swap the suspect component for a higher-temperature-rated or better-characterized part and re-run the sweep.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish loop instability from current-limit or thermal-shutdown behavior from the waveforms alone? What would you change in the compensation network if the root cause turned out to be capacitor ESR drift?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a TVS diode for protecting a medical device's external connector that carries both power and a sensitive analog signal, and what parameters matter most?

**Answer:** The starting point is the threat, not the part. I'd define what the connector has to survive — IEC 61000-4-2 ESD levels, any surge or transient requirements from the applicable standard, and the normal operating voltage and signal swing on each pin. Then I'd choose the TVS so that its standoff voltage (Vrwm) sits above the maximum normal operating voltage with margin, so it doesn't leak or clamp during normal operation, while its breakdown and clamping voltage (Vcl) stay below the absolute maximum of the protected device — and for the analog pin, below the level that would forward-bias the input protection diodes or disturb the signal.

Parameters that matter most: standoff voltage and leakage current (critical for a high-impedance analog input — even a few microamps of leakage can create an offset), clamping voltage at the specified surge current, peak pulse power and pulse duration rating, and capacitance. Capacitance is the big one for the analog signal: a TVS with tens of picofarads on a signal line forms a low-pass filter with the source impedance and can distort or attenuate the signal, so for a sensitive analog pair I'd look for a low-capacitance TVS or a dedicated low-capacitance array, and I'd check the capacitance versus voltage curve since it's nonlinear. For the power pin, capacitance matters less but the surge rating matters more.

I'd also consider the topology: a single bidirectional TVS across a differential pair versus two unidirectional devices to a common reference, and whether the TVS should be placed before or after any series impedance. Placement matters as much as selection — the TVS has to be right at the connector with a short, low-inductance path to the return, or the lead inductance lets the transient through before the clamp responds. Finally, I'd verify the whole protection scheme on the bench with an ESD gun and confirm the analog signal's noise and bandwidth are unaffected.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a TVS and a varistor or a spark gap for a given threat level? What would you check on the bench to confirm the TVS isn't degrading the analog signal?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a shared problem to solve rather than a position to defend. First I'd make sure I understand the manufacturing engineer's actual concern — is it purely cost, or is there a yield or supply issue behind it? Then I'd lay out the technical reasons for the current finish in terms the whole review can evaluate: the BGA's fine pitch and the need for coplanarity and reliable joint formation, the ADC's requirement for a flat, uniform surface to avoid voids and inconsistent wetting, and any reliability or inspection implications. I'd bring data rather than assertion — the assembly house's process capability for the alternative finish, any historical yield or field-return data, and the actual cost delta including rework and scrap, not just the bare-board price.

Then I'd look for a path that satisfies both concerns. Options might include keeping the specified finish only on the critical boards or the critical side, qualifying the cheaper finish on a test vehicle and comparing yield and joint quality, or negotiating volume pricing. If the cheaper finish genuinely can't meet the reliability requirement, I'd want that documented — a qualification run showing the failure mode is far more persuasive than an opinion, and it protects the decision if it's ever questioned later. If the data shows the cheaper finish is actually adequate, I'd be willing to change my mind; the goal is the right answer, not my answer.

Throughout, I'd keep the conversation focused on the risk to the patient and the product rather than on whose proposal wins, and I'd make sure the decision and its rationale get recorded in the design review minutes so the team has a clear record.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure a qualification test to compare the two finishes fairly? What would you do if the manufacturing engineer's cost pressure came from a deadline rather than a technical concern?
