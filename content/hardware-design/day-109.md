# hardware-design — Day 109

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a gate driver for a high-side N-channel MOSFET used as a load switch, where the source floats at the battery voltage and the switch must turn on slowly enough to limit inrush current but turn off fast enough to protect the load?
**Answer:** The core problem is that a high-side N-channel MOSFET needs its gate driven above the source, and the source sits at the battery rail — so the driver must generate a gate-to-source voltage that stays within the device's Vgs(max) while the source moves. I'd start by deciding between a dedicated high-side gate driver with an internal charge pump or bootstrap, versus a charge-pump-based load-switch IC. For a battery-powered medical device where the rail is only a few volts, a bootstrap needs a switching node to refresh from, which a static load switch doesn't have — so a charge-pump driver or an integrated load switch is usually the cleaner choice. Then I'd size the turn-on: inrush into the load's bulk capacitance is I = C·dV/dt, so I'd add a controlled slew-rate pin or an RC network on the gate to set a dV/dt that keeps the inrush below the upstream supply's current limit and below the MOSFET's SOA for the duration of the ramp. Turn-off is the opposite concern — I want the gate discharged quickly through an active pull-down rather than a weak resistor, so a fault condition collapses the rail fast. I'd check the driver's source/sink current capability, its undervoltage lockout behavior (the switch must not turn on with a marginal gate drive, which would put the FET in its linear region and cook it), and the charge pump's startup behavior. Finally I'd verify the whole thing on the bench with a current probe on the input during plug-in, and a thermal check on the FET during the ramp.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would the SOA calculation change if the load capacitance were much larger and the ramp had to be stretched to hundreds of milliseconds? What happens to the gate drive if the battery voltage sags during the inrush?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a 16-bit ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?
**Answer:** The first question is what the ADC is actually being asked to resolve. At 16 bits with a 2.5V reference, one LSB is about 38 µV, so the reference has to be stable to well under that over the operating range — which almost always rules out a filtered supply rail for a precision measurement. A filtered rail inherits the regulator's initial accuracy, its load and line regulation, and its drift, and those errors land directly on the conversion result as a gain error. A dedicated reference gives me specified initial accuracy, a temperature coefficient in ppm/°C, and — critically — a specified long-term drift, which matters for a device that gets calibrated once at manufacturing and then has to hold accuracy for years. The parameters I'd actually pull from the datasheet are: initial accuracy, tempco (and whether it's box method or the more optimistic "typical" curve), long-term drift, output noise (both low-frequency 0.1–10 Hz and wideband, since reference noise appears directly in the conversion), and the output impedance / load regulation, because the ADC's reference input draws a switched charge during conversion and the reference has to recover from that without drooping. I'd also look at the reference's dropout headroom against the supply, and whether it needs an output capacitor for stability — some references are fussy about ESR. If the ADC has a built-in reference buffer, that changes the load-regulation requirement. The trade-off against a filtered rail is cost, board area, and power; a filtered rail can be adequate for a ratiometric measurement where the same rail feeds both the sensor excitation and the reference, because the errors cancel — but that's a specific architecture, not a general substitute.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify on the bench that the reference, not the ADC, is limiting your effective resolution? What would you do if the reference's specified tempco were marginal but its typical curve looked fine?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?
**Answer:** Audible noise from an inductor means the converter is generating mechanical excitation in the audio band — roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz — and at light load that almost always points to the converter entering a pulse-skipping or burst mode, where the switching frequency drops into the audible range and the inductor sees current pulses at that rate. So the first thing I'd do is confirm the operating mode: scope the switch node and the inductor current at light load and see whether the converter is in continuous conduction, discontinuous conduction, or burst mode, and measure the repetition rate of the bursts. If it's burst mode, the fix is usually architectural rather than a component swap — either force the converter into forced-PWM (continuous conduction) at light load, accept the efficiency penalty, or choose a converter whose light-load mode operates above the audio band. If the converter is genuinely switching in the audio band, I'd look at whether the inductor is the culprit or whether it's the ceramic output capacitors singing (they can, via piezoelectric effect) — a quick way to tell is to probe with a stethoscope or press gently on components. If it's the inductor, I'd check whether the part is a shielded, potted type versus an open drum, and whether the core is saturating or the winding is loose. I'd also check for subharmonic oscillation or instability that's modulating the switching frequency, since that can produce an audio-band envelope even when the nominal switching frequency is well above 20 kHz. The key point is that "within specification" for voltage and ripple doesn't mean the converter is behaving well — audible noise is a symptom of a mode of operation that may also have poor transient response or EMI consequences, so I'd want to understand the mechanism before deciding whether to change the converter, the inductor, or the operating mode.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you check if the noise appeared only at a specific load current and disappeared at both lighter and heavier loads? How would you decide whether the efficiency penalty of forced-PWM is acceptable in a battery-powered device?

## Q4: How would you approach characterizing the power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR) of an analog front-end, and how would you use that measurement to decide whether the supply noise is acceptable?
**Answer:** PSRR is the ratio of a disturbance injected on the supply to the resulting change at the output, usually expressed in dB, and it's frequency-dependent — it typically degrades as frequency rises because the feedback loop of the amplifier or regulator runs out of gain. To characterize it, I'd inject a known AC signal onto the supply rail — either with a network analyzer driving the rail through a summing transformer or injection resistor, or with a signal generator and a series injection capacitor if the rail can tolerate it — and measure the output with the input held at a known condition (usually shorted to the reference or to mid-supply, so I'm measuring only the supply-to-output path). I'd sweep from DC or a few Hz up to at least the switching frequency of any converter on the board and its first few harmonics, because that's where the real disturbance lives. The measurement has to be done carefully: the injection network must not load the rail or change the regulator's stability, and the probe ground has to be short to avoid picking up radiated noise. Once I have the curve, I'd combine it with the actual measured supply ripple — both the low-frequency content and the switching-frequency content — to estimate the output-referred noise contribution, and compare that against the front-end's noise budget. If the supply contributes a meaningful fraction of the total, I'd look at adding a filter, improving the regulator's PSRR, or moving the disturbance away from a band the front-end cares about. The important discipline is to do this as a budget: PSRR alone doesn't tell you whether the supply is acceptable — it's PSRR times the actual supply noise, compared against the signal chain's own noise floor.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure PSRR at the switching frequency if your network analyzer can't inject at that frequency? What would you do if the PSRR were fine at low frequency but degraded badly right at the converter's switching frequency?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** I'd start by separating the factual question from the judgment question. The factual question is what the actual mating-cycle exposure is: is the cable truly connect-once, or does the use case include field replacement, cleaning/reprocessing, or service events that involve disconnecting? I'd want to look at the use environment and the service model rather than rely on my assumption — if the device goes out to patients and the cable is a wear item, the reliability engineer may well be right, and my "connected once" premise may only hold for the first installation. The judgment question is how much margin is appropriate for a Class II device, where a connector failure in the field is a complaint, a service action, and potentially a safety issue. I'd bring the discussion back to the risk file: what's the severity of a connector failure, what's the likelihood given the real mating-cycle count, and what does the reliability engineer's number actually assume? I'd ask for the data behind their concern — field history on similar products, the connector's specified cycle rating with its derating, and whether the proposed alternative's cost premium is justified by the risk reduction. If the data supports their position, I'd change the part; if it supports mine, I'd document the rationale and the assumed use case in the design record so the decision is traceable. Either way I'd want the disagreement resolved on evidence rather than on whose assumption is louder, and I'd want the outcome captured so it doesn't get relitigated. The one thing I wouldn't do is dismiss the concern because the current part is cheaper — in a medical device, the cost of a field failure dominates the connector BOM delta.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the risk-file entry if you decided to keep the lower-cycle connector? What would you do if the reliability engineer's concern were valid but the higher-cycle connector didn't fit the mechanical envelope?
