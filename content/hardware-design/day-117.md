# hardware-design — Day 117

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a load switch versus a discrete MOSFET-plus-gate-driver for power-gating a peripheral rail in a battery-powered medical device, and what factors would drive the decision?

**Answer:** I'd start by framing the actual requirements rather than jumping to a part: rail voltage and current profile (steady and inrush), acceptable voltage drop, quiescent current budget when the rail is off, required turn-on slew rate, and whether any fault behavior (current limit, reverse-current blocking, thermal shutdown) is needed. Those requirements usually make the decision for you.

An integrated load switch is attractive when the current is modest and you want a small, well-characterized solution: it bundles the pass FET, gate drive, and often current limiting, thermal protection, and a controlled soft-start in one package with a datasheet you can point to in a risk file. That reduces part count, layout area, and the number of things that can go wrong in bring-up, and the built-in protections are hard to replicate discretely without real design effort. The trade-offs are cost at higher currents, limited flexibility in threshold and slew rate, and the fact that you inherit the vendor's behavior — including its quiescent current and its fault response — which you need to verify rather than assume.

A discrete MOSFET plus gate driver makes sense when the current is high enough that an integrated switch becomes expensive or thermally awkward, when you need a specific turn-on profile, or when you want the pass element and its thermal path under your own control. The cost is that you now own the gate drive design: you need enough gate voltage to keep the FET in low-RDS(on) operation across the whole battery range, you need to manage inrush deliberately (a controlled gate ramp or a dedicated inrush limiter), and you need to think about what happens on brownout, reverse current, and fault conditions — none of which come for free.

For a battery-powered medical device specifically, I'd weight the off-state quiescent current heavily, because a load switch that leaks even tens of microamps when "off" can dominate a sleep budget over days of operation. I'd also check reverse-current behavior when the peripheral rail is back-driven, and confirm the turn-on slew rate keeps inrush within the battery's and connector's limits. Then I'd verify the chosen approach on the bench across the battery voltage range and temperature, not just at nominal.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you measure the off-state leakage of a load switch in a way that isolates it from the rest of the board's sleep current?
- If the peripheral draws a large capacitive inrush, how would you decide between slowing the gate ramp and adding a dedicated inrush-limiting element?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting the switching frequency for a buck converter that must supply a sensitive analog rail, where the output ripple must stay below a few millivolts and the converter sits close to the analog front-end on the same board?

**Answer:** The switching frequency is a multi-way trade, and for a noise-sensitive analog rail I'd treat it as a system decision rather than a converter-only one.

Higher frequency shrinks the inductor and output capacitor for a given ripple, which helps board area and transient response, and it pushes the switching fundamental above the analog signal band so a modest post-filter can attenuate it. But higher frequency also raises switching losses, can reduce efficiency, and — critically — moves the ripple and its harmonics to frequencies where they may couple more efficiently into the analog front-end through parasitics, ground bounce, or radiated coupling from the switching loop. Lower frequency gives better efficiency and lower switching loss but needs a larger inductor and more output capacitance, and puts the fundamental closer to the band of interest, which makes filtering harder.

So my approach: first establish the analog front-end's band of interest and its PSRR/CMRR versus frequency, so I know which switching frequencies actually threaten the measurement. Then pick a frequency that puts the fundamental and its low harmonics clear of that band, ideally with margin, and confirm the converter's control loop is stable and the ripple at that frequency is achievable with a reasonable LC. I'd also consider whether the converter can be synchronized to an external clock so the switching noise lands at a known, filterable frequency rather than drifting, and whether spread-spectrum is acceptable — for a precision measurement, a fixed, known frequency is often easier to filter than a smeared one.

Layout matters as much as the number: keep the hot loop (input cap, switch, inductor, catch diode or low-side FET) tight and away from the analog section, and use a downstream LDO or an LC/ferrite filter to knock down the residual ripple. I'd verify on the bench by looking at the analog output's noise spectrum with the converter running, not just at the converter's output ripple — the ripple spec is necessary but not sufficient.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether a downstream LDO alone is enough to clean up the switching ripple, or whether you also need an LC filter ahead of it?
- What would you check in the converter's loop response to make sure the higher switching frequency hasn't compromised stability margin?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the current and gate voltage both look right but the part is hot, the first thing I'd do is separate "the FET is dissipating more than expected" from "the FET is fine but something around it is heating it." I'd measure the actual power dissipation rather than infer it: VDS across the FET while it's on, times the measured drain current, gives conduction loss; if that's small, the heat is coming from somewhere else — switching loss if it's being PWM'd, gate-drive loss, or thermal coupling from a nearby hot component.

For a low-side switch, the most common culprits are: the FET not actually being fully enhanced (gate voltage measured at the pin can look fine while the source is bouncing or the gate resistor/driver is too weak to charge the gate quickly, so it spends time in the linear region during transitions); RDS(on) being much higher than the datasheet's headline number because the part is hot (RDS(on) rises with temperature, so a part that's marginal cold runs away hot); or the on-resistance spec being quoted at a gate voltage the circuit doesn't actually deliver at the source potential. I'd also check whether the "2A resistive load" is really resistive — a load with inrush or a capacitive element can push instantaneous current well above 2A.

I'd work through it methodically: scope VGS and VDS at the FET pins (not at the driver) to see the real switching behavior and confirm it's fully on; measure the case and junction temperature rise versus dissipated power to check the thermal path (thermal vias, copper area, airflow); and compare measured RDS(on) at temperature against the datasheet curve. If it's a static switch, switching loss shouldn't exist, which narrows it to conduction loss or thermal path. If it's PWM'd, I'd look at transition times and gate charge. The fix depends on the root cause — a stronger driver or smaller gate resistor, a lower-RDS(on) part, better thermal copper, or correcting a gate-voltage assumption — so I'd want the root cause before changing parts.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish a thermal-path problem from a genuine excess-dissipation problem using only temperature measurements?
- If the FET is being PWM'd, how would you estimate switching loss from scope waveforms?

## Q4: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The starting question is what accuracy and stability the measurement actually needs, because a filtered supply rail can be perfectly adequate for a 10- or 12-bit measurement and completely inadequate for a 16-bit one.

A dedicated reference IC gives you a defined initial accuracy, a specified temperature coefficient, and — importantly — a specified long-term drift and output noise, all of which you can put in an error budget and defend in a design review. The parameters that actually matter for a high-resolution ADC are: initial accuracy (which you can often calibrate out), temperature drift (which you usually can't, unless you calibrate per-unit over temperature), output noise over the band the ADC cares about (reference noise adds directly to conversion noise), long-term drift, and the reference's own load regulation and thermal hysteresis. For a SAR ADC, the reference also has to recover quickly from the charge injected at each conversion, so its output impedance and settling behavior matter as much as its DC specs.

A filtered supply rail is cheaper and simpler, but its accuracy and drift are whatever the rail's regulator delivers, and its noise is whatever survives the filter — both usually worse and less well-characterized than a reference IC. The catch is that "filtered" is doing a lot of work in that sentence: you need to know the filter's attenuation across frequency and the rail's drift over temperature, and you need the rail to be quiet under all load conditions, which is hard to guarantee when the same rail feeds switching or digital loads.

My decision process: build the error budget from the ADC's resolution and the system's accuracy requirement, allocate a noise and drift budget to the reference, and see whether a filtered rail can meet it with margin across temperature and lifetime. If it can, use it and save the part. If it's marginal, the reference IC is usually the right call because it converts an unknown into a specified number. I'd also check whether the ADC has an internal reference that's good enough — often it isn't at high resolution, but it's worth confirming against the datasheet rather than assuming.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you measure a reference's output noise in a way that reflects what the ADC actually sees?
- What would you check to confirm a reference can recover from the ADC's sampling charge injection without drooping?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a cost-versus-risk conversation and try to make the trade-offs explicit and evidence-based rather than positional, because the manufacturing engineer's concern about cost is legitimate and I don't want to dismiss it.

First I'd make sure we're comparing the same thing: get the actual cost delta per board at the expected volume, not a rough impression, and separate the finish cost from the downstream costs it might affect — yield, rework, inspection difficulty, and field reliability. A cheaper finish that raises BGA assembly yield loss or creates intermittent joints can easily cost more than it saves, but I need numbers, not assertions, to make that case.

Then I'd lay out the technical reasons the finish matters for this specific board: the BGA's fine pitch and the flatness/uniformity needed for consistent joint formation, and the high-resolution ADC's sensitivity to the surface and joint quality under it. I'd frame it as risk to yield and to long-term reliability, and I'd propose how to resolve it rather than just defending my choice — for example, a small build with the cheaper finish to compare assembly yield and inspectability against the current finish, or a review of the assembly house's capability data for both finishes on similar packages. If the data shows the cheaper finish is fine for this board, I should be willing to change my mind; if it shows elevated risk, the data makes the decision for the team instead of it being my opinion versus theirs.

I'd also bring in the quality and reliability functions, since surface finish affects solder joint reliability and that's a shared concern, and I'd document whatever we decide with the rationale so it's traceable later. The goal is a decision the whole team owns, backed by evidence, not a win for either of us.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If a trial build isn't feasible on the schedule, what other evidence would you use to settle the question?
- How would you document the final decision so it holds up if the issue resurfaces during qualification or in the field?
