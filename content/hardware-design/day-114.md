# hardware-design — Day 114

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the output capacitor for a buck converter that feeds a load with periodic high-current bursts, where the output voltage must stay within a tight tolerance during the transient and the capacitor also has to satisfy the loop stability requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as two separate but interacting requirements: transient response and loop stability, and then reconcile them with the physical constraints of the capacitor technology.

For the transient requirement, the output capacitor has to supply the difference between the load's burst current and the inductor's slew-limited current for the duration of the burst. The relevant parameters are the capacitance value, the ESR, and the ESL. The voltage deviation during a load step is roughly the sum of three terms: the capacitive droop (I·Δt/C), the ESR step (I·ESR), and the ESL spike (L·di/dt). For a burst load, I'd estimate the worst-case burst amplitude and duration, decide how much droop I can tolerate, and back out a minimum capacitance. Then I'd check whether ESR alone would blow the budget — with modern ceramic capacitors ESR is usually small, but with electrolytics or polymer caps it can dominate.

For stability, the output capacitor is part of the compensation network. Most current-mode converters are compensated assuming a certain output capacitance and ESR zero; too little capacitance pushes the crossover frequency up and can make the loop unstable, while too much can push the crossover too low and slow the transient response. I'd check the converter's datasheet stability guidance and, if needed, run a bode plot or load-step simulation to confirm phase margin.

The practical reconciliation is usually: pick a ceramic capacitor bank sized for the transient, verify the ESR is low enough that the ESR zero doesn't cause issues, and check that the total capacitance is within the range the compensation was designed for. If the transient demands more capacitance than the loop can tolerate, I'd consider adding a feed-forward capacitor, adjusting the compensation, or adding a small bulk capacitor with controlled ESR in parallel. I'd also derate for DC bias and temperature — a 10 µF ceramic can lose most of its capacitance at rated voltage, so the effective capacitance at the operating point is what matters.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a bank of small ceramic capacitors and a single larger polymer capacitor for this application? What bench measurements would you make to confirm the output capacitor is actually doing its job during a load transient?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a MOSFET and a relay for a load-disconnect function in a battery-powered medical device, where the load must be isolated when the device is off and the switch has to be reliable over many cycles?

**Answer:** I'd frame the decision around four axes: isolation quality, reliability over cycle count, control complexity, and failure modes.

A relay gives galvanic isolation — when open, there's essentially no leakage path, which matters if the load is sensitive or if there's a safety requirement for physical disconnection. It also has very low on-resistance when closed. But relays are electromechanical: they have a finite cycle life (often tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand operations), they're larger, they draw coil current while energized, and they can fail welded closed or fail to close. Contact bounce can also inject transients into the load.

A MOSFET gives no galvanic isolation — even when off, there's leakage through the body diode and off-state drain current, and a back-to-back pair is needed if the load could be reverse-biased. But it has essentially unlimited cycle life, no moving parts, fast switching, low control power, and small size. The failure modes are different: a MOSFET can fail short (which for a disconnect function is a loss of the safety function) or fail open. For a safety-critical disconnect, a MOSFET's short-failure mode is a real concern, and I'd want to think about whether the architecture can tolerate it — for example, a series redundant pair, or a monitoring circuit that detects a shorted FET.

For a medical device that must isolate a load when off, I'd lean toward a MOSFET for cycle life and size, but I'd add a back-to-back configuration to block body-diode conduction, and I'd consider whether the application requires true galvanic isolation — if it does, a relay or a solid-state relay (which is optically isolated) may be the right answer despite the trade-offs. I'd also check the leakage current requirement: if the spec says "no current flows when off," a MOSFET's leakage may not meet it, and a relay or a depletion-mode device might be needed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you detect a MOSFET that has failed short in a load-disconnect circuit? What relay parameters would you check if you did choose a relay, and how would you mitigate contact bounce?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device's analog front-end works correctly when powered from a bench supply, but shows elevated noise and occasional offset shifts when powered from the device's internal battery and boost converter?

**Answer:** The fact that the behavior changes with the power source is the key clue — it points at the power delivery path rather than the signal chain itself. I'd work through it systematically.

First, I'd characterize the difference between the two supplies. The bench supply is likely low-noise and well-regulated; the boost converter is a switching source with ripple at the switching frequency and its harmonics, plus potential low-frequency instability or burst-mode behavior at light load. I'd measure the supply rail at the analog front-end's power pins with a scope (AC-coupled, short ground lead) under both conditions and compare the ripple amplitude and frequency content. I'd also check for low-frequency drift — boost converters can have slow output voltage wander if the control loop is marginal, and that would show up as an offset shift.

Second, I'd look at the PSRR of the front-end. If the boost ripple is at a frequency where the front-end's PSRR is poor — often the case at low frequencies or at the switching frequency if there's no post-regulation — the ripple will couple into the signal. I'd check whether there's an LDO between the boost and the analog rail, and if so, whether its PSRR is adequate at the boost's switching frequency. If there's no LDO, that's a likely culprit.

Third, I'd check grounding and layout. The boost converter's switching currents flow in a loop that includes the input capacitor, inductor, and ground; if the analog front-end's ground reference shares any of that return path, the switching currents will develop voltage drops that appear as noise. I'd verify the analog ground is a quiet, separate return that ties to the power ground at a single point, and that the boost's hot loop is physically compact and away from the analog section.

Fourth, I'd check for conducted and radiated coupling — the boost inductor and switching node can radiate, and if the analog front-end's input traces run near them, that's a coupling path. I'd probe with a scope and a small loop to find the aggressor, and consider shielding or rerouting.

The offset shift is interesting because it suggests a DC or low-frequency mechanism rather than pure ripple — possibly thermal (the boost converter heating the board and shifting an op-amp's offset), or a ground-reference shift, or the boost's output voltage changing slightly with load and the front-end's reference being derived from it. I'd measure the reference voltage and the front-end's offset under both supplies to separate those.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure PSRR of the front-end at the boost's switching frequency, and what would you do if it turned out to be inadequate? If the issue turned out to be thermal, how would you confirm it?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting the gate resistor for a MOSFET used as a high-side load switch, where the switch must limit inrush current into a large capacitive load but also turn off quickly enough to protect the load during a fault?

**Answer:** The gate resistor sets the trade-off between turn-on speed (and therefore inrush current) and turn-off speed (and therefore fault response), and the two requirements often pull in opposite directions, so I'd start by quantifying both.

For turn-on, the inrush current into a capacitive load is roughly C·dV/dt, and the dV/dt is set by how fast the gate charges through the resistor and how the MOSFET's transfer characteristic responds. A larger gate resistor slows the turn-on, reducing inrush, but it also increases the time the MOSFET spends in its linear region, which means higher instantaneous power dissipation — the safe operating area (SOA) has to be checked for that pulse. I'd calculate the worst-case inrush energy and compare it to the MOSFET's SOA curve for the expected pulse width, and I'd also check the load's own inrush tolerance.

For turn-off, a smaller gate resistor discharges the gate faster, turning the FET off quickly. But if the load is inductive or has significant current, fast turn-off can cause a voltage spike (L·di/dt) that could exceed the FET's Vds rating or couple into sensitive circuitry. So turn-off speed is also bounded by the load's characteristics and any snubber or clamp.

The common solution when the two requirements conflict is an asymmetric gate drive: a diode in parallel with the gate resistor so that turn-on goes through the resistor (slow) and turn-off goes through the diode (fast), or vice versa depending on which direction needs to be slow. I'd also consider an active inrush limiter — a dedicated hot-swap controller or a current-limited gate drive — if the inrush requirement is severe enough that a passive resistor can't satisfy both ends.

I'd also check the gate driver's source/sink capability, because the resistor value only matters if the driver can actually deliver the current; and I'd verify the gate voltage stays within the FET's absolute maximum during transients, especially if there's any ringing on the gate node.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the inrush current and SOA margin on the bench without damaging the FET? What would you do if the asymmetric gate drive still couldn't meet both requirements?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the quality engineer argues that your chosen approach to documenting the analog front-end's noise budget is insufficient — they want a formal, traceable calculation showing how each component's noise contribution rolls up to the system-level requirement, rather than the spreadsheet summary you've prepared. You believe the spreadsheet captures the same information and that a formal document would add schedule risk without changing the design. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd start by recognizing that the quality engineer is raising a legitimate concern about traceability, not just format — in a regulated medical device context, the ability to show that a design meets its requirements, and to defend that showing to an auditor, is part of the deliverable, not overhead. So I'd treat the disagreement as a question of "what does the evidence need to demonstrate, and what's the most efficient way to demonstrate it," rather than "whose document format wins."

First, I'd ask the quality engineer to walk me through what specifically they need to see that the spreadsheet doesn't provide — is it the traceability from each noise source to a requirement, the calculation methodology, the review sign-off, or the format for the design history file? Often the gap is narrower than it first appears, and the spreadsheet may already contain most of the content but lack the linking and sign-off structure.

Second, I'd propose a path that meets the requirement without duplicating work: for example, converting the spreadsheet into a controlled document with a requirements traceability matrix that links each noise contributor to its source, its calculation, and the system-level allocation, and having it reviewed and signed off. That's largely a restructuring of existing content rather than new analysis, so the schedule impact is smaller than a from-scratch document.

Third, if there's genuine schedule pressure, I'd escalate the trade-off transparently — bring the project lead into the conversation with a clear statement of what the formal document costs in time and what the risk of skipping it is. I wouldn't unilaterally decide to skip a quality requirement; that's a decision above my pay grade and it's the kind of thing that comes back to bite in an audit.

Finally, I'd look for the root cause: if this is the second or third time a documentation-format disagreement has come up, it may be worth agreeing on a standard template for noise budgets and similar analyses early in the project so it doesn't recur. That's a process improvement that pays for itself.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure a noise budget traceability matrix so that it's actually useful to the design team and not just an audit artifact? If the project lead decided to defer the formal document to later in the schedule, what risk would you flag and how would you mitigate it?

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the boost converter's ripple is acceptable at the analog front-end without adding an LDO, and what measurement would settle it? If the root cause turned out to be a ground return shared between the boost and the analog section, how would you fix it without a board respin?
