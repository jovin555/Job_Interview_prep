# hardware-design — Day 106

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the sense resistor and amplifier topology for measuring current in a bidirectional H-bridge motor drive, where the current can flow in either direction, the peak magnitude is a few amps, and the measurement needs to be reasonably accurate over temperature without wasting power in the shunt?

**Answer:** The first decision is where to sense: high-side, low-side, or in-line. Low-side sensing is the simplest and cheapest — the shunt sits between the bridge's low-side return and ground, so the amplifier sees a near-ground common mode and you can use a standard difference amplifier. The catch is that it creates a ground disturbance between the bridge return and the system ground, which can inject noise into sensitive analog circuitry and breaks the "motor return is solid ground" assumption. High-side sensing keeps the ground intact but forces the amplifier to handle a common-mode voltage that swings with the bridge and can move fast during PWM. In-line sensing (inside the bridge, in series with the motor) gives you the true winding current including recirculation, but requires an amplifier with very high common-mode range and good CMRR at high frequency.

For a bidirectional measurement, I'd pick a current-sense amplifier (or a difference amplifier) with a defined common-mode range that covers the full bridge swing, and I'd check CMRR across frequency, not just at DC — during PWM edges the common mode slews quickly, and poor AC CMRR turns that into a current-measurement glitch. For the shunt itself, I'd size it from the accuracy budget: the full-scale voltage across the shunt should be large enough that the amplifier's offset and input-referred noise are a small fraction of full scale, but small enough that I²R loss and self-heating stay acceptable. A few tens of millivolts at peak current is a common compromise. I'd choose a low-TCR shunt (metal alloy rather than thick film) so the reading doesn't drift with self-heating, and I'd lay it out with Kelvin connections so the sense traces don't pick up the high-current path's IR drop.

Then I'd budget the error terms explicitly: shunt tolerance, shunt TCR, amplifier offset and offset drift, gain error, and CMRR error at the actual common-mode voltage. If the budget doesn't close with a single-stage amplifier, I'd consider a dedicated bidirectional current-sense IC with an internal matched resistor network, which typically has much better CMRR than a discrete difference amp built from four separate resistors.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the PWM-induced common-mode transient if the amplifier's CMRR is marginal — would you filter, sample synchronously, or change the topology? And how would you verify the shunt's actual TCR and the amplifier's CMRR on the bench rather than trusting the datasheet?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a linear regulator and a switching regulator for a low-current analog rail in a medical device, and what factors would drive the decision beyond efficiency?

**Answer:** Efficiency is usually the least interesting factor for a low-current analog rail — if the rail draws tens of milliamps, the absolute dissipation is small and the efficiency difference between an LDO and a switcher may be a few tens of milliwatts. What actually drives the decision is noise, PSRR, and the downstream sensitivity of the load.

An LDO's main virtue is that it has no switching node: no inductor, no switching harmonics, no radiated field from a hot loop, and its output noise is low and broadband rather than concentrated at a switching frequency and its harmonics. Its PSRR is also high at low frequency and rolls off with frequency, so it cleans up low-frequency supply variation well. The cost is dissipation: the drop across the pass element times the load current, which becomes a thermal problem if the input-output differential is large or the rail current is significant, especially inside a sealed enclosure.

A switching regulator wins when the input-output differential is large, the current is high, or the thermal budget is tight. But its output ripple and switching noise can couple into a sensitive analog front-end, and even a downstream LDO only attenuates that noise by its PSRR at the switching frequency — which is often only 20–40 dB at a few hundred kHz, not the 60+ dB it provides at low frequency. So "switcher followed by LDO" is not automatically clean; it depends on the LDO's high-frequency PSRR and on how well the switching harmonics are filtered before the LDO.

My decision framework: if the rail current is low and the input-output differential is modest, an LDO is usually the right answer for noise reasons and simplicity. If the differential or current is large enough that an LDO would dissipate meaningfully, I'd use a switcher but treat the analog rail as a noise-sensitive load — choose a switching frequency that avoids the analog bandwidth, add an LC or ferrite-plus-capacitor filter ahead of the LDO, and verify the residual ripple at the load with a scope and a spectrum analyzer rather than assuming the LDO cleans it up.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the LDO's high-frequency PSRR is adequate, and what would you measure to confirm it? And if the LDO is dissipating too much, what intermediate options exist between "pure LDO" and "switcher plus LDO"?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?

**Answer:** Temperature-dependent behavior with unchanged load points at a parameter that drifts with temperature — either in the regulator's control loop, in a passive component, or in the feedback network. I'd start by separating "the regulator is misbehaving" from "the regulator is fine but something around it changed."

First, I'd measure the actual output ripple and the switching node waveform at temperature, not just the DC output. If the ripple grows but the switching frequency and duty cycle look normal, I'd suspect the output capacitor: electrolytics and some ceramics lose capacitance and gain ESR with temperature, and a capacitor that's marginal at room temperature can push the loop toward instability or simply fail to absorb the inductor ripple. I'd check the capacitor's temperature characteristics against the datasheet and, if possible, swap in a known-good part at temperature to confirm.

If the switching waveform itself changes — duty cycle wandering, pulse skipping, or frequency shifting — I'd look at the feedback divider and the reference. Resistor dividers drift with temperature, and if the divider's tempco is poor or the resistors are mismatched, the effective setpoint moves and the loop compensates in ways that can show up as ripple or dropout. I'd also check the regulator's internal reference drift and whether the part is operating near a thermal limit where it enters thermal foldback or hiccup mode — "occasional dropout" is a classic symptom of a thermal or current-limit protection engaging intermittently.

I'd also consider the inductor: saturation current falls with temperature in many ferrite materials, and if the design is marginal, the inductor can start saturating at high temperature even at the same load current, which shows up as a sudden increase in ripple and a droop in output. And I'd check for cold solder joints or connector resistance that changes with thermal expansion — a marginal joint can behave differently when the board is warm.

The disciplined approach is to instrument the board at temperature: log input voltage, output voltage, switching node, and if possible the feedback pin, and correlate the ripple increase with a specific node. That usually localizes it to one component rather than guessing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish output-capacitor ESR drift from a loop-stability problem when both produce increased ripple? And what would you check in the layout if the problem only appears on some boards and not others?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting and verifying the load capacitance for a crystal oscillator, and what would you check on the bench to confirm the circuit has adequate startup margin across temperature?

**Answer:** The load capacitance the crystal "sees" is the series combination of the two load capacitors plus stray capacitance — PCB traces, the MCU pin capacitance, and the crystal's own package capacitance. The datasheet's CL specification is the total the crystal wants, so the external capacitors are chosen so that C1 in series with C2, plus stray, equals CL. A common mistake is to set each capacitor equal to CL, which over-loads the crystal and pulls the frequency low; the correct starting point is roughly 2×CL minus stray for each capacitor, then trim empirically.

The reason this matters beyond frequency accuracy is startup margin. A crystal oscillator is a feedback loop that needs enough loop gain to start oscillating from noise, and the negative resistance the amplifier presents must exceed the crystal's equivalent series resistance (ESR) by a comfortable margin — a common rule of thumb is at least 3–5× the ESR, with more margin for high-ESR crystals or low-drive oscillators. Too much load capacitance reduces the negative resistance and can prevent startup, especially at temperature extremes where the crystal's ESR rises and the amplifier's transconductance falls.

On the bench, I'd verify startup margin by measuring the negative resistance directly: inject a small signal at the crystal frequency through a series resistor and observe whether oscillation sustains, or use the standard technique of adding series resistance until oscillation just fails and confirming the margin. I'd do this at the temperature extremes, not just room temperature, because that's where margin is thinnest. I'd also check drive level — the power dissipated in the crystal — against the datasheet maximum, since over-driving ages or damages the crystal, and I'd verify the actual oscillating frequency against the expected pull from the load capacitance.

Finally, I'd check startup time and behavior at power-on: does it start reliably every time, including at cold temperature and with the supply ramping slowly? A crystal that starts "most of the time" is a latent field failure.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a load-capacitance problem from a drive-level problem if the oscillator starts but the frequency is off? And what layout practices matter most around the crystal to keep stray capacitance predictable?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the software lead argues that the device's power-on self-test can be simplified by removing the hardware checks — rail monitors, reference checks — and relying on firmware to verify the system by reading the ADC and checking that values are in range. The software lead argues this reduces component count and board area. You believe the hardware checks are necessary because they verify the analog signal chain independently of the ADC and the firmware. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a question about what the self-test is actually supposed to prove, and try to get the team aligned on that before arguing about components. The software lead's proposal is reasonable on its face — fewer parts, less board area — but it changes the nature of the test: a firmware-reads-the-ADC check verifies that the ADC and firmware agree with each other, not that the analog chain ahead of the ADC is healthy. If the reference has drifted, or a rail is out of tolerance, or an amplifier is saturated, the ADC can still return "in-range" values and the self-test passes. That's the gap I'd want the team to see explicitly.

I'd frame it as a coverage question: what failure modes does each approach actually detect, and which ones matter for this device's risk profile? I'd walk through the analog chain and list what a hardware rail monitor and reference check catch that a firmware ADC read cannot — reference drift, rail collapse, front-end saturation, a stuck channel — and what the firmware check catches that hardware doesn't. Then I'd ask whether the risk file supports dropping the hardware coverage, because in a medical device the self-test's role is usually tied to a hazard mitigation, and removing a mitigation needs to go through risk management, not just a design-review vote.

I'd also look for a middle path rather than a binary. Sometimes a single hardware reference check plus a rail monitor is enough to anchor the firmware test — the firmware can then verify the rest of the chain relative to a known-good reference, which gives most of the coverage at much lower part count than a full hardware self-test. If the software lead's concern is board area, I'd explore whether the monitors can be integrated into an existing supervisor or PMIC rather than added as discrete parts.

Throughout, I'd keep it collaborative: the software lead may be right that the current hardware self-test is over-built, and the conversation is more productive if I'm genuinely open to that. But I'd be clear that the decision needs to be made on detection coverage and risk, with the risk management file as the arbiter, not on component count alone. If we can't agree, I'd escalate to the risk-management review with both proposals documented, so the decision is made with the trade-offs visible.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document the coverage comparison so the decision is defensible in a design history file? And if the team chooses the reduced hardware approach, what would you want to verify on the bench to confirm the firmware test actually catches the failure modes you're relying on it for?
