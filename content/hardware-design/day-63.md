# hardware-design — Day 63

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?

**Answer:** I'd start by deciding where the shunt sits in the circuit, because that choice drives everything else. A high-side shunt keeps the sense element at the supply rail and preserves a solid ground reference for the motor, but it forces the amplifier into a high common-mode voltage, so I'd need a current-sense amp (or difference amp) with a common-mode input range that comfortably covers the full bus voltage plus transients. A low-side shunt is simpler and cheaper to sense, but it lifts the motor's ground reference and can confuse downstream circuits that assume a true ground — and it can't detect a short to ground on the high side. For a bidirectional load, either way I need a topology that can measure current in both directions, which usually means a difference amplifier or a dedicated bidirectional current-sense amp with a mid-supply reference so that zero current reads as mid-scale.

For the shunt value itself, I'd work backwards from the accuracy budget. The full-scale sense voltage should be large enough that the amplifier's input offset voltage and its drift over temperature are a small fraction of full scale — offset drift is often the dominant error term, so I'd pick a shunt that gives a sense voltage of at least tens of millivolts at full load. Then I'd check power dissipation: P = I²R at the maximum continuous current, and confirm the resistor's power rating, temperature coefficient, and derating curve all hold up. A four-terminal (Kelvin) shunt is worth the extra cost when accuracy matters, because it removes the solder-joint and trace resistance from the measurement. I'd also verify the amplifier's gain error, CMRR, and offset drift over the full temperature range, and add a small differential RC filter at the inputs to reject switching noise from the motor drive without loading the shunt.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you lay out the shunt and the sense traces to avoid picking up magnetic noise from the motor current itself?
- If the motor is PWM-driven, how would you keep the switching edges from corrupting the current measurement?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?

**Answer:** Temperature-dependent behavior that appears with no change in load points to a parameter that drifts with temperature, so I'd approach it as a systematic hunt rather than a guess. First I'd characterize the failure precisely: at what temperature does it start, does it correlate with a specific load step or just ambient soak, and is the "dropout" a true loss of regulation or a transient dip? I'd monitor the switch node, the output, the feedback pin, and the input rail simultaneously with a scope, using a thermal chamber or a heat gun with a thermocouple to map the onset temperature.

Then I'd work through the likely culprits. The output capacitor is a common one — many capacitor dielectrics lose capacitance and gain ESR as temperature rises, which degrades loop stability and increases ripple. The inductor's saturation current and DCR also shift with temperature, and if the design is marginal on saturation margin, the inductance can fall enough to change the ripple current. The feedback network's resistors drift, and if the reference is internal, its tempco matters. The controller's own current-limit or thermal-foldback behavior could also be kicking in. I'd also check whether the compensation is marginal at room temperature and simply crosses into instability as component values drift — a Bode plot or a load-step response at both temperature extremes would reveal that. The fix depends on the cause: a better-dielectric output cap, more margin on inductor saturation, a compensation network with more phase margin, or a different reference. The key is to confirm the mechanism before changing parts, otherwise you're just swapping components and hoping.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you verify that the loop has adequate phase margin across the full temperature range without a full Bode analyzer?
- What would you check first if the dropout only happens on a cold start rather than a warm soak?

## Q3: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The core distinction is that a comparator is designed to be operated open-loop and to saturate cleanly, while an op-amp is designed for closed-loop linear operation and is usually compensated to be stable in that mode. For a protection threshold, I want a clean, fast, well-defined digital transition at the crossing point, so a comparator is the natural choice — it has specified propagation delay, output logic levels that interface directly to the rest of the system, and often built-in hysteresis to prevent chatter near the threshold. An op-amp used as a comparator can work in a pinch, but it may have slow saturation recovery, unpredictable behavior if the inputs are driven far apart, and no guaranteed output swing to logic levels without extra circuitry.

That said, the decision isn't automatic. If the threshold needs to be very precise and the comparator's input offset or hysteresis is too coarse, I might look at a precision comparator or add a trim. If I need the threshold to be adjustable or to have a specific hysteresis window, I'd size the positive-feedback network accordingly. If the signal is slow and noisy, hysteresis is essential to avoid multiple transitions. If the protection must respond in microseconds, I'd check the comparator's propagation delay and the slew rate of the signal feeding it. And if the output needs to drive a latch or a logic input with a specific polarity, I'd confirm the comparator's output stage matches. In short: comparator by default for threshold detection, op-amp only if the application genuinely needs linear gain and the threshold is a secondary function.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you set the hysteresis window so it rejects noise but doesn't delay the response past the protection requirement?
- What would you check in the comparator datasheet to make sure it behaves predictably when the inputs are driven well beyond the threshold?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?

**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware POST is to confirm that the analog signal chain is alive and within its expected operating envelope before the device is trusted to make measurements — and critically, to do so without relying on the very firmware and ADC that the test is meant to validate. So I'd design it to inject a known stimulus or check a known reference at the hardware level and compare against a hardware-defined window, not a firmware-computed one.

Concretely, I'd want to verify a few things. First, that the supply rails are present and within tolerance — a simple window comparator or a supervisor IC per rail, with the outputs ANDed into a single "rails good" signal. Second, that the voltage reference is at its expected value, again via a comparator against a divided-down version of a known-good reference. Third, that the signal chain has continuity — for example, injecting a small known current or voltage at the front end and confirming the output of the conditioning stage moves within an expected band. Fourth, that the ADC's input is not stuck at a rail or floating, which a window check on the buffered signal can catch. The outputs of these checks would feed a hardware latch or a logic gate that holds the system in a safe state until the test passes, so the firmware can't simply ignore a failure.

The key design principle is independence: the test should use its own reference, its own comparators, and its own logic, so a firmware hang or an ADC failure can't mask a real fault. I'd also make the test verifiable — documented thresholds, a way to inject a fault during validation, and a clear pass/fail indication that the firmware can read but not override.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you avoid false failures during power-up transients while still catching a genuine rail fault?
- How would you validate that the POST actually catches a stuck-at fault in the signal chain during design verification?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements question first, not a component argument, because the disagreement is really about the assumed use case. My first move would be to get the actual expected mating-cycle count on the table — from the product requirements, the clinical workflow, and any field-service assumptions — rather than each of us arguing from our own mental model. If the requirement genuinely is "connect once and leave in place," I'd want that documented and agreed, because it changes the connector spec. If field replacement or repeated disconnection is plausible, then the reliability engineer's concern is legitimate and I'd need to either accept the higher-cycle part or find a middle ground.

I'd also separate the two failure modes: the connector's rated mating cycles versus its other reliability attributes — contact resistance stability, retention force, sealing, and resistance to cleaning agents. A connector can meet the cycle count but fail on one of those, or vice versa. I'd propose we evaluate the candidate parts against the full set of requirements, not just cycle count, and if the data isn't available, run a small qualification test or pull the vendor's reliability data. If the higher-cycle part is clearly the safer choice and the cost delta is small relative to the device's overall BOM and the risk of a field failure, I'd support the change. If the cost is significant and the use case truly is single-connect, I'd document the assumption and the rationale, and make sure the risk file reflects it. The goal is to make the decision on evidence and agreed requirements, not on whose preference wins.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you document the agreed mating-cycle assumption so it's traceable in the design history file?
- If the reliability engineer and you still disagreed after the data review, how would you escalate or resolve it?
