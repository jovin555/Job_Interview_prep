# hardware-design — Day 122

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the output capacitor for a buck converter that feeds a load with periodic high-current bursts, where the output voltage must stay within a tight tolerance during the transient and the capacitor also has to satisfy the loop stability requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as two separate but coupled requirements and size for whichever one dominates. First, the transient requirement: during a load step, the output capacitor supplies the difference between the inductor's current and the load's demand until the loop responds. The voltage deviation has three contributors — the initial IR drop across the capacitor's ESR, the capacitive droop from charge depletion, and the recovery overshoot from the loop's finite bandwidth. I'd estimate the charge the cap must supply during the loop's response time (roughly the inverse of the crossover frequency) and pick capacitance so the resulting droop stays inside the tolerance, then check that the ESR contribution at the burst current is acceptable. For a burst load, the burst repetition rate matters too — if bursts come faster than the loop can recover, the cap has to absorb the average deficit, not just the single-event deficit. Second, the stability requirement: the output capacitor's ESR forms a zero in the loop, and the capacitance sets the power-stage pole. Too little capacitance pushes the crossover frequency up and can leave inadequate phase margin; too much can slow the loop and hurt transient recovery. I'd pull the regulator's datasheet stability curves — most specify a minimum and sometimes maximum output capacitance and an ESR window — and confirm my chosen part sits comfortably inside it across tolerance and temperature, since ceramic capacitance derates significantly with DC bias and temperature. I'd also consider whether a mix of bulk and ceramic is better than one large ceramic: bulk electrolytic or polymer gives cheap energy storage for the burst, ceramics give low ESR for the high-frequency component. Finally, I'd verify on the bench with a real load-step test at the worst-case burst amplitude and repetition rate, and check the loop's phase margin with a network analyzer or by observing the transient ringing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the burst load needs a dedicated bulk capacitor local to the load rather than relying on the converter's output cap? What would you check if the transient response looked fine at room temperature but degraded at temperature extremes?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The core question is what the circuit actually needs to do at the decision point. A comparator is designed for open-loop operation: it has high gain, fast propagation, and often built-in hysteresis, and its output is intended to slam between logic levels. An op-amp is designed for closed-loop linear operation — its open-loop behavior is not characterized for clean switching, it may slew slowly, it can oscillate or exhibit phase reversal when overdriven, and its output swing may not reach the logic rails cleanly. For a protection threshold, I'd default to a comparator unless there's a specific reason not to. The drivers: first, speed — if the protection must respond in microseconds, I'd look at comparator propagation delay and make sure it's well inside the budget, including the comparator's own delay plus the downstream logic and switch. Second, hysteresis — a protection threshold sitting near a noisy signal will chatter without hysteresis, so I'd want either a comparator with internal hysteresis or an external positive-feedback network, and I'd calculate the hysteresis band against the expected noise amplitude. Third, input characteristics — offset voltage and bias current matter because they shift the effective threshold; for a precision threshold I'd pick a comparator with low offset and check offset drift over temperature, since a threshold that moves with temperature may trip early or late. Fourth, output type — open-drain versus push-pull determines how it interfaces to the rest of the circuit; open-drain is useful for wired-OR fault buses. An op-amp can be pressed into threshold service, and sometimes it's the right call when the same amplifier is already in the design and the speed and hysteresis requirements are modest, but I'd be explicit about the risks: no guaranteed clean switching, potential phase reversal on overdrive, and slower response. For anything safety-relevant, I'd use a proper comparator and document the rationale.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you add hysteresis to a comparator without compromising the threshold accuracy? What failure modes would you check on a comparator used in a protection path — for example, what happens if its input is driven beyond its supply rails?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting and verifying the load capacitance for a crystal oscillator, and what would you check on the bench to confirm the circuit has adequate startup margin across temperature?

**Answer:** The load capacitance is set by the crystal's specified CL and the board's stray capacitance. The two load caps in the typical Pierce configuration appear in series from the crystal's perspective, so each cap is roughly 2×(CL − Cstray), where Cstray includes the PCB traces, the MCU pin capacitance, and any pad capacitance — typically a few picofarads. I'd start from the crystal datasheet's CL, estimate stray from the layout, and pick standard values close to the calculated result, then verify rather than assume. Getting CL wrong pulls the frequency off nominal and, more importantly, can reduce the negative resistance margin the oscillator needs to start and sustain oscillation. On the bench I'd check several things. First, startup: power-cycle the board many times at the cold and hot temperature extremes and confirm the oscillator starts every time, not just typically — I'd look at the oscillator output on a scope with a low-capacitance probe and confirm it reaches stable amplitude within the MCU's specified startup time. Second, drive level: measure the amplitude across the crystal and compare against the crystal's maximum drive level; overdriving ages or damages the crystal. Third, frequency accuracy over temperature: measure the actual output frequency against a reference at several temperatures and confirm it stays inside the budget, which also indirectly validates that CL is close to right. Fourth, negative resistance margin: the standard technique is to add a series resistor in the crystal path and increase it until oscillation just fails, then confirm the failure point is several times the crystal's specified ESR — a healthy margin means the oscillator will start reliably despite part-to-part variation and aging. I'd also check that the layout keeps the crystal traces short and away from noisy nodes, since stray coupling can injection-lock or disturb the oscillator.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the oscillator started reliably at room temperature but failed at cold — how would you distinguish a load-capacitance problem from a drive-level or ESR problem? How does the choice of crystal (fundamental versus overtone, or a resonator versus a crystal) change the verification approach?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's inductor is audible (whining) at light load, even though the output voltage and ripple are within specification?

**Answer:** Audible noise from an inductor means the converter is exciting the inductor's mechanical resonance in the audio band, typically because the switching frequency has dropped into that range or because the converter is entering a pulse-skipping or burst mode at light load. The first thing I'd do is scope the switch node and look at the actual switching behavior at light load — is it fixed-frequency, is it skipping pulses, is it bursting with a repetition rate in the hundreds of hertz to a few kilohertz? Many converters deliberately enter a power-saving mode at light load that reduces switching frequency and skips cycles, and the burst repetition rate is often right in the audible range. That's a normal behavior, but it can be unacceptable in a quiet medical environment. I'd then check whether the inductor itself is the culprit or whether it's the ceramic output capacitors singing — ceramics exhibit piezoelectric effects and can be surprisingly loud, and the fix is different. For the inductor, the mechanical noise comes from magnetostriction and winding vibration; a different core material, a potted or overmolded inductor, or a mechanically damped part can help. For the ceramics, changing to a different dielectric or adding mechanical decoupling can help. On the electrical side, the options are: force the converter into fixed-frequency PWM at light load (often a mode pin or a register setting), raise the switching frequency above the audible band if the design allows, or add a minimum load to keep the converter out of the skipping region. I'd weigh each against efficiency, since forcing PWM at light load costs quiescent current, which matters in a battery-powered device. I'd also confirm the noise isn't a symptom of something worse — for example, unstable loop behavior or a saturating inductor — by checking the switch waveform for subharmonic oscillation and confirming the inductor current stays below saturation. Finally, I'd verify the fix across the full load range and temperature, because the audible behavior often only appears in a narrow band of load and temperature.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish inductor noise from capacitor noise on the bench? If the converter's light-load efficiency mode is the cause and you can't disable it, what other approaches would you consider?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the manufacturing engineer argues that your chosen PCB surface finish is too expensive and proposes replacing it with a cheaper alternative. You believe the finish you specified is necessary because the board includes a high-density BGA package and a high-resolution ADC that need a flat, uniform surface for reliable solder joints and consistent electrical performance. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a technical decision that needs to be made on evidence, not on seniority, and I'd want the manufacturing engineer to feel heard rather than overruled. First, I'd make sure I understand their actual concern — is it purely cost, or is there a yield, availability, or process-compatibility issue behind it? Sometimes the cost argument is a proxy for something else, like a supplier that can't run the finish I specified. Then I'd lay out the technical requirements explicitly: the BGA's pitch and pad count, the flatness and coplanarity the assembly process needs, the solder-joint reliability expectations over the device's life, and any electrical concerns like contact resistance or surface finish effects on high-frequency performance. I'd ask the manufacturing engineer to bring data on the proposed alternative — its flatness, its performance on fine-pitch BGA, its history in similar assemblies, and its cost delta including any yield impact. If the alternative genuinely meets the requirements, I should be open to it; the goal is the right board, not my board. If it doesn't, I'd want to quantify the risk: what's the expected yield loss or field-failure risk, and how does that compare to the cost saving? For a Class II medical device, I'd also bring in the regulatory angle — a finish change late in the design may trigger re-qualification, and that cost and schedule impact belongs in the comparison. I'd propose a path forward: if there's uncertainty, build a small test vehicle with both finishes and run assembly and reliability checks, so the decision is made on data rather than opinion. And I'd document the decision and rationale in the design record, because if we later see field issues, the reasoning needs to be traceable. The key is to keep the conversation on requirements and evidence, escalate only if we can't converge, and make sure the final decision is one the whole team understands and owns.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the manufacturing engineer's data showed the cheaper finish met the requirements on paper but you still had concerns about long-term reliability? How would you handle it if the disagreement escalated to your manager and the manufacturing engineer's manager without resolution?
