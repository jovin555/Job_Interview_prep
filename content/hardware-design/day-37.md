## Q1: How would you approach designing a battery management system for a medical device that uses a 2-cell Li-ion battery pack and must support both charging and discharging while the device is in continuous use?

**Answer:** The first priority is safety and reliability, since this is a medical device. I'd start by defining the system requirements: charge current, discharge current profile, operating voltage range (5.0–8.4V for 2S), and whether the device needs to operate while charging. For a 2S configuration, cell balancing becomes important — I'd use a dedicated battery protection IC that handles overvoltage, undervoltage, overcurrent, and short-circuit protection on each cell, plus passive balancing during charging. For the charging topology, I'd evaluate a switching charger for efficiency (since the device is in continuous use, heat dissipation matters) versus a linear charger for simplicity and lower noise. Given continuous operation, a switching charger with good EMI filtering is usually the right call. For the "charge while in use" requirement, I'd design the power path so the charger and the system load are properly isolated — either using a dedicated power-path management IC or a discrete OR-ing topology with MOSFETs — so that the load doesn't see charging ripple or voltage dips when the charger transitions between CC and CV modes. I'd also include a fuel gauge with coulomb counting and voltage-based correction, since the device has variable loads. Finally, I'd ensure the charging and protection circuitry is isolated from the patient-accessible parts per IEC 60601, and that the battery pack itself has thermal monitoring with a hardware-based cutoff independent of the main processor.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle the transition between charging and discharging when the external power is removed or applied?
- What specific IEC 60601 considerations apply to the battery charging circuitry in a patient-connected device?

## Q2: Walk me through how you would debug a circuit where a low-dropout regulator (LDO) output is stable at light load but oscillates when the load current increases beyond a certain threshold.

**Answer:** This is a classic LDO stability issue, typically related to the output capacitor's equivalent series resistance (ESR) and the LDO's phase margin. I'd approach this systematically. First, I'd verify the output capacitor: check its value, ESR, and dielectric type. Many LDOs require a minimum ESR for stability, and ceramic capacitors with very low ESR can push the zero frequency too high, reducing phase margin. If the capacitor is ceramic, I'd check if the effective capacitance drops under DC bias — a 10µF X5R capacitor at 3.3V bias might only be 4–5µF, which could push the pole frequency into an unstable region. I'd also check the load transient response with an oscilloscope to see the oscillation frequency and waveform shape — this tells me whether it's a control-loop stability issue or something else like thermal cycling. Next, I'd examine the PCB layout: is the output capacitor placed close enough to the LDO's output pin? Is there excessive parasitic inductance in the trace between the LDO and the capacitor? I'd also check if there's a large bulk capacitor downstream that might be interacting with the LDO's output impedance. If the design uses a capacitor with adjustable ESR (like a tantalum or electrolytic), I'd verify the ESR is within the LDO's specified range. If everything checks out, I'd look at the load itself — is there a large capacitive load at the point of load that's creating a second pole? I might need to add a small series resistor or ferrite bead between the LDO output and the load to isolate the load capacitance. Finally, I'd review the LDO's datasheet stability curves and verify my operating point (input voltage, output voltage, load current) is within the stable region.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine whether the oscillation is a control-loop stability issue versus a layout/parasitic issue?
- What if the LDO is stable with a 10µF ceramic but unstable with a 22µF ceramic — what would that tell you?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a gate drive circuit for a high-side N-channel MOSFET used in a battery-powered load switch, where the battery voltage ranges from 3.0V to 4.2V and the load can draw up to 3A?

**Answer:** The key challenge here is that an N-channel MOSFET requires a gate voltage significantly above the source voltage to fully enhance — and since the source sits at the battery voltage, I need a gate voltage higher than the battery. With a 3.0V minimum battery voltage, I can't use a simple gate driver powered from the battery alone. I'd consider several approaches. The most common is a charge pump or bootstrap circuit that generates a gate voltage of roughly 2× the battery voltage. A charge pump using a small capacitor and a few diodes or a dedicated charge-pump IC can generate 6–8V above ground, which is sufficient for most logic-level MOSFETs. Alternatively, I could use a low-side N-channel MOSFET with a high-side P-channel MOSFET in a complementary configuration, but that adds complexity. For the gate drive itself, I'd use a dedicated gate driver IC with proper timing — it should source enough peak current to switch the MOSFET quickly (to minimize switching losses) but also include a gate resistor to control slew rate and reduce EMI. I'd also add a pull-down resistor on the gate to ensure the MOSFET is off during power-up or when the driver is unpowered. For the MOSFET selection, I'd look for a logic-level device with a low threshold voltage (Vgs(th) around 1–2V) and low Rds(on) at 2.5V or 3V gate drive, since the charge pump output might sag under load. I'd also verify the MOSFET's safe operating area (SOA) for the inrush current at turn-on — if the load has significant capacitance, I might need a soft-start circuit to limit inrush. Finally, I'd consider the reverse battery protection: if the battery can be inserted backwards, I'd need to ensure the gate drive circuit doesn't forward-bias the MOSFET's body diode.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle the inrush current when the load has a large bulk capacitor?
- What are the trade-offs between using a charge pump versus a dedicated gate driver IC with integrated charge pump?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a ferrite bead for a power rail that supplies both a sensitive analog sensor (drawing 10mA) and a digital microcontroller (drawing 50mA with 10mA transient spikes at 1 MHz)?

**Answer:** This is a common mixed-signal power distribution problem. The ferrite bead needs to isolate the analog sensor from the digital noise while not starving either load. I'd start by defining the requirements: the analog sensor needs a clean supply with minimal voltage ripple and noise, while the microcontroller's 10mA transient spikes at 1 MHz are the noise source I'm trying to attenuate. First, I'd check the DC resistance (DCR) of the ferrite bead — at 60mA total current, a DCR of even 0.5Ω would drop 30mV, which might be acceptable or not depending on the analog sensor's supply tolerance. I'd look for a ferrite bead with DCR under 0.2Ω to keep the voltage drop under 12mV. Next, I'd examine the impedance-versus-frequency curve. At 1 MHz, I want the impedance to be high enough to attenuate the transient spikes — typically I'd want at least 100–300Ω at 1 MHz. But I also need to check the impedance at lower frequencies (100 kHz or below) to make sure I'm not creating a resonance with the decoupling capacitors. The ferrite bead and the bulk capacitor form an LC low-pass filter, and I need to ensure the resonant frequency is well below the noise frequency and that there's enough damping to prevent peaking. I'd also consider the current rating — the ferrite bead must handle the 60mA continuous plus the 10mA transients without saturating, since saturation reduces the impedance. I'd place the ferrite bead in series with the analog sensor's supply, with a bulk capacitor (10–47µF) on the input side and a smaller ceramic capacitor (0.1–1µF) on the output side, close to the sensor. I'd also verify the ferrite bead's impedance doesn't drop significantly at the operating temperature, since some ferrite materials have strong temperature dependence. Finally, I'd simulate or measure the actual attenuation at 1 MHz to confirm the design meets the sensor's noise requirements.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine the acceptable voltage drop across the ferrite bead for the analog sensor?
- What happens if the ferrite bead saturates at the transient current — how would you detect and mitigate that?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review, the firmware lead argues that the ADC sampling rate should be increased from 100 Hz to 1 kHz to improve measurement resolution through oversampling. You believe the higher rate will alias 50/60 Hz noise into the passband and require a much steeper anti-aliasing filter, increasing component count and board area. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a technical trade-off that needs to be resolved through data and analysis, not through positional authority. First, I'd acknowledge the firmware lead's valid point — oversampling can improve effective resolution through averaging, and a higher sampling rate does give more flexibility in digital filtering. Then I'd lay out my specific concerns: at 1 kHz sampling, any noise above 500 Hz would alias into the passband, and 50/60 Hz mains noise and its harmonics are a real concern in medical devices. The current 100 Hz sampling rate with a simple first-order RC filter at 50 Hz is adequate because the anti-aliasing requirements are modest. At 1 kHz, I'd need a much steeper filter — likely a 2nd or 3rd order active filter — which adds components, board area, cost, and potential noise sources. I'd propose a structured evaluation: let's quantify the actual noise spectrum at the ADC input, measure the current noise floor, and simulate what oversampling would actually buy us. If the noise is predominantly white, oversampling gives a real benefit; if it's dominated by 50/60 Hz and its harmonics, oversampling won't help and might make things worse. I'd suggest we run a quick experiment — sample at both rates with the current hardware and compare the effective resolution and noise performance. I'd also ask the firmware lead to clarify the specific measurement accuracy requirement they're trying to meet — maybe there's a different way to achieve it, like better analog filtering or a higher-resolution ADC. If we still disagree after the analysis, I'd escalate to the project manager with a clear summary of both positions, the data we've collected, and a recommendation based on the technical evidence. The key is to keep the discussion focused on what's best for the product, not on who's right.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What if the firmware lead's oversampling approach actually does improve resolution in your testing — how would you reconcile that with your concerns about aliasing?
- How would you involve other team members (e.g., systems engineering, regulatory) in this decision?
