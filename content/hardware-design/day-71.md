# hardware-design — Day 71

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor value and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?

**Answer:** Start by budgeting the error and the power dissipation together, because they trade against each other. A larger shunt gives a bigger differential signal at the amplifier input, which improves signal-to-noise and reduces the relative impact of the amplifier's offset and gain error — but it dissipates more power (P = I²R) and drops more voltage in the load path. For a 1A full-scale current, a shunt in the 10–50 mΩ range is a reasonable starting window: at 50 mΩ you dissipate 50 mW at full scale and develop 50 mV, which is comfortably above the offset floor of most current-sense amplifiers; at 10 mΩ you dissipate only 10 mW but develop just 10 mV, so the amplifier's input offset voltage becomes a much larger fraction of full scale.

For the ±2% over-temperature budget, decompose the error: shunt tolerance (use a low-TCR metal-element or four-terminal shunt, since a thick-film part's TCR alone can blow the budget), amplifier gain error, input offset voltage and its drift, and CMRR error from the common-mode voltage the shunt sits at. A shunt with 50 ppm/°C TCR and 0.5% initial tolerance leaves room for the amplifier. Choose a current-sense amplifier with low input offset (tens of µV, not millivolts), because offset referred to a 10–50 mV full-scale signal is directly a percentage error. For bidirectional measurement you need a topology that can handle the common-mode range spanning both polarities of load current — either a dedicated bidirectional current-sense amplifier with a reference pin to set the zero-current output at mid-scale, or a difference amplifier with a well-matched resistor network. Matched on-chip resistor networks are strongly preferred over discrete resistors because CMRR depends on resistor matching, and discrete 0.1% resistors give poor CMRR that drifts with temperature.

Also consider where the shunt sits: high-side sensing keeps the ground reference clean but demands high common-mode range and good CMRR; low-side sensing is simpler and cheaper but breaks the load's ground reference, which can be unacceptable if the motor shares a ground with sensitive analog circuitry. Finally, Kelvin-connect the shunt (separate force and sense traces) so solder-joint and trace resistance don't add to the measured value, and verify the amplifier's bandwidth is adequate for the motor's current-transient content, not just the DC accuracy.

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where an op-amp's output is correct at DC but shows a slow, large-amplitude drift over minutes when the board is warmed by nearby power components?

**Answer:** A slow drift over minutes, correlated with board warm-up, points to a thermal effect rather than a noise or stability problem — so the first move is to confirm the correlation and localize the heat source. Use a thermal camera or a thermocouple to map which components heat up and how the drift tracks temperature. If the drift begins when a specific regulator or power device warms, that's the prime suspect.

Then separate the possible mechanisms. First, the op-amp's own input offset voltage drift: offset voltage has a temperature coefficient, and if the op-amp is physically near a heat source, its die temperature rises and the offset shifts. Check whether the drift direction and magnitude are consistent with the datasheet's offset drift spec over the observed temperature rise. Second, thermoelectric (Seebeck) effects at solder joints and component terminations: dissimilar metals at a junction with a temperature gradient generate small voltages, which is why layout symmetry and avoiding thermal gradients across the input pair matter. Third, resistor drift: if the gain-setting resistors have poor TCR or are mismatched in temperature (one near the heat source, one not), the gain changes with warm-up. Fourth, input bias current drift interacting with source impedance — if the source impedance is high, bias-current drift times that impedance produces an offset drift.

To isolate: cool individual components with freeze spray or a directed air stream and see if the drift reverses; swap the op-amp for a low-drift part to see if the magnitude changes; measure the actual temperature at the op-amp package versus the resistors. If the drift is dominated by the op-amp's offset drift, the fix may be a chopper-stabilized or zero-drift amplifier, or simply relocating the op-amp away from the heat source and adding thermal relief. If it's thermoelectric, fix the layout symmetry. If it's resistor drift, move to matched low-TCR resistors or a matched network. The key is to attribute the drift to a specific mechanism before changing parts, otherwise you may swap components without fixing the root cause.

- How would you distinguish offset-voltage drift from thermoelectric EMF experimentally, given both produce a slow DC shift?
- What layout practices reduce thermal gradients across a precision analog front-end?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting between a crowbar protection scheme and a clamping scheme for a low-voltage rail that feeds sensitive analog circuitry, and what would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision hinges on what the downstream circuitry can tolerate during a fault and what the protection is actually guarding against. A clamping scheme — typically a TVS diode, Zener, or active clamp — limits the rail voltage to a defined ceiling but lets the rail continue to operate at that clamped level during the event. A crowbar scheme deliberately short-circuits the rail (or triggers a latch/SCR) to force the upstream supply's overcurrent protection to trip or a fuse to blow, dropping the rail to near zero.

The first driver is the downstream circuitry's absolute maximum ratings. Sensitive analog parts often have absolute maximum supply voltages only slightly above nominal, so a clamp that merely limits the rail to, say, 5.5V on a 3.3V rail may still exceed the analog IC's abs-max and damage it. In that case a crowbar that removes the rail entirely is safer, because the analog circuitry sees no overvoltage at all — it just loses power. Conversely, if the downstream circuitry can survive a brief clamped overvoltage and the system needs to keep running through a transient, a clamp is preferable because it's non-destructive and self-recovering.

The second driver is the nature of the threat. For a transient overvoltage (ESD, inductive kick, hot-plug surge), a clamp is usually the right tool — it absorbs the transient energy and returns to normal. For a sustained overvoltage from a failed upstream regulator, a crowbar is more appropriate because a clamp would just sit there dissipating the fault energy continuously and could overheat. The third driver is recoverability: a crowbar typically requires a power cycle or a deliberate reset to recover, which may be acceptable or even desirable in a medical device where a fault should latch and require operator attention; a clamp recovers automatically, which is convenient but can mask an intermittent fault.

Practical considerations: a crowbar must be coordinated with the upstream supply's current limit and any fuse so the fault current is interrupted before the crowbar device itself is destroyed; the crowbar device's surge rating must exceed the available fault energy. A clamp must be selected so its standoff voltage is above the normal rail and its clamping voltage is below the downstream abs-max, and its capacitance and leakage must not disturb the analog rail. In many designs the answer is a combination: a TVS clamp for fast transients plus a crowbar or overvoltage-disconnect for sustained faults.

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?

**Answer:** The goal of a hardware-based POST is to verify that the analog signal chain is actually functional and within tolerance, using means that don't depend on the very firmware and ADC that the chain feeds — otherwise a fault in the ADC or firmware could mask a fault in the analog path, or vice versa. So I'd want the test to inject a known stimulus and check the response through an independent path, or to check reference and rail conditions with dedicated hardware monitors.

Concretely, I'd want the POST to verify several things. First, that the supply rails are within tolerance — using dedicated rail-monitor ICs or comparators with resistor dividers, not the ADC, so a dead ADC doesn't hide a dead rail. Second, that the voltage reference is present and at the correct value — again with a comparator against a divided-down known reference, or by checking the reference against a second independent reference. Third, that the analog front-end has gain and offset within expected bounds: inject a known test signal (from a DAC, a switched reference, or a precision divider) at the front-end input and check that the output lands in an expected window, using a comparator or window detector rather than relying solely on the ADC reading. Fourth, that key nodes aren't stuck at a rail or floating — a window comparator on the front-end output can catch a saturated or open-circuit condition.

The independence requirement is the crux. If the POST uses the ADC to read the front-end output, then a failed ADC could report "all good" while the front-end is dead, or a failed front-end could be masked by an ADC that happens to read mid-scale. So the strongest POST uses comparators and window detectors that produce a pass/fail logic signal directly, and that signal feeds into a hardware fault path (a latch or a supervisor) that can inhibit therapy or flag the fault regardless of firmware state. The firmware can read the POST result for logging and user display, but the pass/fail decision and any safety-relevant action should be hardware-verifiable.

I'd also want the POST to be self-checking: the test circuitry itself should be verifiable, for example by having the comparator's reference switchable so the test can confirm the comparator can detect both a pass and a fail condition. And I'd want the POST to run at power-up before therapy is enabled, with the result latched so a transient pass can't be faked later.

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements question first, not a component-argument question, because the disagreement is really about the intended use and the maintenance model, and both of us are making assumptions about that. So the first step is to get the actual use case and reliability requirements on the table: what does the product requirements document or the risk analysis say about how often the cable is expected to be connected and disconnected, whether field replacement is a supported scenario, and what the device's expected service life is. If the requirements genuinely say "connect once, leave in place," then the reliability engineer's concern may be about an unstated scenario — field service, reprocessing, or user error — that isn't captured in the requirements. If the requirements are silent or ambiguous, that's the real problem to fix, and it should be resolved with the systems and clinical stakeholders, not by the two of us picking a connector.

Once the use case is clear, I'd quantify it. Mating cycles are a specified parameter; if the expected number of cycles over the service life is well within the current connector's rating with margin, the current connector is defensible. If the expected cycles approach or exceed the rating — especially if field replacement is a real scenario — then the reliability engineer is right and the higher-cycle connector is justified. I'd want to see the numbers, not just the concern, and I'd want the reliability engineer to show the cycle-count estimate and its basis.

I'd also consider the risk-management angle: if there's genuine uncertainty about the use case, the conservative choice may be the higher-cycle connector, because the cost of a field failure (patient cable disconnecting mid-use, or a connector that won't mate reliably after replacement) is much higher than the incremental connector cost. In a medical device, the asymmetry of consequences usually argues for the more robust option when the requirement is uncertain. But I wouldn't just concede on cost grounds without understanding the trade — the higher-cycle connector may have other implications (size, mating force, cost, availability) that matter too.

The way I'd handle the disagreement itself: acknowledge that the reliability engineer is raising a legitimate concern, agree that the requirements need to be pinned down, and propose that we jointly take the question to the systems/clinical owner to confirm the intended use and field-replacement model. If the requirements confirm "connect once," I'd document that assumption and the connector's margin against it; if they confirm field replacement, I'd accept the higher-cycle connector. Either way the decision is traceable to a requirement rather than to who argued harder, which is what matters for the design history file.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the requirements are genuinely ambiguous and the schedule doesn't allow waiting for clarification, how would you decide?
- How would you document this decision so it's defensible in a design review or audit?
