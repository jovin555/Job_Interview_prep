# hardware-design — Day 118

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the switching frequency for a buck converter that must supply a sensitive analog rail, where the output ripple must stay below a few millivolts and the converter sits close to the analog front-end on the same board?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a multi-constraint optimization rather than a single-number choice. The first constraint is ripple: higher switching frequency shrinks the required inductance and output capacitance for a given ripple, which helps meet a tight millivolt-level target in a small area. But the second constraint pulls the other way — the converter's switching node is a broadband EMI source, and if it sits near the analog front-end, pushing the fundamental higher can actually make it easier to filter out of the signal band, provided the harmonics don't land where the front-end is sensitive. So I'd first map the analog front-end's bandwidth and any known sensitive frequencies (e.g., the ADC's modulator rate or a signal of interest), then choose a switching frequency whose fundamental and low-order harmonics fall outside that band and can be attenuated by a modest input/output filter.

I'd also weigh efficiency and thermal headroom: higher frequency means higher switching losses, which matters in a sealed enclosure. And I'd check whether the converter's control loop stays stable at the chosen frequency with the small inductor and capacitor values needed for low ripple — very high frequency can push the loop crossover up and make layout parasitics matter more. Finally, I'd consider spread-spectrum or synchronization options if the part supports them, since dithering the switching frequency smears the EMI spectrum and can reduce peak emissions at the cost of slightly higher output ripple. The decision is really: pick the lowest frequency that keeps the passive components small enough for the space, then verify on the bench that the ripple and the conducted/radiated emissions at the analog front-end both meet budget.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify on the bench that the chosen frequency isn't coupling into the ADC's conversion results? What would make you reconsider the frequency after the first prototype?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a high-resolution ADC, and which datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The starting point is the ADC's resolution and the error budget. For a high-resolution converter, the reference is effectively in series with every measurement, so its initial accuracy, temperature drift, and long-term stability all fold directly into the system error. A filtered supply rail can look adequate at room temperature on a bench, but its drift over temperature and its noise over frequency are usually not specified tightly enough to guarantee a resolution-level error budget across the operating range — so for anything where the reference error must stay within a fraction of an LSB, a dedicated reference IC is the safer default.

The datasheet parameters I'd actually weigh: initial accuracy (in % or LSB), temperature coefficient (ppm/°C — this usually dominates over the operating range), long-term drift, output noise (both low-frequency 0.1–10 Hz and broadband, since the ADC's input referred noise budget includes the reference), and the reference's output impedance and load regulation, because the ADC's reference input draws switched charge and the reference must recover between conversions. I'd also check the reference's dropout headroom against the supply and its quiescent current if the device is battery-powered. A filtered rail only wins when the ADC's effective resolution requirement is modest, the supply is already very clean, and board area or cost is the dominant constraint — and even then I'd want to characterize the rail's noise and drift rather than assume.

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating conduction loss from switching loss, because "gate voltage looks correct" only rules out one failure mode. First I'd measure the actual drain-source voltage across the MOSFET while it's on and multiply by the load current — that gives the real conduction loss, and comparing it against the datasheet's Rds(on) at the actual junction temperature tells me whether the part is behaving as expected or whether Rds(on) is higher than assumed (which happens if the gate drive is marginal, the part is a counterfeit or wrong bin, or the junction is already hot and Rds(on) has risen with temperature — a positive feedback loop).

If conduction loss looks right, I'd look at switching: with a resistive load and a slow or weak gate drive, the device can spend significant time in the linear region during each transition, and at a high PWM frequency that adds up. I'd scope the gate voltage and drain voltage together to see the crossover time, and check the gate resistor value and the driver's source/sink capability. I'd also check for oscillation — a MOSFET can oscillate at the gate or drain if the layout has excessive parasitic inductance, and that shows up as heat without an obvious current anomaly. Finally, I'd verify the thermal path: junction-to-ambient thermal resistance, copper area, and whether the part is actually soldered down well. The fix depends on which of these dominates — better gate drive, a lower-Rds(on) part, a snubber, or more copper.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish gate oscillation from legitimate switching with a scope probe? What would you check first if the part is hot only at high duty cycle?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?

**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware POST is to prove the analog signal chain is alive and within tolerance before the firmware trusts any measurement — so it has to inject a known stimulus or check a known reference and compare against hardware thresholds, not rely on the ADC and firmware to judge themselves. I'd structure it around a few independent checks: a reference check that confirms the voltage reference is within a window (using a comparator against a divided-down bandgap or a separate reference), a rail monitor that confirms each supply is above its minimum, and a signal-path check that switches a known calibration source or a precision resistor divider into the front-end and verifies the output lands in an expected window.

The key design principle is independence: the checks should use comparators or window detectors that don't depend on the ADC or the main processor, so a failed ADC or hung firmware can't mask a failed front-end. I'd also make the POST results latch into a status register or a hardware fault line that the firmware must read and clear deliberately, so a transient failure during POST is captured rather than lost. On the medical side, I'd want the POST to fail safe — if a check doesn't complete or the result is ambiguous, the device should go to a safe state rather than assume pass. And I'd document the coverage: which nodes are checked, what the pass windows are, and what fault each check is intended to catch, so it can be traced to the risk analysis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you avoid the POST itself injecting noise or disturbing the patient-connected front-end? How would you verify the POST actually catches a fault during design verification?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the firmware lead argues that the ADC sampling rate should be increased from 100 Hz to 1 kHz to improve measurement resolution through oversampling. You believe the higher rate will alias 50/60 Hz noise into the passband and require a much steeper anti-aliasing filter, increasing component count and board area. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd start by making sure we're actually disagreeing about the same thing — oversampling only improves resolution if the noise is white and the signal is band-limited, and in a medical front-end the dominant noise is often mains interference and low-frequency drift, which oversampling doesn't average away. So I'd ask the firmware lead to walk me through the noise model they're assuming and what resolution improvement they expect, then compare it against the front-end's actual noise floor and the ADC's ENOB. If the front-end noise already dominates, more samples won't buy resolution — they'll just digitize the same noise faster.

Then I'd bring data rather than opinion: a quick bench measurement of the front-end output spectrum with the input quiet, showing where the 50/60 Hz energy sits and what the anti-aliasing filter's roll-off looks like at the proposed 1 kHz Nyquist. If the higher rate really does fold mains noise into the band of interest, that's a concrete, shared fact we can design around. I'd also lay out the cost of the steeper filter — component count, board area, and the schedule impact — so the trade-off is visible to the whole team, not just asserted. The goal is to converge on a sampling rate and filter that meet the measurement requirement with margin, and if we can't agree, escalate to the systems lead with the data so the decision is made on evidence. I'd keep the tone collaborative — the firmware lead's instinct to improve resolution is reasonable; the question is whether the mechanism works for this noise environment.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the bench data showed the higher rate actually helped? How would you document the final decision so it's traceable in the design history file?
