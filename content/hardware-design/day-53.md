# hardware-design — Day 53

## Q1: How would you approach designing a hardware-based inrush current limiting circuit for a device that plugs into a live backplane connector, where the input capacitance is large enough that the initial charging surge could trip the upstream supply's overcurrent protection?

**Answer:** The core problem is that an uncharged bulk capacitance looks like a short at the instant of connection, so the inrush current is limited only by the source impedance and the ESR of the caps — which is usually far more than the upstream supply wants to deliver. I'd start by estimating the worst-case surge: I = C × dV/dt, or more practically, the peak current the source can deliver into a near-short, and compare that against the upstream supply's current limit and any connector or trace ratings. Then I'd choose a limiting strategy based on the energy involved and the allowed startup time.

For modest capacitance and a few amps, a simple NTC thermistor or a fixed series resistor with a bypass relay/MOSFET works, but the NTC has a self-heating recovery problem — after a hot unplug/replug it's still warm and no longer limits. For anything where reliability matters, I'd prefer an active approach: a series pass MOSFET with a controlled gate ramp, so the device sees a defined dV/dt and the current is set by C × dV/dt rather than by a resistor. A dedicated hot-swap controller IC handles this well — it manages the gate ramp, provides a programmable current limit, and often includes a timer to latch off if the load doesn't come up in time. The trade-off is complexity and the need to size the MOSFET for the safe operating area during the ramp, since it's dissipating real power while the caps charge.

Key trade-offs: a resistor-based limiter is cheap and simple but wastes steady-state power and slows startup; an active limiter is efficient and repeatable but needs careful SOA analysis and a gate drive that can't be defeated by a slow-rising input. I'd also add a bleeder resistor across the bulk cap so a quick disconnect/reconnect doesn't start from a partially charged state, and I'd verify the whole thing at both hot and cold extremes, since MOSFET Rds(on) and cap ESR both shift with temperature.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the series MOSFET's SOA for the ramp, and what would you do if the ramp time required to stay within SOA conflicts with the system's startup-time budget? What happens if the input voltage rises very slowly — does your limiter still behave predictably?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting and placing a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?

**Answer:** I'd start from the accuracy budget the application actually needs, because that drives everything else. If the requirement is loose — say, a few hundred ppm over temperature — a standard AT-cut crystal with a modest load-capacitance spec is fine. If it's tight — tens of ppm or better — I'd be looking at a TCXO or at least a crystal with a specified temperature coefficient and a matching load-capacitance tolerance, and I'd account for the fact that the total error is the sum of initial tolerance, temperature drift, aging, and the load-capacitance-induced pull.

The load capacitance is where most designs go wrong. The crystal is specified for a particular CL, and the actual CL seen by the crystal is the series combination of the two load caps plus the stray capacitance of the PCB traces and the MCU pin. If I pick 12 pF caps for a crystal specified at 12 pF CL, the actual CL is higher because of strays, and the oscillator runs fast. So I'd compute the load caps from the specified CL minus the estimated stray (typically a few pF), and I'd keep the traces short and symmetric to keep the two sides balanced.

On the bench I'd verify three things: startup margin, frequency accuracy, and drive level. Startup margin is the one people skip — I'd check that the oscillator starts reliably at the cold and hot temperature extremes and at the low end of the supply voltage, because negative-resistance margin degrades with temperature and supply. Frequency accuracy I'd measure against a known-good reference, ideally at temperature if I have a chamber, and I'd compare against the crystal's specified pull range. Drive level matters because over-driving the crystal causes aging and can crack it; I'd measure the drive current or voltage across the crystal and confirm it's within the manufacturer's rating. If any of these are marginal, the fix is usually a different crystal, a different CL, or a different oscillator topology — not a bodge on the board.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative-resistance margin without a chamber? What would you do if the crystal starts reliably at room temperature but fails at cold — what are the likely causes and how would you distinguish them?

## Q3: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The decision usually comes down to what the downstream logic needs and how fast the decision has to be made. A comparator is purpose-built for the job: it has a defined output that swings to logic levels, it's designed to be fast and to have low input offset, and it typically has hysteresis built in or easily added. An op-amp used as a comparator will work in a pinch, but it's a compromise — its output swing may not reach the logic thresholds cleanly, its slew rate and recovery from saturation are usually worse, and it may not have the same input common-mode range or offset performance.

So my default is a comparator for anything that's a genuine threshold decision, especially in a protection path where the response time matters. The exceptions are when the signal is slow and the threshold is loose, or when I already have a spare op-amp channel and adding a comparator would cost board space and a part number — in that case an op-amp can be acceptable if I add hysteresis externally and verify the output levels against the logic input thresholds.

The bigger design questions are around the threshold itself. I'd want to know the accuracy required over temperature, because that drives whether I need a precision reference or can use a resistor divider off the supply. I'd add hysteresis to prevent chatter when the input is near the threshold, and I'd size it so the hysteresis band is wider than the worst-case noise but narrower than the smallest legitimate signal excursion. For a protection path, I'd also think about fail-safe behavior: if the comparator loses power or its reference fails, does the output go to the safe state? That often means choosing the output polarity and pull-up so that a loss of supply or an open output results in the protected device being disabled, not enabled.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you add hysteresis to a comparator without degrading the threshold accuracy? What would you check in the comparator's datasheet to confirm it will meet the response-time requirement over temperature?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?

**Answer:** The fact that it's temperature-dependent and load-independent points away from a load-transient or layout issue and toward a parameter that shifts with temperature. I'd start by measuring the actual behavior at temperature — scope the switch node, the output, and the feedback pin, and look at whether the switching frequency is shifting, whether the duty cycle is changing, or whether the regulator is entering some kind of hiccup or burst mode. That tells me whether the control loop is misbehaving or whether a component is drifting out of spec.

The usual suspects are the output capacitor and the inductor. Electrolytic caps lose capacitance and gain ESR as they cool, but some ceramic dielectrics — particularly high-K types like Y5V — lose a large fraction of their capacitance as they heat, which raises the output ripple and can push the loop toward instability. If the output cap is a high-K ceramic, that's my first check: measure the actual capacitance at temperature, or swap in a stable dielectric like X7R or C0G and see if the behavior changes. The inductor is the other candidate: saturation current drops with temperature, so an inductor that's marginal at room temperature can saturate at elevated temperature, causing a sudden increase in ripple current and a drop in effective inductance. I'd check the inductor's saturation curve against the actual peak current.

Beyond the passives, I'd look at the feedback network and the reference. If the reference drifts with temperature, the output voltage shifts, which can interact with the load's behavior. And I'd check the compensation — if the loop is marginally stable at room temperature, a shift in the output cap's ESR or the inductor's value can push it over the edge. The fix is usually to replace the marginal passive with one that's specified over the full temperature range, or to re-tune the compensation for worst-case conditions rather than room-temperature nominal.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between an output-capacitance problem and an inductor-saturation problem on the bench? If the regulator uses internal compensation, what options do you have if the instability is temperature-driven?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the mechanical engineer proposes moving a temperature sensor from the PCB to a location closer to the patient-contact surface, arguing it will give a more accurate reading of the actual patient temperature. You're concerned that the new location is electrically noisy and mechanically difficult to route, and that the sensor's own self-heating and the thermal mass of the new location may actually degrade accuracy. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a measurement-accuracy question first, not a turf question, because the mechanical engineer's underlying goal — measuring the temperature the patient actually experiences — is the right goal. The disagreement is about whether the proposed location achieves it. So I'd want to get concrete about what "more accurate" means: what's the accuracy requirement, over what range, and what's the current error budget? If the current location already meets the requirement with margin, the change may not be worth the risk. If it doesn't, then we need to solve the problem, and the question becomes where and how.

I'd propose a joint evaluation rather than a debate. That means characterizing both locations — measuring the actual thermal response at the patient-contact surface versus at the PCB, including the time constant and the effect of self-heating, and checking the noise environment at the new location. If the mechanical engineer is right that the PCB location is thermally decoupled from the patient, that's a real finding and we should act on it. If the new location turns out to be noisy or slow to respond, that's also a real finding, and we can look for a third option — for example, keeping the sensor on the PCB but improving the thermal path to the patient surface, or using a remote sensor with a shielded, filtered cable.

The key is to keep the conversation on the data and the requirement, not on whose proposal wins. I'd also flag the regulatory angle early: if the sensor location is part of the device's validated design and the change affects the measurement, it may trigger re-testing or a design change under the quality system, so the decision has schedule and compliance implications that both of us need to weigh. If we can't agree on the data, I'd escalate to the systems lead or the design review board with the evidence, rather than let it stall.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the evaluation so it's fair to both proposals and produces a decision the team can stand behind? If the change is adopted and later fails verification, how would you handle the root-cause investigation?
