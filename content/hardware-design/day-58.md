# hardware-design — Day 58

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start with the power/accuracy trade-off on the shunt itself. At 1A full scale, a 100 mΩ shunt dissipates 100 mW and gives a 100 mV full-scale drop — comfortable for accuracy but wasteful in a battery-powered device; a 10 mΩ shunt dissipates only 10 mW and gives 10 mV, which now demands a low-offset amplifier and careful Kelvin sensing. Pick the largest shunt the thermal and headroom budget allows, then let the amplifier's offset and gain error fit inside the remaining error budget. For bidirectional current, a current-sense amplifier with a matched resistor network and a reference pin (so the output can be biased mid-rail and swing both ways) is usually cleaner than a discrete difference amplifier, because the internal resistor matching directly sets CMRR and gain drift. Check the datasheet's offset voltage *and* its drift over temperature, since a part with tight room-temperature offset but poor drift will blow the ±2% budget at temperature extremes. Use a four-terminal (Kelvin) connection to the shunt so solder-joint and trace resistance don't add to the sensed voltage, keep the sense traces matched and close together to reject magnetic pickup, and place the amplifier near the shunt to minimize loop area. Finally, verify the amplifier's common-mode input range covers the full bus voltage at both extremes of the PWM cycle, since motor drive is rarely a clean DC current.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would the topology change if the motor were driven by a high-side PWM bridge rather than a low-side switch? What would you do if the amplifier's common-mode range didn't quite cover the bus voltage during switching transients?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?
**Answer:** Temperature-dependent behavior with a fixed load points at a parameter that drifts — an electrolytic capacitor's ESR, a MOSFET's on-resistance and threshold, a diode's forward drop, or the controller's internal reference and oscillator. First, characterize the failure precisely: is the ripple frequency unchanged but amplitude growing (suggesting ESR or loop stability), or is the switching frequency itself shifting (suggesting the oscillator or a timing component)? Scope the switch node, the output, and the feedback pin simultaneously while heating with a hot-air tool, and watch which node moves first. Electrolytic output capacitors are a common culprit — ESR rises steeply at low temperature but capacitance and ESR both change with heat, and a marginal loop can go unstable as the zero moves. Check the inductor too: saturation current falls with temperature, so a part running close to its rating at room temperature can enter saturation when hot, producing a sudden ripple increase and effective dropout. Verify the feedback divider's temperature coefficient and the controller's reference drift against the datasheet, and confirm the compensation network was designed for the worst-case operating point rather than nominal. If the dropout is intermittent, look for thermal shutdown or current-limit hiccup engaging briefly. The fix is usually to re-derive the worst-case loop and derate the magnetics and capacitors for the hot corner, not to add bulk capacitance blindly.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish inductor saturation from loop instability on the scope? What bench setup would let you sweep temperature while capturing the switch node and output simultaneously?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a hot-swap protection circuit for a medical device that receives power from an external supply through a connector, where the input voltage is 12V and the device can draw up to 3A steady-state?
**Answer:** The core problem is limiting the inrush into the input capacitance when the connector mates, without dropping too much voltage in steady state and without latching off spuriously. A dedicated hot-swap controller with an external N-channel MOSFET gives the most control: it ramps the gate to limit di/dt, monitors the sense resistor for overcurrent, and can latch off on a fault. Size the MOSFET for the safe operating area during the ramp — the device spends real time in the linear region, so SOA at the expected ramp duration matters more than Rds(on). Set the current limit above the 3A steady-state with margin for transients, but low enough to protect the upstream supply, and add a timer so a sustained overcurrent latches off rather than hiccupping indefinitely. Include reverse-polarity protection (either a series MOSFET or an ideal-diode controller) and a TVS at the connector for surge, plus a bulk capacitor sized so the ramp doesn't sag the rail below the downstream converters' UVLO. For a medical device, also consider whether the hot-swap controller should report fault status to the host so the system can enter a safe state, and whether the latch should require a deliberate reset. Verify the design by hot-plugging into a live supply repeatedly and scoping the input current and rail voltage.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you choose between a P-channel and N-channel MOSFET for the pass element here? What would change if the input could also be back-fed from a battery?

## Q4: How would you approach choosing between a comparator and an op-amp for a threshold-detection function in a hardware protection circuit, and what would drive the decision?
**Answer:** The decision usually comes down to speed, hysteresis, and output interface rather than raw gain. A comparator is purpose-built for the job: it has a fast, well-defined propagation delay, an output stage that swings to a logic level (or is open-drain for level shifting), and internal hysteresis or a dedicated pin to set it. An op-amp used as a comparator has unpredictable saturation recovery, no guaranteed logic-level output, and its input stage may phase-invert when overdriven — all of which are dangerous in a protection path where the output must be deterministic. So for a protection threshold, default to a comparator unless there's a specific reason not to. The reasons to consider an op-amp instead are usually cost, board space, or the need for a linear response rather than a hard decision — but if the function is genuinely a threshold, that's a comparator's job. When specifying the comparator, check input offset voltage and drift (they set the threshold accuracy), propagation delay (it sets the response time), input common-mode range (it must include the threshold point, not just the rails), and whether the output needs hysteresis to avoid chatter on a slow-moving input. Add external hysteresis with positive feedback if the internal hysteresis isn't enough, and verify the threshold over temperature, since the reference and the comparator's offset both drift.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you calculate the hysteresis window for a given input slew rate and noise amplitude? What would you check if the comparator's output chattered despite the hysteresis?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading the hardware design for a medical device, and a supplier notifies you that a component you've designed in — a regulator that's already qualified and in your design history file — is being discontinued, with a last-time-buy window that closes before your production ramp. How would you handle the situation?
**Answer:** First, quantify the exposure before reacting: how many units does the last-time-buy cover, what's the forecast uncertainty, and how long would the stock need to last? That tells you whether this is a bridge problem or a redesign problem. In parallel, start the technical evaluation of alternatives — but treat it as a change-control exercise, not a drop-in swap. Any replacement has to be re-qualified against the same requirements: electrical performance at the operating corners, thermal behavior, EMI, and the regulatory documentation that references the original part. If the device is already in a design history file, the change likely triggers a formal change order and possibly a re-test, so the schedule impact is real and should be surfaced to the project lead early rather than absorbed silently. Options to weigh: a last-time-buy to cover production plus service spares, a pin-compatible alternate from a second source, or a board respin with a new regulator. Each has a different cost, schedule, and regulatory burden. I'd bring the trade-off to the cross-functional team — manufacturing, regulatory, quality, and program management — with a recommendation, because the right answer depends on volumes and timelines I don't own alone. Throughout, I'd document the decision and the rationale, since a component obsolescence decision is exactly the kind of thing an auditor will ask about later.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide how much last-time-buy inventory to carry given forecast uncertainty? What would you do if the only pin-compatible alternate had a different output capacitor requirement?
