# hardware-design — Day 119

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the input capacitor for a switching regulator, and what parameters would you check to make sure it actually does its job across the full operating range?

**Answer:** The input capacitor's job is to supply the high-frequency pulse current the converter draws during the switch on-time, so the upstream source doesn't see that ripple and the input voltage doesn't sag. I'd start by calculating the RMS ripple current the capacitor must handle — for a buck it's roughly I_out·√(D(1−D)), which peaks at 50% duty cycle — and choose a capacitor whose ripple-current rating at the actual operating temperature exceeds that with margin. Capacitance value matters less than people think for the high-frequency loop; what matters is the impedance at the switching frequency and the ESR/ESL. I'd pick a ceramic (X5R/X7R) with low ESR, check the DC-bias derating curve because a 10 µF X5R at 12 V might only be 4–5 µF effective, and verify the self-resonant frequency is above the switching frequency so it behaves capacitively where it needs to. I'd also check the temperature coefficient and aging, and confirm the voltage rating has margin — derating to ~50–80% of rated voltage is common practice for ceramics to limit DC-bias loss and improve reliability. Finally, layout matters as much as part selection: the input cap must be physically close to the high-side switch and the ground return, minimizing the loop area, or the parasitic inductance swamps the capacitor's benefit. On the bench I'd verify by measuring input voltage ripple with a short-ground-tip probe right at the cap and checking it against the converter's ripple spec.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between one large ceramic and several smaller ones in parallel? What would you do if the input ripple still exceeds spec after the capacitor is correctly sized and placed?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a linear regulator and a switching regulator for a low-current analog rail in a medical device, and what factors would drive the decision beyond efficiency?

**Answer:** Efficiency is usually the least interesting factor for a low-current analog rail — if the rail draws tens of milliamps, the absolute dissipation is small and a linear regulator's simplicity often wins. The real drivers are noise, PSRR, and the downstream sensitivity. A linear regulator has no switching artifacts, so its output noise is dominated by the reference and error amplifier — typically tens of µV RMS — and its PSRR rolls off with frequency but is excellent at low frequency, which is exactly where a switching regulator's ripple and low-frequency control-loop noise live. For a sensitive analog front-end, an LDO fed from a switching rail is a common and effective architecture: the switcher handles the voltage conversion efficiently, and the LDO cleans up the residual ripple. The trade-offs I'd weigh: dropout voltage and headroom (an LDO needs the input to stay above V_out plus dropout across the full load and temperature range, which constrains the upstream rail), thermal dissipation in a sealed enclosure (P = (Vin − Vout)·Iout, and in a sealed medical device that heat has nowhere to go), transient response (LDOs are generally slower to respond to fast load steps than a well-compensated switcher, though for a steady analog load that's rarely an issue), and board area and cost. I'd also consider whether the rail needs to be sequenced or tracked relative to others, since LDOs are easy to sequence with an enable pin. The decision usually comes down to: is the load sensitive enough that switching noise is a real risk, and is the dissipation low enough that a linear pass element is thermally viable? If both answers are yes, linear is the lower-risk choice.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you evaluate whether an LDO's PSRR is sufficient to clean up a specific switching regulator's ripple? What would you check on the bench to confirm the analog rail is quiet enough?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting and verifying the load capacitance for a crystal oscillator, and what would you check on the bench to confirm the circuit has adequate startup margin across temperature?

**Answer:** The crystal datasheet specifies a load capacitance C_L, and the two external capacitors plus stray capacitance must present that value to the crystal — roughly C1 = C2 = 2(C_L − C_stray), where C_stray is the PCB and pin capacitance, typically a few pF. Getting this wrong shifts the oscillation frequency and, more importantly, affects the negative resistance margin. I'd start from the datasheet C_L, estimate stray capacitance from the layout (short traces, ground guard, minimal parasitics), and pick standard capacitor values that land close to the target. Then I'd verify on the bench rather than trusting the calculation: measure the actual oscillation frequency with a frequency counter and compare against the crystal's specified tolerance at the load capacitance — if it's off, the effective load capacitance is wrong. For startup margin, the standard approach is the negative-resistance test: inject a variable series resistance into the crystal path and increase it until oscillation stops; the circuit should still start with at least 3–5× the crystal's specified maximum ESR (the "negative resistance" should be several times the crystal's ESR). I'd repeat this at the temperature extremes, because crystal ESR rises and drive level margin shrinks at cold, and startup is usually worst at the coldest specified temperature. I'd also check drive level with a current probe or by measuring the voltage across the crystal and computing power, to make sure it's within the crystal's rated drive level — too much drive ages the crystal, too little risks startup failure. Finally, I'd scope the startup waveform at power-on at each temperature corner and confirm it starts cleanly and settles within the expected time, with no evidence of spurious modes or slow-growth oscillation.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the circuit starts reliably at room temperature but fails at cold, and the load capacitance calculation looks correct? How does a parallel resistor across the crystal change the startup behavior, and when would you consider adding one?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a 2A resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the current is right and the gate voltage is right, the heat is coming from somewhere the steady-state calculation doesn't capture, so I'd work through the loss mechanisms systematically. First, conduction loss: P = I²·R_DS(on), but R_DS(on) rises with junction temperature — a MOSFET specified at 25°C might have 1.5–2× that resistance at 100°C, so I'd check the datasheet curve at the actual operating temperature and confirm the part was selected with that in mind. Second, switching loss: even a "resistive load" switched at PWM has transition losses during turn-on and turn-off, and if the gate drive is weak or the gate resistor is large, the device spends time in the linear region dissipating significant power. I'd scope V_DS and I_D simultaneously during switching and look at the crossover — if the transitions are slow, that's the culprit. Third, gate drive integrity: "10 V looks correct" on a multimeter doesn't mean the gate isn't ringing or that the driver can supply the peak current needed to charge the gate quickly; I'd scope the gate waveform at the device pin and look for slow edges, ringing, or a plateau that suggests the driver is current-limited or the gate resistor is too large. Fourth, thermal path: even correct dissipation can overheat the die if the thermal resistance from junction to ambient is worse than assumed — poor copper area, no thermal vias, a small package, or a hot neighbor. I'd measure case temperature and back-calculate junction temperature from the datasheet R_θJC and the actual power, and compare against the SOA and thermal derating curves. Fifth, check for shoot-through or body-diode conduction if there's any inductive component in the "resistive" load — wiring inductance alone can cause transient reverse conduction. The fix depends on which mechanism dominates: a lower R_DS(on) part, a stronger gate driver, a smaller gate resistor, better thermal copper, or a snubber to tame the switching node.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish conduction loss from switching loss with bench measurements alone? What would you check in the layout if the thermal calculations say the part should be running much cooler?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements question first, not a component argument, because the disagreement is really about the assumed use case — "connected once" versus "field-replaceable" — and that assumption should be documented somewhere. I'd ask the reliability engineer to walk me through the field-replacement scenario they're envisioning: is this a clinical workflow where the cable is routinely disconnected for cleaning, reprocessing, or replacement, or is it a failure-mode contingency? If it's a routine workflow, the mating-cycle requirement is real and my "connect once" assumption is wrong, and the higher-cycle connector is justified. If it's a contingency, the question becomes how many replacement cycles are plausible over the device life and whether the current connector's rating covers that with margin. I'd pull the actual numbers — the connector datasheet's mating-cycle rating, the device's expected service life, and the clinical workflow assumptions — and put them side by side rather than trading opinions. I'd also bring in the systems or clinical stakeholder who owns the use-case definition, because they can settle whether field replacement is in scope. If the data shows the current connector is marginal, I'd accept the change and manage the cost and schedule impact openly rather than defending the original choice. If the data shows it's adequate, I'd document the rationale and the assumed use case in the design record so the decision is traceable, and propose that the reliability engineer's concern be captured as a use-case assumption to validate. The key is to resolve it on requirements and data, not on whose component preference wins, and to make sure whichever way it goes, the assumption is written down and agreed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the clinical stakeholder couldn't give a definitive answer on whether field replacement is in scope? What would you do if the higher-cycle connector also had a longer lead time that threatened the schedule?
