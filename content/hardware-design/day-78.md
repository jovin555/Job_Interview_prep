# hardware-design — Day 78

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor value and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start by treating the shunt and the amplifier as a single error budget, because the two decisions are coupled. The shunt value sets the full-scale sense voltage, and that voltage has to be large enough that the amplifier's offset voltage, offset drift, and input bias current don't eat the accuracy budget, but small enough that I²R dissipation stays acceptable. At 1A, a 100 mΩ shunt dissipates 100 mW at full scale and gives 100 mV of signal — a good middle ground. A 10 mΩ shunt only burns 10 mW but gives 10 mV full scale, which means a 1 mV offset already consumes 10% of the range; that's hard to hold to ±2% over temperature without a very good amplifier. So I'd pick the shunt value from the accuracy budget first, then confirm the power dissipation is within the resistor's rating with derating.

For topology: for bidirectional current I need a difference amplifier or a current-sense amplifier that supports a reference/bias pin so the output can be centered mid-rail at zero current. A simple high-side shunt with a difference amplifier works, but I'd check the common-mode input range — high-side sensing at the motor supply means the amplifier must tolerate that common-mode voltage, and its CMRR directly limits accuracy when the supply moves. A dedicated current-sense amplifier with matched internal gain resistors is usually better than a discrete op-amp difference amp because the resistor matching (and matching drift) dominates CMRR and gain error. If the motor is ground-referenced and the supply is high, low-side sensing is simpler and cheaper but breaks the ground reference and can inject noise into the ground plane — a real concern in a medical device with a sensitive analog front-end nearby.

Then I'd verify the error budget over temperature: shunt tolerance and tempco (a 0.5% / 50 ppm/°C part is a reasonable starting point), amplifier offset drift, gain error, and CMRR drift. I'd also check the amplifier's bandwidth and slew against the motor current's di/dt — if the current has fast edges, a slow amplifier will distort the waveform even if the DC accuracy is fine. Finally, layout: Kelvin (4-wire) connections to the shunt, symmetric routing to both amplifier inputs, and keep the sense traces away from the switching node.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between high-side and low-side sensing if the motor supply is also the analog supply? What would you do if the required accuracy were ±0.5% instead of ±2%?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The point of a hardware POST is to verify the analog signal chain using known, on-board references and switches, so that the result doesn't depend on the ADC or the firmware being correct. If the test itself relies on the ADC and firmware to interpret results, it can't catch a fault in either.

Concretely, I'd want the hardware to be able to: (1) inject a known reference voltage at the input of the analog front-end and check that the signal chain produces the expected output — this exercises the amplifier, filter, and ADC input; (2) verify the ADC's reference itself against an independent reference, or at least verify that the reference is present and within a window; (3) check supply rails with comparators or a supervisor IC, independent of the ADC; (4) verify that the sensor excitation (if any) is present and within tolerance; and (5) confirm continuity of the patient-cable path where that's feasible, since an open electrode is a common failure mode.

The design pattern is a small set of analog switches or a mux that can disconnect the real sensor and connect a known source, plus comparators or window detectors that produce a pass/fail signal the firmware can read as a GPIO. The firmware's job is then just to sequence the test and read the result — it isn't part of the measurement path being tested. I'd also want the test to be able to fail safe: if the POST can't confirm the signal chain is good, the device should refuse to enter a measurement mode rather than silently report bad data.

The trade-offs are board area, cost, and the risk that the test switches themselves become a failure point. I'd keep the injected signal simple — a divided-down reference is usually enough — and make sure the switches are fail-open or fail-safe depending on what the device does.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the POST itself is working, given that a stuck-at-pass fault would be invisible? What would you do if the POST adds too much board area for the product's form factor?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a switching regulator's output is stable at room temperature but shows increased ripple and occasional dropout as the ambient temperature rises, even though the load current is unchanged?
**Answer:** Temperature-dependent behavior with a constant load points at something whose parameters drift with temperature, so I'd work through the candidates systematically rather than swapping parts.

First, I'd separate "increased ripple" from "dropout" — they may have different causes. Increased ripple at temperature often comes from the output capacitor: electrolytics lose capacitance and gain ESR as they cool, but some ceramic dielectrics (especially high-K, Class II) lose capacitance as they heat, and if the output cap is already marginal, losing capacitance raises ripple. I'd measure the actual ripple waveform and its frequency content — if the ripple frequency is the switching frequency, it's likely output capacitance or ESR; if it's a subharmonic or a low-frequency envelope, it may be loop instability.

Dropout at temperature with constant load suggests the regulator is losing headroom or the control loop is degrading. Candidates: the inductor's DCR rises with temperature, increasing conduction loss and reducing available headroom; the current-limit threshold shifts with temperature; the feedback divider's tempco shifts the setpoint; or the compensation network's behavior changes because the output cap's ESR has moved. I'd also check whether the regulator is entering current limit or thermal foldback — some parts reduce current limit as die temperature rises, which would look like dropout.

The practical sequence: measure the switch node, the output, and the input at temperature; check the inductor current waveform for saturation or slope changes; verify the feedback node voltage against the expected setpoint; and compare the measured ripple against a simulation with the capacitor's temperature derating applied. If the loop is marginal, a small change in output cap ESR can push it into instability, which would explain both symptoms at once.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish output-capacitor ESR drift from loop instability as the root cause? What would you check in the compensation network if the regulator uses peak-current-mode control?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?
**Answer:** The starting point is the accuracy requirement over temperature, because that drives whether a standard crystal is adequate or whether I need a TCXO or a crystal with a specified temperature coefficient. A typical AT-cut crystal has a parabolic frequency-vs-temperature curve with a turnover point near 25°C, so the error at the temperature extremes is what matters — I'd get the crystal's temperature characteristic from the datasheet and check the worst-case deviation against the budget, including the initial tolerance, aging, and load-capacitance pulling.

Load capacitance is the next decision. The crystal's specified load capacitance (CL) must match the actual load the oscillator circuit presents, which is the series combination of the two load caps plus stray capacitance. If CL is wrong, the frequency is pulled off nominal, and the pull is temperature-dependent, so it compounds the error. I'd calculate the load caps from the crystal's CL and the MCU's pin capacitance, then verify on the bench by measuring the actual frequency and, if needed, trimming.

Drive level matters too: too little drive and the oscillator may not start reliably, especially at temperature extremes; too much and the crystal ages faster or is damaged. I'd check the crystal's drive-level spec against the oscillator's drive, and verify startup margin at the temperature extremes — cold startup is often the worst case because the oscillator's transconductance drops and the crystal's ESR rises.

On the bench I'd verify: startup time and reliability at the cold and hot extremes, frequency accuracy at several temperatures against a reference, and the oscillator's negative resistance margin (the standard test is to add series resistance until oscillation stops, then confirm the margin is several times the crystal's ESR). For a real-time-clock crystal at 32.768 kHz, the same logic applies but the drive levels are much lower and the startup margin is tighter, so I'd be especially careful there.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify negative resistance margin without a network analyzer? What would you do if the crystal's temperature drift alone exceeded the budget?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** The disagreement is really about an assumption, not about the connector — I assumed the cable is mated once and left in place, and the reliability engineer is assuming it may be replaced in the field. So the first thing I'd do is surface that assumption and get it resolved with data rather than argue about the part. I'd ask: what does the use case actually say? Is the cable a single-use disposable, a reusable accessory, or a permanently attached cable? What does the instructions-for-use say about replacement? If the IFU says the cable is not user-replaceable, my assumption holds and the reliability engineer's concern is about a scenario the product doesn't support. If the IFU allows field replacement, or if service procedures involve disconnecting the cable, then the reliability engineer is right and the connector needs to be rated for it.

If the assumption is genuinely ambiguous, I'd treat that as a requirements gap and escalate it to the product owner rather than resolve it unilaterally — because the answer affects not just the connector but the IFU, the service model, and possibly the regulatory submission. I'd also want to understand the cost delta and whether there's a middle option: a connector with a higher cycle rating that isn't the most expensive one, or a design change that makes the cable truly non-replaceable so the lower-cycle part is defensible.

Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative — the reliability engineer is raising a legitimate concern, and the right outcome is a documented decision with a rationale, not one of us "winning." If we can't agree, I'd propose we both bring the data to the design review and let the team decide, with the decision recorded in the design history file.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document the decision if the team decides the lower-cycle connector is acceptable? What would you do if the IFU were still being written and the replacement policy wasn't decided yet?
