# hardware-design — Day 84

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the input filter for a switching regulator, and how would you decide whether a simple capacitor is enough versus needing an LC or π-filter?
**Answer:** Start by understanding what the filter is actually protecting against. There are two distinct concerns: conducted emissions going back onto the input rail (which matters for EMC compliance and for keeping other loads on that rail clean), and the impedance the regulator itself sees at its input, which affects loop stability. A single bulk capacitor handles the low-frequency charge reservoir and the regulator's own switching-frequency current draw, but it does little for the higher-frequency harmonics that ride on the switching edges. Those are what a small series element — a ferrite bead or an inductor — combined with a second capacitor can attenuate.

The decision between a plain capacitor and an LC or π-filter comes down to the noise spectrum and the impedance environment. If the regulator is fed from a low-impedance source and the downstream loads are not sensitive, a well-placed bulk plus a small high-frequency capacitor may be sufficient. If the rail feeds a sensitive analog section, or if the input cable is long enough to act as an antenna, an LC filter is worth the board area. The key trade-off is that adding series inductance creates a resonant tank with the capacitors — you need to check the resonant frequency, the source and load impedances, and consider damping (a small series resistor or an RC snubber across the inductor) to prevent peaking. An undamped LC filter can actually make conducted emissions worse at the resonant frequency than no filter at all. I'd also verify that the filter's DC resistance doesn't drop the input voltage below the regulator's minimum at worst-case load, and that the inductor's saturation current comfortably exceeds the peak input current.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure whether the filter is actually doing its job — what would you look at on a scope or spectrum analyzer? What happens to the regulator's loop response if you add significant series impedance at its input?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently in the enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?
**Answer:** Intermittent, motion-correlated failures usually point to something mechanical or something that changes with physical position — a connector, a solder joint, a cable routing, or a component that's marginal and shifts with flex or vibration. I'd start by trying to reproduce the failure in a controlled way: flex the board, tap components, wiggle connectors, and see if I can correlate a specific action with the failure. If I can localize it to a region, that narrows the search considerably.

From there, I'd look at the usual suspects in order of likelihood. Connectors and board-to-board interfaces are common — a pin that's not fully seated, a crimp that's marginal, or a mating force that changes when the enclosure is assembled. Solder joints, especially on larger components or ones with thermal mass, can crack and make intermittent contact. Cable routing matters too: a cable that's fine on the bench may be pinched or strained once the enclosure is closed, and that strain can propagate to a connector or a trace. I'd also consider whether the enclosure itself changes the electrical environment — for example, a metal enclosure can detune an antenna or change the impedance of a nearby trace, and a plastic enclosure can trap heat that shifts a marginal component out of spec.

The diagnostic approach is to instrument the failure. If I can get a scope probe or a logic analyzer onto the suspect signals while the device is in the enclosure, I can see whether the failure is a power glitch, a signal integrity issue, or a digital communication error. If I can't probe inside the enclosure, I'd bring the board out but replicate the mechanical constraints — same cable routing, same mounting, same thermal environment — and see if the failure follows. Once I have a reproducible trigger, I can isolate the specific net or component. The fix is usually mechanical (strain relief, connector retention, reflow of a suspect joint) or a design change (rerouting a cable, adding a keep-out, changing a component to one with better tolerance).

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the root cause is mechanical or thermal if the failure only appears after the device has been running for a while? What would you do if the failure only reproduces in the field and never on the bench?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The purpose of a hardware-based POST is to verify that the analog signal chain is functional before the firmware starts trusting its readings. The key word is "independently" — if the self-test relies on the same ADC and the same firmware that will later be used for measurements, a fault in either could mask itself. So the hardware POST should exercise the chain through a separate path or a known stimulus.

A common approach is to inject a known reference signal at the front end — either a precision voltage derived from a dedicated reference, or a current injected into the sensor input through a calibrated resistor — and then verify that the signal appears at the ADC input within an expected window. This checks the amplifier, the filter, and the ADC input stage together. To make it independent of the ADC's own accuracy, you can compare against a comparator threshold rather than a full conversion: the comparator only needs to confirm that the signal is present and within a coarse band, which is enough to catch an open trace, a dead amplifier, or a shorted input. If you want to verify the ADC itself, you can use a separate DAC or a PWM-derived reference to generate a known ramp and check that the ADC's output code changes monotonically.

The design considerations are: the stimulus must be disconnected or disabled during normal operation so it doesn't affect measurements; the test must be fast enough not to delay startup unacceptably; and the pass/fail criteria must account for component tolerances, temperature, and supply variation so you don't get false failures. I'd also want the POST to be able to detect a stuck-at fault (input shorted to a rail) and an open-circuit fault (no signal), since those are the most common failure modes. Finally, the result should be latched or reported in a way that the firmware can act on — but the detection itself should not depend on the firmware being healthy.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the case where the POST passes but the device later drifts out of calibration? Would you run the POST periodically or only at startup, and why?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting between a linear regulator and a switching regulator for a low-current analog rail in a medical device, and what factors would drive the decision beyond efficiency?
**Answer:** Efficiency is usually the first thing people mention, but for a low-current analog rail it's often not the deciding factor. The real drivers are noise, PSRR, transient response, and the thermal and board-area budget.

A linear regulator's main advantage for analog is that it doesn't switch, so it doesn't inject switching harmonics into the rail. That matters when the downstream circuit is a high-resolution ADC or a low-noise amplifier, because those are sensitive to noise in the band of interest. A switching regulator, even a well-designed one, produces ripple at the switching frequency and its harmonics, and that ripple can couple into the analog signal path through the supply pins or through ground. You can filter it, but that adds components and board area, and the filter itself can have resonances.

The trade-off is heat. A linear regulator dissipates the voltage drop times the load current as heat. For a low-current rail — say tens of milliamps — that's manageable, and the thermal design is simple. For higher currents, the heat becomes a problem, and a switching regulator becomes attractive despite the noise. In a medical device, you also have to consider the enclosure: if the device is sealed and there's no airflow, a linear regulator's heat has nowhere to go, and that can push you toward switching even at moderate currents.

Other factors: a linear regulator is simpler, has fewer external components, and is easier to qualify for reliability. A switching regulator needs an inductor, capacitors, and careful layout, and the inductor can be a source of EMI. For a medical device, the regulatory burden of a switching regulator is higher because you have to demonstrate that its emissions don't affect other functions. So my default for a low-current analog rail is a linear regulator, possibly fed from a switching pre-regulator if the input voltage is high enough that the linear's dissipation would be excessive. The decision is really about where the heat goes and how much noise the downstream circuit can tolerate.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you evaluate the PSRR of the downstream analog circuit to decide how much supply noise it can tolerate? What would you do if the linear regulator's dropout voltage is too high for the available input?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen connector for the patient cable is not rated for the number of mating cycles the device will see over its lifetime, and proposes a more expensive, higher-cycle connector. You believe the current connector is adequate because the cable is intended to be connected once and left in place, but the reliability engineer is concerned about field replacement. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the factual question from the judgment question. The factual question is: what is the actual expected number of mating cycles in the field? That's not something either of us should assume — it depends on the use case, the clinical workflow, and the maintenance plan. If the device is used in a hospital where cables are routinely swapped between patients, the number could be high. If it's a home device where the cable is connected once and stays connected, it could be very low. I'd want to look at the use case and, if possible, get input from the clinical or marketing team who understand how the device is actually used.

The judgment question is: given that number, what's the right margin? Reliability engineers tend to design for worst-case field behavior, which is a reasonable instinct in a medical device. I'd want to understand the connector's rated cycle life and how it degrades — does it fail gradually (increasing contact resistance) or suddenly (loss of contact)? A gradual failure is easier to detect and less likely to cause a patient hazard. I'd also want to know whether the connector is user-replaceable or service-replaceable; if it's the latter, the cycle count is much lower.

If the data supports the reliability engineer's concern, I'd change the connector — the cost difference is usually small compared to the risk of a field failure in a medical device. If the data supports my position, I'd document the rationale clearly: the expected cycle count, the connector's rating, the margin, and the failure mode. I'd also propose a compromise if one exists — for example, a connector with a higher cycle rating but similar cost, or a design change that reduces the number of cycles (such as a strain relief that keeps the cable connected). The goal isn't to win the argument; it's to make a defensible decision that both of us can sign off on. If we still disagree after looking at the data, I'd escalate to the project lead or the design review board, because a disagreement about reliability in a medical device shouldn't be resolved by seniority.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the connector's cycle life if the datasheet rating is ambiguous or the vendor won't provide test data? What would you do if the higher-cycle connector isn't available in time for the production ramp?
