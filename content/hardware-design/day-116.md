# hardware-design — Day 116

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the input capacitor for a switching regulator, and what parameters would you check to make sure it actually does its job across the full operating range?

**Answer:** The input capacitor's job is to supply the high-frequency pulse current the converter draws so that current doesn't have to come all the way from the source through the trace inductance — that's what keeps input ripple and radiated EMI under control. I'd start from the RMS ripple current the converter will draw, which is roughly a function of output current and duty cycle, and pick a capacitor whose ripple-current rating exceeds that with margin at the expected ambient. Then I'd look at the effective capacitance under DC bias — a small ceramic in a small package can lose a large fraction of its nominal value at the applied voltage, so the datasheet's nominal number is not the number you get. ESR and ESL matter too: the self-resonant frequency tells you where the cap stops behaving like a capacitor, so for the high-frequency component I'd want a small, low-inductance package close to the switch node and the ground return, and I'd parallel a larger bulk cap for the lower-frequency component. Layout is part of the selection: the loop from input cap through the high-side switch and back to the cap's ground must be as small as possible, because trace inductance in that loop defeats the capacitor. Finally I'd verify on the bench — measure input ripple with a proper tip-and-barrel probe, and check the cap's temperature rise under worst-case load.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a single ceramic is enough or whether you need a bulk electrolytic in parallel? What would you check if the input ripple looks fine at room temperature but the converter misbehaves at cold?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a SAR ADC and a delta-sigma ADC for a medical device that measures a slowly varying physiological signal with high resolution, and what trade-offs would drive the decision?

**Answer:** The signal is slow, so bandwidth isn't the constraint — resolution, noise, and the cost of the analog front-end around the converter are. A delta-sigma converter is usually the natural fit for a slow, high-resolution physiological signal: it oversamples and shapes quantization noise out of the band of interest, so you can get very high effective resolution without an aggressive analog anti-alias filter, and many parts integrate a PGA and reference buffer that simplify the front-end. The trade-offs are latency and output data rate — the digital filter introduces group delay, which matters if the signal is used in a closed loop or for real-time alarms — plus higher power in some parts and the need to understand the filter's settling behavior when you switch channels. A SAR is the better choice when you need low latency, multi-channel multiplexing with fast settling, or a well-defined sample instant, and when the signal chain already has enough gain and filtering that 16–18 bits of SAR resolution is sufficient. I'd also weigh the reference and drive requirements: a SAR's sampling cap needs a fast, low-impedance driver, while a delta-sigma's input is more forgiving but its digital filter characteristics become part of the system design. The decision usually comes down to whether the system needs low latency and channel count (SAR) or maximum resolution with relaxed filtering (delta-sigma).

**Possible follow-ups:** How would the anti-aliasing filter requirement differ between the two? What would you check in the delta-sigma's digital filter datasheet before committing?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the current is right and the gate voltage is right, the dissipation should be predictable from I²R, so the first thing I'd do is check whether the part is actually in full enhancement or is spending time in the linear region. A gate voltage that "looks correct" on a DC multimeter can be misleading — I'd look at it with a scope at the switching edges to see whether the gate is actually reaching the plateau quickly, or whether the driver is weak, the gate resistor too large, or there's miller-induced slowdown that keeps the device in the linear region during each transition. If the load is switched at any meaningful frequency, switching loss can dominate conduction loss, and that shows up as heat with no DC current anomaly. I'd also verify the on-resistance at the actual junction temperature — RDS(on) rises with temperature, so a part that's fine cold can run away thermally if the thermal path is poor. Then I'd check the thermal design: package thermal resistance, copper area, whether the part is in a small package with no thermal vias, and whether it's sitting next to another heat source. Finally I'd rule out shoot-through or body-diode conduction if there's any inductive component, and check for gate ringing or overshoot that could be stressing the gate oxide. The systematic approach is to separate conduction loss, switching loss, and thermal-path problems, because each has a different fix.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish switching loss from a thermal-path problem experimentally? What would you change first if the gate waveform shows slow turn-off?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?

**Answer:** I'd start from the frequency tolerance budget the system actually needs, then split it across the crystal's initial tolerance, its temperature drift, aging, and the load-capacitance-induced pulling error. For a wide temperature range, an ordinary crystal's frequency shifts with temperature following its cut — an AT-cut has a turnover region, so the tolerance over the range is not symmetric around room temperature, and I'd read the crystal's temperature characteristic curve rather than assume a single ppm number. If the budget is tight, I'd consider a TCXO or a crystal with a specified tight temperature tolerance, and I'd make sure the load capacitance I design matches the crystal's specified CL — mismatch pulls the frequency and also affects startup. On the bench I'd verify startup margin across temperature and supply: check that the oscillator starts reliably at cold and hot extremes, measure the negative resistance margin (typically you want several times the crystal's ESR), and confirm the drive level is within the crystal's rating so it isn't over-driven. I'd also measure the actual frequency error over temperature against the budget, and check that the load caps are the values the design intended after accounting for PCB parasitics.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative resistance margin on the bench? What would you do if the crystal starts reliably but the frequency error exceeds budget at the cold extreme?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the firmware lead argues that the ADC sampling rate should be increased from 100 Hz to 1 kHz to improve measurement resolution through oversampling. You believe the higher rate will alias 50/60 Hz noise into the passband and require a much steeper anti-aliasing filter, increasing component count and board area. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a shared engineering problem rather than a win/lose argument, because both concerns are legitimate — oversampling genuinely can improve resolution, and aliasing is a real risk if the analog filter isn't matched to the new rate. First I'd make sure we're talking about the same signal chain: what the current anti-aliasing filter's stopband attenuation actually is at the new Nyquist frequency, and whether the firmware's oversampling and decimation would reject the aliased content or just fold it in. I'd bring data — a quick calculation or a bench measurement of the noise floor at both rates with a representative input — so the discussion is about the system, not about preferences. If the data shows the higher rate does alias mains hum into the band, I'd propose options: keep the higher rate but add or steepen the analog filter (with the cost and area implications stated), or keep the lower rate and get resolution through averaging that's properly synchronized to reject mains, or move to a converter architecture that oversamples internally. I'd want the decision documented with the trade-offs, and I'd loop in the systems or regulatory lead if the change affects a verified requirement. The goal is a decision the whole team understands and can defend in design history, not just my preferred answer.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the bench test to settle the question objectively? What would you do if the firmware lead's approach turns out to be correct and your concern was unfounded?
