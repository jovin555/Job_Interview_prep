# hardware-design — Day 123

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the output capacitor for a boost converter whose load is a wireless radio that draws short, high-current bursts, and what would you check to make sure the capacitor actually holds the rail within tolerance during a burst?

**Answer:** In a boost converter the output capacitor is doing two jobs at once: it supplies the load during the interval when the inductor is charging (the diode is off), and it absorbs the energy the inductor dumps when the switch opens. So the capacitor sees a large, discontinuous ripple current at the switching frequency, and its ESR and ESL matter as much as its capacitance. For a burst load I'd start by writing down the transient requirement: burst amplitude, burst duration, and the allowed voltage deviation. If the burst is short relative to the converter's loop bandwidth, the capacitor alone supplies the charge, so ΔV ≈ (I_burst × t_burst) / C plus the ESR step (I_burst × ESR). That immediately tells me whether I need bulk capacitance, low ESR, or both. I'd also check the capacitor's ripple-current rating against the RMS current it will actually see — in a boost the output cap RMS current is roughly I_out × √(D/(1−D)), which grows quickly at high duty cycle, so a part that looks fine on capacitance can still run hot. Then I'd verify loop stability: the output cap's ESR zero and the capacitance value both affect the compensation, so I'd confirm the chosen value keeps the crossover and phase margin where the converter's compensation was designed for, and check the datasheet's recommended output capacitance range. Finally I'd derate for DC bias (ceramic capacitance falls sharply with applied voltage), temperature, and aging, and confirm the capacitor's voltage rating has margin over the boost output plus transients. On the bench I'd verify with a current probe on the burst load and a scope on the rail, looking at both the initial ESR step and the recovery.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to add a small electrolytic or polymer bulk cap in parallel with ceramics versus just more ceramics? What changes if the burst frequency is close to the converter's loop bandwidth?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a SAR ADC and a delta-sigma ADC for digitizing a slowly varying physiological signal that needs high resolution, and what datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The signal is slow, so bandwidth isn't the constraint — the decision comes down to resolution, noise, and the cost of the supporting circuitry. A delta-sigma converter is usually the natural fit for a slow, high-resolution signal: it oversamples and noise-shapes, so you get very high effective resolution (18–24 bits) without needing a fancy front-end, and its digital filter provides inherent anti-aliasing that relaxes the analog filter. The trade-offs are latency (the digital filter's group delay and settling time matter if you're multiplexing channels or need a fast response), and the fact that the modulator's sampling capacitor draws charge from the source, so the input driver still has to settle. A SAR is faster, has lower latency, and is simpler to multiplex, but at 16 bits its noise floor and INL are harder to achieve, and it needs a well-driven input and a clean reference. The datasheet parameters I'd actually compare: effective resolution / RMS noise at the target data rate (not just the headline bit count), INL and offset/gain error over temperature, the reference requirements (delta-sigma parts often have an integrated reference or a ratiometric input, which can simplify things), input bias current and the switched-cap input model, and for the delta-sigma the output data rate versus noise trade-off and the filter's settling behavior. I'd also weigh system-level cost: a delta-sigma may let me drop an external PGA or reference, while a SAR may need more analog conditioning. Then I'd confirm against the actual signal's noise budget — if the sensor's own noise is well above the converter's, the extra resolution buys nothing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the effective resolution on the bench rather than trusting the datasheet? What would make you choose a SAR despite the resolution disadvantage?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a MOSFET used as a low-side switch for a resistive load runs hot, even though the gate drive voltage looks correct and the load current measures as expected?

**Answer:** If the current and gate voltage are both as expected, the dissipation should be predictable, so the first thing I'd do is separate conduction loss from switching loss. Conduction loss is I² × R_DS(on), but R_DS(on) is specified at a particular V_GS and temperature — it rises significantly with junction temperature, so a part that looks fine cold can be dissipating much more once it's warm, which is a positive-feedback loop. I'd check the actual V_GS at the gate pin, not at the driver output, because gate resistance, trace inductance, and the driver's source return can all reduce the effective drive, and I'd confirm the part is fully enhanced rather than in a marginal linear region. Then I'd look at switching loss: if the load is switched at any meaningful frequency, the crossover time during turn-on and turn-off dominates, and a weak or slow gate drive, or a gate resistor that's too large, stretches that. I'd scope V_GS and V_DS simultaneously and look at the overlap area during transitions. I'd also check for unintended avalanche or body-diode conduction — with an inductive or even a long-wire resistive load there can be a transient that the body diode clamps, and that dissipates. Thermal path is the other half: I'd verify the thermal resistance from junction to ambient for the actual copper area and airflow, and whether the part is a small package with a poor thermal pad connection. Finally I'd check for oscillation — a MOSFET can oscillate with its gate loop or with the load wiring, and that shows up as heat without an obvious current change. The fix depends on which mechanism it is: better gate drive, a snubber, more copper, a lower-R_DS(on) part, or a slower switching edge if EMI allows.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish conduction loss from switching loss experimentally? What would you check first if the MOSFET is hot only when the load is disconnected?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a crystal for a microcontroller that must maintain timing accuracy over a wide temperature range, and what would you verify on the bench?

**Answer:** The first decision is the crystal's own stability spec over temperature — that's the dominant term, and it's a datasheet number you can't fix in the circuit. I'd pick a part whose frequency tolerance over the operating range, plus aging and the load-capacitance-induced pull, fits inside the system's timing budget with margin. Then I'd match the load capacitance: the crystal is specified at a particular C_L, and the board's actual load capacitance is the series combination of the two load caps plus stray capacitance, so I'd choose the caps to hit that C_L and account for the fact that the effective load capacitance is C1×C2/(C1+C2) plus C_stray. Getting this wrong pulls the frequency and, more importantly, affects startup margin. I'd also check the crystal's drive level rating against the oscillator's drive, and the ESR against the oscillator's maximum ESR spec — a high-ESR crystal may not start reliably, especially cold. On the bench I'd verify startup margin across temperature: at the cold and hot extremes, confirm the oscillator starts reliably every time from power-on, measure the startup time, and check the negative resistance margin by adding a series resistor and seeing how much the oscillator tolerates before it fails to start — a healthy margin is typically several times the crystal's ESR. I'd also measure the actual frequency error over temperature against the budget, and check the drive level with a current probe if the oscillator is sensitive to overdrive. If startup is marginal, the fix is usually load capacitance and ESR, not adding a parallel resistor, which masks the problem and burns power.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative resistance margin on the bench? What would you do if the crystal meets its spec but the system's timing budget is still exceeded?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the firmware lead argues that the ADC sampling rate should be increased from 100 Hz to 1 kHz to improve resolution through oversampling. You believe the higher rate will alias 50/60 Hz noise into the passband and require a much steeper anti-aliasing filter, increasing component count and board area. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a technical disagreement to resolve with data rather than a debate to win. First I'd make sure I understand the firmware lead's actual goal — if it's resolution, oversampling only helps if the noise is white and the signal is band-limited, and I'd want to agree on what "improve resolution" means in terms of the measurement requirement. Then I'd lay out the signal chain: at 1 kHz the Nyquist frequency is 500 Hz, so 50/60 Hz mains and its harmonics are well inside the band and will alias unless the anti-aliasing filter attenuates them before sampling — and a filter steep enough to do that at 100 Hz passband is a real cost in parts and board area. I'd propose a concrete comparison: keep the 100 Hz rate with the existing filter, and separately evaluate whether the resolution target can be met by averaging or by a modest rate increase with a filter that's still practical, and quantify the noise performance of each. If the firmware lead's concern is real, I'd want to find the cheapest way to meet it — maybe a higher-resolution converter or a different filter topology is better than raising the rate. I'd bring the discussion back to the measurement requirement and the error budget, and if we still disagree, escalate to the systems or quality lead with the trade-off documented, because on a medical device the decision needs to be traceable. The key is to keep it collaborative and make the decision on evidence, not seniority.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you quantify the aliasing risk experimentally before committing to a design change? What would you do if the firmware lead's approach turned out to be correct?
