# hardware-design — Day 107

## Q1: How would you approach selecting the sense resistor value and amplifier gain split for a low-side current-sense path in a battery-powered medical device, where the full-scale current is 2A, the ADC reference is 2.5V, and the measurement must resolve small currents in standby without burning excessive power in the shunt?

**Answer:** The shunt and the gain are two halves of one decision, so I'd start from the two competing constraints and work toward the middle. The shunt sets the power loss and the signal amplitude: at 2A full scale, even a 10 mΩ shunt dissipates 40 mW at peak, which is significant in a device that spends most of its life in standby, while a 1 mΩ shunt dissipates only 4 mW but produces just 2 mV at full scale — small enough that amplifier offset and drift become a large fraction of the reading. So the first step is to decide the minimum full-scale shunt voltage I can tolerate given the amplifier's offset and noise, then check the resulting dissipation against the thermal and battery-life budget.

Once the shunt is chosen, the gain is set by the ADC's full-scale input: gain = V_ref / (I_max × R_shunt). But I wouldn't automatically use the whole ADC range — I'd leave headroom for transients and calibration, and I'd check that the amplifier's input common-mode range and offset spec are compatible with the small shunt voltage at low currents. The key insight is that resolution at low current is limited by the amplifier's input-referred offset and noise, not by the ADC, so a high-gain stage on a tiny shunt can actually perform worse than a moderate shunt with moderate gain. I'd also consider a zero-drift or chopper-stabilized amplifier if the standby measurement must hold accuracy over temperature, and I'd verify the shunt's temperature coefficient and Kelvin connection layout, since a few milliohms of parasitic trace resistance in the sense path will swamp the measurement.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you lay out the Kelvin sense connections to avoid picking up trace resistance and switching noise? What would change if the same shunt had to measure bidirectional current?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a voltage reference IC and a filtered supply rail as the reference for a 16-bit ADC, and what datasheet parameters would actually drive the decision?

**Answer:** The reference is often the quiet bottleneck in a high-resolution measurement, so I'd treat it as a signal-chain component rather than a power component. The first question is what accuracy the system actually needs at the ADC input: at 16 bits with a 2.5V reference, one LSB is about 38 µV, so the reference's initial tolerance, temperature drift, and long-term drift all have to be small relative to that budget — and critically, drift matters more than initial accuracy because initial error can be calibrated out at manufacturing while drift cannot.

The parameters I'd compare are initial accuracy, temperature coefficient (in ppm/°C, converted into volts over the operating range), long-term drift, output noise (both low-frequency 0.1–10 Hz and wideband, since wideband noise aliases into the conversion), and dropout headroom against the supply. A filtered supply rail can look adequate on a bench at room temperature, but its noise and load regulation are usually far worse and unspecified over temperature, so it tends to fail the drift and noise budget even when the DC value is fine. I'd also check the reference's output capacitor requirements and its transient response to the ADC's switched-capacitor load — a reference that sags on each conversion will produce gain error that varies with sample rate. Finally, I'd verify the reference's specified performance is guaranteed over the full operating temperature range, not just at 25°C, because marginal datasheet numbers at temperature extremes are where these designs usually break.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you buffer a reference that can't drive the ADC's sampling transient directly? How would you verify reference noise on the bench without a low-noise measurement setup?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a boost converter's output is correct at room temperature but the output voltage sags and the converter becomes unstable at low temperature, even though the load is unchanged?

**Answer:** Temperature-dependent instability in a converter usually points at a component whose parameters move with temperature, so I'd work through the loop systematically rather than shotgunning parts. First I'd confirm the failure is really the converter and not the load: measure the input voltage, switch node waveform, and output ripple at cold with a scope, and check whether the converter is dropping out of regulation, hitting current limit, or oscillating. The switch node waveform is the most informative single measurement — it tells me whether the duty cycle is railing, whether there's pulse-skipping or subharmonic behavior, and whether the inductor current is going discontinuous.

Then I'd look at the components most likely to shift: the output capacitor's ESR rises sharply at cold, which can move the loop's compensation and degrade phase margin; the inductor's saturation current and DCR change; and the feedback divider's resistors and the compensation network's capacitors drift. I'd also check the reference and error amplifier inside the converter, and whether the converter's own datasheet specifies a minimum operating temperature for stable loop behavior. A common culprit is that the design was compensated at room temperature with a capacitor whose ESR was doing part of the job, and at cold the ESR rises enough to shift the zero and destabilize the loop. The fix is usually to re-derive the compensation with worst-case component tolerances over temperature, and to verify by measuring loop gain or at least step response at the temperature extremes rather than only at 25°C.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure loop stability on the bench without a network analyzer? What role does the output capacitor's ESR play in the compensation, and how would you design so the loop doesn't depend on it?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting a MOSFET for a load switch that must block reverse current when the device is powered off, and what failure modes would you check beyond the datasheet's on-resistance?

**Answer:** A load switch that has to block reverse current is really a bidirectional-blocking problem, so the first decision is topology: a single N-channel or P-channel MOSFET has an intrinsic body diode that conducts in one direction regardless of gate state, so a single device can't block reverse current on its own. The options are a back-to-back pair of MOSFETs (common source or common drain) to get true bidirectional blocking, or an integrated load switch that already includes the blocking structure. I'd pick based on whether the switch needs to block in both directions or only one, and on how much quiescent current the gate drive can afford.

Beyond on-resistance, the failure modes I'd check are: the body diode's behavior during the off state and whether it can forward-bias into the load; gate-source and gate-drain voltage ratings during transients, especially if the input can see inductive kickback; the safe operating area during inrush into a capacitive load, since the MOSFET spends time in the linear region and can be destroyed even though steady-state dissipation looks fine; thermal behavior in the package during that inrush; and the gate drive's behavior at low battery voltage, where a P-channel may not fully enhance or an N-channel high-side driver may not have enough headroom. I'd also check leakage current in the off state, since a load switch that leaks microamps defeats the purpose of power-gating a rail in a battery device, and I'd verify the switch's behavior during supply sequencing — a load switch that turns on before its control logic is powered can glitch the rail.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you protect the MOSFET during inrush into a large capacitive load? What would drive the choice between a P-channel high-side switch and an N-channel high-side switch with a charge pump?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the reliability engineer argues that your chosen crystal oscillator circuit has insufficient startup margin at cold temperature and proposes adding a parallel resistor across the crystal to guarantee startup. You're concerned that the resistor will increase power consumption and may not actually solve the root cause, which you suspect is the load capacitance selection. How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a data problem rather than a debate about whose instinct is right, because both concerns are legitimate — startup margin at cold is a real risk, and a blind fix that adds power consumption without addressing the cause is also a real risk. My first move would be to agree on what we're actually measuring: startup margin isn't a single number, it's the negative resistance the oscillator circuit presents compared to the crystal's resistance, measured across the temperature range. I'd propose we characterize that on a few boards at cold, hot, and room temperature, and compare the measured negative resistance against the crystal vendor's recommended margin — typically several times the crystal's ESR. That converts the disagreement into a shared measurement.

If the measurement shows margin is genuinely thin at cold, then the fix should target the cause: load capacitance that's mismatched to the crystal's specified value, stray capacitance from layout, or a drive level that's marginal. Adding a parallel resistor can help startup in some topologies, but it also loads the oscillator and burns current continuously, so I'd want to understand whether it's masking a load-capacitance problem rather than solving it. I'd bring the reliability engineer into that analysis rather than overrule them — their concern is valid, and if the data shows the margin is fine, that's a stronger answer than my opinion. If the data shows it's marginal, we fix the root cause and re-verify at cold, and if a parallel resistor turns out to be the right fix for the topology, we accept the power cost with the data to justify it. The goal is a design that's demonstrably robust at the temperature extremes, not a design that wins the review.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure negative resistance and startup margin on the bench? What layout practices most affect crystal startup margin?
