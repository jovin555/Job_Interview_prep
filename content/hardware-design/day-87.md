# hardware-design — Day 87

## Q1: How would you approach selecting a shunt resistor value and current-sense amplifier topology for measuring a bidirectional motor current of up to 1A, where the measurement must be accurate to ±2% over temperature and the shunt must not dissipate excessive power?
**Answer:** Start by budgeting the error and the power dissipation together, because they trade against each other. A larger shunt gives more signal for the amplifier and improves signal-to-noise, but dissipates more power (P = I²R) and drops more voltage in the motor path. A smaller shunt reduces loss but pushes the amplifier's offset and gain error to dominate the accuracy budget. For a 1A full-scale with a ±2% total accuracy target, I'd allocate the error budget across shunt tolerance and drift, amplifier offset and its drift, gain error, and the reference (if the amplifier output is digitized). A common starting point is a shunt that produces a full-scale drop in the tens of millivolts — enough to keep the amplifier's input-referred offset small relative to the signal, but low enough that I²R loss stays well under a watt and the drop doesn't rob the motor of meaningful voltage.

For topology, bidirectional measurement means the amplifier must handle both polarities of differential input and produce a bipolar (or mid-rail-referenced) output. The two main choices are a difference amplifier built from an op-amp and matched resistor network, or a dedicated current-sense amplifier IC. A dedicated current-sense amp is usually preferable because it integrates the matched gain network (so gain error and CMRR are specified and trimmed), and many parts offer a reference pin so the output can be biased to mid-supply for bidirectional swing. If I build it discretely, the resistor matching directly sets CMRR and gain error, so I'd need 0.1% or better resistors and would still expect worse drift than an integrated part.

Key parameters to check: input offset voltage and its temperature drift (this often dominates at low shunt drops), CMRR over the common-mode range the motor sees, gain error and gain drift, and bandwidth/slew for the motor's current dynamics. For the shunt itself, a low-TCR alloy (not a standard thick-film chip) matters because self-heating and ambient swing will otherwise shift the reading. Kelvin (four-terminal) connection to the shunt is essential to keep solder-joint and trace resistance out of the measurement.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you lay out the shunt and the sense traces to preserve the Kelvin connection and reject the motor's switching noise?
- If the motor is PWM-driven, how would that change your choice of amplifier bandwidth and filtering?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a hardware-based power-on self-test for a medical device's analog signal chain, and what would you want it to verify independently of firmware?
**Answer:** The point of a hardware POST is to verify the analog signal chain using known, on-board references and injected signals — so that a fault is caught even if the ADC, the firmware, or the calibration data is wrong. If the test relies on the same ADC and firmware it's supposed to be checking, it can't distinguish "the sensor is broken" from "the ADC is broken" from "the firmware is misreading."

I'd structure it around a few independent checks. First, rail monitors: comparators or supervisor ICs that confirm each supply is within window before the processor is allowed to run, and that flag brownout. Second, a reference check: switch the ADC input (via an analog mux or a dedicated test path) to a known precision reference and confirm the conversion lands within a tight window — this exercises the reference, the mux, the ADC, and the digital readback path together. Third, a signal-chain integrity check: inject a known stimulus (a divided-down reference, or a DAC-generated tone) at the front-end input and verify the chain's gain and offset are within tolerance. Fourth, an open/short check on sensor connections, often done by injecting a small current and looking for the expected impedance.

The design decisions are: what's the known-good stimulus, how tight is the pass/fail window (accounting for the reference's own tolerance and the chain's specified accuracy), and how is the result latched so a failure can't be silently ignored. I'd want the failure to set a hardware flag or hold the device in a safe state, not just be a value the firmware is trusted to interpret. For a device that must fail safe, the POST result should gate enabling of any therapeutic output.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you keep the POST stimulus injection from loading or disturbing the normal signal path during operation?
- How would you decide the pass/fail window so you don't get false failures from normal component tolerance?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a circuit where a device works correctly on the bench but fails intermittently once it's installed in its enclosure, and the failure correlates with the device being handled or moved?
**Answer:** The correlation with handling and movement is the key clue — it points to something mechanical or physical rather than a purely electrical design flaw. I'd treat it as an intermittent-connection or mechanical-stress problem first, and only fall back to electrical causes if that's ruled out.

My first steps: reproduce it deliberately. Flex the board, tap components, wiggle connectors and cables, and apply the specific motion that triggers it, while monitoring the failing signal. If I can make it fail on demand, I can bisect. Common culprits: a connector that's not fully seated or whose retention is marginal once the cable is routed in the enclosure; a solder joint with a hairline crack (often on a large or heavy component, or a connector, or a part stressed during assembly); a board that flexes against a standoff or enclosure rib and cracks a trace or a ceramic capacitor; a cable that's pinched or pulled when the lid closes; or a component that's thermally coupled differently once enclosed.

I'd use the tools that localize intermittents: a scope with a deep-memory or trigger-on-glitch mode to catch the event, and if needed a current probe or a logic analyzer on the digital side. Freeze spray and a heat gun help separate thermal from mechanical causes. Continuity checks with a meter while flexing can find cracked joints, but a scope is better because it catches microsecond opens a meter won't.

Once localized, the fix is usually mechanical: strain relief on cables, a taller standoff or a cutout so the board doesn't flex, a different connector with better retention, or reflow/underfill on the suspect joint. The important part is to verify the fix under the same motion that reproduced the failure, and to check whether the same root cause could affect other units in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you tell the difference between a cracked solder joint and a cracked ceramic capacitor, given both can present as intermittent?
- What would you change in the design review process to catch this class of problem before it reaches the enclosure?

## Q4: How would you approach choosing between a voltage-mode and a current-mode control architecture for a buck converter that must supply a fast-transient digital load while also feeding a sensitive analog rail through a downstream LDO?
**Answer:** The two loads pull the decision in different directions, so I'd start by being clear about what each one actually needs. The digital load wants fast transient response and good loop bandwidth so the rail doesn't sag on load steps. The analog rail is fed through an LDO, so the LDO's PSRR will attenuate whatever ripple and noise the buck produces — but only up to the LDO's loop bandwidth, and PSRR falls off at higher frequency. So the buck's switching noise and its transient behavior both matter, but the LDO buys me some margin on the analog side.

Current-mode control senses inductor current directly, which gives a single-pole response (the inductor pole is moved by the inner current loop), making compensation simpler and giving inherently good line and load transient response. It also has cycle-by-cycle current limiting, which is useful for protection. The trade-offs are: it needs slope compensation at duty cycles above 50% to avoid subharmonic oscillation, it's more sensitive to noise on the current-sense signal, and the current-sense signal itself can be a noise source. Voltage-mode control has a two-pole response that's harder to compensate and slower to react to load steps, but it's less sensitive to switching noise on the sense node and can be more predictable.

For this case — a fast-transient digital load plus a downstream LDO — I'd lean toward current-mode for the transient response and the built-in current limit, and then make sure the switching noise it produces is handled by the LDO's PSRR plus input filtering. I'd check the LDO's PSRR curve against the buck's switching frequency and its harmonics, and add an LC or π input filter if the LDO's rejection isn't enough at the frequencies that matter. I'd also verify the current-sense signal's noise immunity in layout, since that's where current-mode control is most vulnerable.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide the buck's switching frequency given the LDO's PSRR curve and the transient requirement?
- What would you check on the bench to confirm the LDO is actually rejecting the buck's ripple as expected?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're the lead hardware engineer on a medical device project, and during a design review the software lead argues that the device's power-on self-test can be simplified by removing the hardware checks — rail monitors, reference checks — and relying on firmware to verify the system by reading the ADC and checking that values are in range. The software lead argues this reduces component count and board area. You believe the hardware checks are necessary because they verify the analog signal chain independently of the ADC and the firmware. How would you handle this disagreement?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a question of what the self-test is actually for, and try to get the software lead to agree on that first, because the disagreement usually dissolves once the purpose is clear. The purpose of a POST in a medical device is to detect a fault before the device is used on a patient, and to fail safe if it can't confirm the device is working. If the test is implemented entirely in firmware reading the ADC, then a fault in the ADC, the reference, or the firmware itself can produce a passing result — the test can't detect the very failures it's supposed to catch. That's the core argument, and it's not about cost or board area; it's about whether the test is independent of what it's testing.

I'd make that concrete rather than abstract. I'd walk through a specific failure: the ADC's reference drifts or the ADC itself develops an offset, and the firmware reads a plausible-looking value and passes the test. Or the firmware hangs during POST and never reports a failure. In both cases the device could be enabled with a broken signal chain. I'd ask the software lead how their approach would catch those, and if the answer is "it wouldn't," we've found the gap.

Then I'd address the cost concern honestly, because it's a real constraint. I'd look at whether the hardware checks can be made cheaper — a single supervisor IC covering multiple rails, a mux that reuses the existing ADC to check the reference, a comparator instead of a full monitor — rather than removing them entirely. The goal is to keep the independence while minimizing the added parts. If the software lead's real concern is board area, there may be a way to satisfy both.

If we still disagree after that, I'd escalate to the systems or quality lead with the risk framed clearly: the firmware-only approach leaves a class of failures undetected, and for a device that delivers therapy or monitors a patient, that's a risk-management decision, not just an engineering preference. I'd want it documented either way, so the decision is made with the risk visible.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you frame this in risk-management terms (e.g., ISO 14971) so the decision is traceable?
- If the team decides to go firmware-only for cost reasons, what compensating controls would you insist on?
