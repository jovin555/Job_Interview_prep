# space-rad-hard — Day 100

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** I'd start by treating the reference as the accuracy bottleneck rather than the ADC itself, because the ADC's transfer function is only as good as the reference it's measured against. With no radiation data, the honest position is that the reference's behavior under TID and low-dose-rate exposure is unknown, so the design has to either bound that risk or remove it.

The first move is to characterize what I can. Even a limited test campaign — a few parts at a couple of dose levels, plus a low-dose-rate leg if ELDRS is a concern for the process — gives me a drift envelope to design against, which is far better than assuming zero. If budget doesn't allow testing, I'd look for published data on similar topologies or the same die in a different package, and treat any number as a hypothesis to be validated, not a guarantee.

Architecturally, I'd avoid depending on absolute reference accuracy for the mission life. That means a ratiometric measurement where the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, so reference drift largely cancels in the ratio. Where ratiometric isn't possible, I'd add an in-system calibration path — a stable, low-drift transfer standard or an on-board reference of a different technology that I can switch in periodically and use to correct the primary reference's reading. The calibration has to be able to distinguish reference drift from ADC drift, which usually means measuring the reference against a second, independent artifact.

I'd also design the reference's operating point conservatively: derate the load current well below the datasheet typical, keep it thermally stable, and avoid operating near the top of its temperature range where TID-induced drift tends to compound. A reference that's lightly loaded and thermally quiet has less parametric drift to begin with.

Finally, I'd make the drift observable. If the calibration residuals trend over time, that's a signal the reference is walking, and the firmware can widen its uncertainty band or flag the measurement as degraded rather than silently reporting bad data. The key principle is: don't claim accuracy you can't verify in flight.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the in-system calibration so it doesn't consume mission time or disturb the measurement it's trying to correct? What would you do if the calibration artifact itself drifts and you can't tell which of the two references is wrong?

## Q2: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be acknowledged before anything else — I'd open by restating what they've done and what's good about it, so the conversation is about the margin, not about their competence.

Then I'd try to make the disagreement concrete rather than abstract. "Under-margined" is a judgment; a number is a discussion. I'd ask them to walk me through the worst-case stack-up they used — which radiation environment, which part parameters, which tolerances — and I'd walk through mine. Very often the gap turns out to be a difference in assumptions rather than a difference in competence: they assumed a benign environment, or a typical rather than worst-case parameter, or they didn't include a drift term I think belongs in the budget. Once the assumptions are on the table, the disagreement usually resolves itself, or it narrows to one specific number we can go test or look up.

If we still disagree after that, I'd try to convert it into an experiment or a bounded question rather than a vote. Can we get the data? Is there a test we can run, a datasheet curve we can check, a derating guideline we can cite? A design review that ends with "we'll measure this" is healthier than one that ends with "I'm the lead, so we're doing it my way."

I'd also be genuinely open to being wrong. The engineer may have a mitigation I haven't considered — a clamp, a filter, a graceful-degradation path — that makes the under-margin acceptable in a way I didn't see. If they can show the failure is bounded and recoverable, that's a legitimate engineering answer, and I should say so.

What I'd avoid is letting it become adversarial or letting it drag. If after the data discussion there's still a real safety or mission-risk gap, I'd make the call, explain the reasoning transparently, and record the dissent in the review minutes so the decision and its rationale are traceable. The engineer should leave feeling heard even if overruled — and ideally with a clear path to prove their point later if new data emerges.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer escalates over your head after the review? What would you do differently if the under-margined item were on a schedule-critical path and re-spinning the design would cost weeks?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core idea is that the distribution network has to be segmented so that a fault is contained at the smallest boundary that still lets the system meet its mission. That means thinking in tiers: the primary bus, the branch feeds, and the individual load points, with protection and current limiting at each boundary rather than one big fuse at the top.

I'd start by classifying loads by criticality and by their SEL behavior. A load that's known to latch and draw excessive current needs a current-limited feed with a latch-up detection and power-cycle mechanism — either a dedicated current-sense plus switch, or a latching current limiter that folds back and then retries. A load that's benign but non-critical can share a branch with similar loads, provided the branch itself is protected so one bad load can't drag the branch down. The critical loads get their own branch, ideally with a redundant feed path.

The protection has to be fast enough to act before the latch-up pulls the upstream rail out of regulation, but slow enough not to trip on legitimate inrush. That's the classic tension: a capacitive load's inrush looks a lot like a fault. I'd handle it with a controlled slew rate on the switch, a current limit set above the worst-case inrush but below the latch current, and a retry policy with a defined duty cycle so a persistently latched load doesn't oscillate the bus.

I'd also make sure the protection is latching rather than auto-retrying indefinitely, or at least that repeated trips escalate to a hard-off state and get reported. A load that trips three times in a row is telling you something, and the system should log it rather than keep hammering the bus.

Finally, I'd verify the scheme against the actual failure modes: simulate or inject a shorted load on each branch and confirm the other branches stay within regulation, and confirm the upstream bus doesn't see a transient that trips its own protection. The distribution network is only fault-tolerant if the containment boundary actually holds under the fault current the load can draw.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the retry policy for a latched load — how many retries, and over what interval? What would you do if a critical load's own protection is the thing that fails, rather than the load latching?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part here is that the failure mode is designed to defeat the obvious detector. A heartbeat that keeps toggling looks healthy to a simple watchdog, so the test plan has to verify recovery from a state that is *not* a clean hang — the processor is alive enough to toggle a pin but not alive enough to do its job.

I'd start by defining what "recovered correctly" means in observable terms: the control loop resumes producing valid outputs within a bounded time, the state it resumes from is consistent (no stale setpoints, no corrupted integrator terms), and the event is logged. Then I'd build a fault-injection harness that can put the processor into that specific state on demand — not by irradiating it, but by instrumenting the firmware with a test hook that jumps into a tight loop toggling the heartbeat GPIO while skipping the control task. That gives repeatable, deterministic injection without a beam.

The test then checks three things. First, does the detector actually catch it? If the heartbeat is the only health signal, it won't, so the plan should confirm that the real health monitor is something richer — a task-completion counter, a sequence check, a challenge-response between the control task and the supervisor — and that injecting the SEFI-like state trips it. Second, does the recovery action work? Whether that's a task restart, a subsystem reset, or a full processor reset, the test verifies the system comes back to a known-good state and the control loop resumes. Third, does recovery leave the system safe in the interim? During the window between fault and recovery, the outputs must fail to a safe state, not hold their last value.

I'd also test the boundaries: inject the state during startup, during a critical control transaction, and repeatedly in quick succession, because recovery logic often works once and fails under back-to-back events. And I'd verify the logging — if the event isn't recorded, you can't tell in flight whether recovery happened or whether the system just happened to look fine.

The honest limitation is that ground injection can't reproduce the full radiation environment, so the plan should be explicit that it validates the *recovery mechanism*, and the radiation test campaign separately validates that the mechanism triggers under real SEE. The two together are the argument; neither alone is.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the health monitor so it can't itself be fooled by a corrupted heartbeat? What's the risk of making the recovery action too aggressive and causing more disruption than the fault?

## Q5: How would you approach derating and part selection for a space-deployed board, and how does derating interact with the radiation tolerance you're trying to achieve?

**Answer:** Derating and radiation tolerance are usually treated as separate checklists, but they interact in ways that matter, so I'd approach them together rather than sequentially.

On derating itself, the principle is to keep every part well inside its ratings so that the parametric drift from TID, temperature, and aging doesn't push it out of spec over the mission. That means voltage, current, power, and junction temperature all get margins against the worst-case operating point, not the typical one — and the worst case has to include the radiation-induced shift, not just the pre-rad datasheet numbers. A part that's derated to 70% of its voltage rating at beginning of life may be at 85% after a few tens of krad if its breakdown walks, so the derating budget has to reserve room for that drift.

The interaction cuts both ways. A part with generous derating has more headroom to absorb radiation-induced parametric shift, so derating is itself a radiation mitigation — it buys margin that the drift consumes. Conversely, a part that's tightly derated has no room for drift, so even a modest TID-induced shift can take it out of spec. That's why I'd want the radiation data, or a conservative assumption, before I finalize the derating numbers: the two budgets share the same margin.

On part selection, I'd start from what's qualified and work backward. QML-listed parts with radiation test data are the default for anything critical, and the derating guidelines for those parts are usually published alongside. For COTS parts, the selection criteria tighten: I want a part whose failure modes are bounded and observable, whose parametric drift I can either test or bound conservatively, and whose derating I can set aggressively enough to cover the unknown. A COTS part with no radiation data and no margin to spare is a bad bet regardless of how good its datasheet looks.

I'd also think about derating at the system level, not just the component level. A power supply derated to 60% of its load rating has thermal and electrical headroom that a 90%-loaded supply doesn't, and that headroom is what lets the system survive a transient or a drift without a brownout. The derating philosophy should be visible in the architecture, not just in a spreadsheet.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set a derating number for a COTS part with no radiation data — what assumption would you use, and how would you justify it? Where does derating stop helping and start costing you mass, volume, or efficiency that you can't afford?
