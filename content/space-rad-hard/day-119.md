# space-rad-hard — Day 119

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload where several loads share a common upstream rail, and a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not collapse the rail for the others?

**Answer:** The core principle is to make the shared rail "upstream" of the fault, not "downstream" of it. I'd start by drawing a clear boundary: the common rail is a bulk supply, and every load hangs off it through its own protection element — a current-limited load switch or an eFuse with a hard current limit and a latch-off or auto-retry behavior. The protection element must be sized so that its current limit is above the load's legitimate inrush and steady-state draw but well below the point where the load's fault current would pull the rail down. That means characterizing each load's inrush profile (capacitive loads can draw many times steady-state current for milliseconds) and choosing a switch with an appropriate current-limit profile — ideally one with a controlled slew rate so inrush doesn't trip the limit spuriously.

The second piece is rail stiffness. Even with per-load protection, a fast SEL event can momentarily demand current before the switch limits it, so the upstream rail needs enough bulk capacitance and a source impedance low enough that the transient doesn't dip below the other loads' minimum operating voltage. I'd budget this explicitly: worst-case fault current times the switch's response time gives a charge, and the bulk cap must supply that charge within the allowed rail droop. I'd also consider a fast-acting clamp or crowbar on the rail itself if the fault current is large relative to the rail's capacity.

Third, I'd think about fault isolation and recovery. A latch-off switch that stays off until commanded gives you deterministic behavior but requires the controller to notice and re-enable; an auto-retry switch recovers on its own but can oscillate if the fault is persistent. For a payload with mixed criticality, I'd likely use latch-off with telemetry on the critical loads so the system knows a load dropped out, and auto-retry on non-critical housekeeping loads. Finally, I'd verify the whole scheme under fault injection — short each load output and confirm the rail stays within spec and the other loads keep running.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the current-limit threshold when a load's inrush current is close to its fault current? What telemetry would you want from each protection element, and how would you avoid the telemetry path itself becoming a single point of failure?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage supervisor or reset IC for a space-deployed system, given that most commercial parts are not radiation-characterized?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a risk-tiered selection problem rather than a binary "rad-hard or not" decision. First, I'd define what the supervisor actually has to do and what failure of it means. A supervisor that holds the processor in reset during power-up and asserts reset on undervoltage is a safety-critical function — if it falsely asserts, the system is dead; if it fails to assert, the processor may run out of spec. So the tolerance for radiation-induced behavior change is low, and I'd weight that heavily.

Next, I'd look at what data exists. Even without a radiation report, some manufacturers publish SEE test results or TID data for related parts in the same family, and I'd check whether the part is derived from a process with known radiation behavior. I'd also look at the part's architecture: a simple bandgap-referenced comparator with a trimmed threshold is more susceptible to TID-induced threshold drift than a design with a more robust reference, and a part with hysteresis and a defined output stage is easier to reason about than one with complex internal state. Bipolar parts can show enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity (ELDRS), so I'd want to know the process.

If no data exists and the function is critical, I'd either (a) select a part with published radiation data even at higher cost, (b) qualify the candidate part with a targeted test — TID to the mission dose plus margin, and heavy-ion or proton SEE testing focused on whether the output can glitch or latch — or (c) design around the uncertainty: use a supervisor with a wide threshold window, add an independent second supervisor or a discrete comparator-based reset, and make the reset tree tolerant of a single supervisor failing. I'd also consider whether the supervisor's output can be monitored by the processor so a stuck output is detectable.

The key discipline is documenting the decision: what data you had, what you assumed, and what mitigation covers the gap. That traceability matters both for the design review and for the reliability case.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure a limited-budget radiation test to get the most decision value out of a handful of parts? If you use two supervisors in a redundant arrangement, how do you combine their outputs without creating a new single point of failure?

## Q3: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating two different things the engineer is conflating: redundancy of computation and integrity of the input. TMR on the digital side protects against a flip in a flip-flop or a corrupted register — it ensures the logic computes the same answer three times and votes. But if all three TMR lanes are fed the same corrupted sample from a single analog front-end, they will all agree on the wrong answer, and the voter will confidently output it. TMR protects against random logic faults; it does nothing for a common-mode input fault. So the argument that "the digital side is covered, therefore the analog side is covered" doesn't hold — the analog chain is a single point of failure for the entire measurement.

Then I'd look at what failure modes the analog chain actually has in the radiation environment. The amplifier front-end can experience single-event transients — a momentary spike or dip on its output that the ADC samples as a real signal. The ADC itself can produce a corrupted conversion. The sensor bias or reference can drift with TID. None of these are caught by digital TMR. The question is whether the control loop can tolerate a single bad sample: if the loop has a low-pass characteristic or a plausibility check, a single transient may be filtered out; if it acts on every sample, a transient becomes an actuator command.

So my evaluation would be: what's the consequence of a single corrupted measurement, and what's the cheapest mitigation that addresses it? Options range from a median filter or rate-of-change limit in firmware (cheap, catches single-sample transients), to a plausibility window compared against a redundant sensor, to a fully redundant analog channel with a second amplifier and ADC. The right answer depends on the criticality of the loop. I'd also point out that a redundant analog channel doesn't have to be a full duplicate — sometimes a second, lower-grade sensor used only for cross-checking is enough to catch a gross fault.

The conversation with the engineer would be collaborative: I'd acknowledge that the digital TMR is good work and then walk through a concrete fault — an SET on the amplifier output — and ask what catches it. Usually that reframes the problem without me having to assert authority.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a plausibility check that catches a corrupted sample without adding so much latency that the control loop becomes unstable? If you add a second analog channel, how do you decide which one to trust when they disagree?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The defining characteristic of this failure is that the usual "is it alive?" indicators are lying — the processor is drawing current and the heartbeat GPIO is still toggling, so a naive watchdog that just checks "is the heartbeat moving?" will never fire. The test plan has to specifically exercise that blind spot.

First, I'd define what "recovered correctly" means in observable terms: the control loop resumes producing correct outputs within a bounded time, the system state is consistent (no stale setpoints, no half-updated configuration), and any outputs that were mid-transition are driven to a safe state during the outage. That definition drives the test.

To create the SEFI condition without a particle beam, I'd use fault injection at the firmware or hardware level. On the firmware side, I can deliberately corrupt the program counter, corrupt a loop counter, or force the control task into an infinite loop while leaving a separate heartbeat task running — this reproduces the "alive but not working" signature. On the hardware side, I can use a debugger to halt the core while peripherals keep running, or inject a fault that stalls the control task's scheduler slot. The point is to reproduce the specific signature, not just "crash the processor."

Then I'd verify the detection mechanism. If the design uses a watchdog, the test must confirm the watchdog is fed by the control loop itself, not by the heartbeat task — otherwise the SEFI won't be caught. A windowed watchdog or a challenge-response watchdog (where the processor must answer a question only the control loop can compute) is much stronger here than a simple kick. I'd test that the watchdog fires within the specified window and that the reset brings the system back to a known state.

Finally, I'd test the recovery path end to end: after the injected fault, does the system reinitialize correctly, does it re-establish its outputs safely, and does it log the event? I'd repeat the injection many times, at different points in the control cycle, to catch state-dependent recovery bugs. And I'd verify the safe-state behavior during the outage — the actuator should go to a defined safe output, not hold its last value indefinitely.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a watchdog that can't be fooled by a heartbeat task that's still running? What would you log to distinguish a SEFI recovery from a normal power-on reset in the field?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is make sure I actually understand the proposal before pushing back — confidence plus real work usually means there's reasoning behind it, and I may be missing context. So I'd ask the engineer to walk through the margin analysis: what environment are they designing to, what's the worst case they considered, and where did the numbers come from. That does two things: it respects the work, and it surfaces whether the gap is in the analysis or in my understanding.

If the gap is real, I'd try to make it concrete rather than abstract. "Under-margined" is a judgment; a specific failure scenario is an argument. I'd walk through a particular radiation event — say, a transient on a reference that pushes the output past a threshold — and ask what happens in their design. If they have an answer, great, the design may be fine and I've learned something. If they don't, the gap is now visible to everyone in the room, not just asserted by me. That keeps it technical rather than personal.

I'd also separate the decision from the person. The question isn't "is this engineer right?" but "does this design meet the requirement?" I'd frame it that way explicitly and bring the requirement into the room — the environment spec, the derating guideline, the reliability target. If the design is under-margined against a documented requirement, that's an objective finding, not my opinion versus theirs.

If we still disagree after that, I'd propose a way to resolve it that doesn't depend on seniority: a targeted analysis, a quick test, or a review by a third party with relevant expertise. And I'd be willing to be wrong — if the analysis shows the margin is adequate, I'd say so clearly and move on. The goal is the right decision, not winning the review. What I'd avoid is either steamrolling the engineer or deferring just to keep the peace; both leave the design risk unresolved.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis is correct but you still believe the risk is unacceptable for program reasons? How do you make sure a junior engineer feels able to push back on you in the next review?
