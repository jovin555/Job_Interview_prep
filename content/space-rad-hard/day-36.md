# space-rad-hard — Day 36

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant current-sense circuit for a spacecraft power bus, where the sense resistor itself is exposed to radiation and its value may drift over the mission lifetime?

**Answer:** The first consideration is that passive components are not truly radiation-immune — precision resistors, especially thin-film types, can exhibit resistance drift under total ionizing dose, and the effect is often bias-dependent. I would start by selecting a resistor technology with known radiation behavior, such as bulk metal foil or certain thick-film types that have characterization data, rather than assuming a standard precision resistor will hold its tolerance. The circuit topology should also be designed to tolerate drift: for example, using a ratiometric measurement approach where the sense resistor drift affects both the measurement and a reference path equally, or implementing periodic in-flight calibration against a known current source. I would also consider using a four-wire Kelvin connection to eliminate trace resistance effects, and place the sense resistor and amplifier in a thermally coupled layout so temperature-induced drift is common-mode. The amplifier itself should be chosen with radiation tolerance in mind — an op-amp with known TID behavior, or one that is rad-hard qualified. Finally, I would add firmware-level monitoring: track the sense resistor's apparent value over time by comparing against a calibrated shunt or by measuring a known reference current during idle periods, and flag if drift exceeds a threshold that would compromise measurement accuracy.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between radiation-induced drift in the sense resistor versus drift in the amplifier's gain? What if the calibration current source itself is also radiation-sensitive?

## Q2: You are reviewing a design for a space-deployed system where a junior engineer has placed a single ferrite bead in series with the ADC's analog supply rail to filter switching noise from the DC-DC converter. The ferrite bead has no radiation characterization, and the ADC's power supply rejection ratio (PSRR) is already specified as 60 dB at 1 MHz. How would you evaluate this design choice?

**Answer:** I would evaluate this on several levels. First, the ferrite bead's lack of radiation characterization is a concern, but not necessarily disqualifying — ferrite beads are passive magnetic components, and their primary radiation concern is typically displacement damage affecting the ferrite material's permeability, which could shift its impedance characteristics over the mission. The bigger question is whether the ferrite bead is even necessary. If the ADC's PSRR is 60 dB at the switching frequency, the noise coupled through the supply may already be attenuated to an acceptable level, and adding a ferrite bead introduces a series impedance that can cause supply droop under dynamic load currents, potentially degrading ADC performance in a different way. I would ask the engineer to show the actual noise budget: what is the switching ripple amplitude, what is the ADC's PSRR at that frequency, and what is the resulting noise contribution at the ADC's reference input? If the PSRR is sufficient, the ferrite bead is adding risk without benefit. If it is needed, I would recommend characterizing the ferrite bead's impedance shift under radiation, or selecting a part with known radiation behavior. I would also check whether the ferrite bead creates a resonant circuit with the ADC's decoupling capacitors that could amplify noise at certain frequencies, and whether the DC resistance of the bead causes unacceptable voltage drop under worst-case ADC current draw.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the ADC's PSRR degrades with total ionizing dose? How would you verify the ferrite bead's impedance characteristics without radiation testing?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem for a space-deployed system where a single-event transient (SET) on the sample-and-hold circuit could corrupt a critical measurement, and the system cannot simply retry because the measurement is time-sensitive?

**Answer:** This requires a layered approach. At the circuit level, I would consider using multiple ADC channels sampling the same signal simultaneously, with the results voted in firmware or hardware — if two of three readings agree, that value is used. This is effectively analog-domain TMR, and it handles SETs on the sample-and-hold, the ADC conversion, and the digital output path. The trade-off is cost, board area, and power, so I would first assess the criticality of the measurement and the expected SET rate. If the measurement is truly time-sensitive and safety-critical, the redundancy is justified. At the signal conditioning level, I would add filtering to limit the bandwidth of the input signal — an SET on the sample-and-hold is essentially a broadband glitch, and a low-pass filter with a time constant matched to the signal's maximum slew rate can attenuate transients that are faster than the signal of interest. I would also consider using a track-and-hold architecture with a larger hold capacitor, which makes the circuit less sensitive to charge injection from an SET. In firmware, I would implement plausibility checking: compare the reading against the previous value and the expected rate of change, and if a reading is implausible, flag it and use the last valid value or a model-based estimate. The key is to have a defined fallback behavior — the system must know what to do when a measurement is suspect, and that decision should be made before the event occurs, not in the moment.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine the voting threshold — how much disagreement between channels is acceptable before you declare a fault? What if the SET affects the analog input multiplexer rather than the sample-and-hold?

## Q4: Imagine you are leading a design review for a space-deployed system that uses a radiation-hardened FPGA and a COTS DC-DC converter for the 3.3V rail. The converter's datasheet specifies a maximum output voltage of 3.47V under worst-case conditions, and the FPGA's absolute maximum rating is 3.6V. A junior engineer argues that the 130 mV margin is acceptable because "the FPGA will never actually see 3.47V in practice — the converter will typically output 3.3V, and the FPGA's absolute maximum rating already includes some safety margin." How would you handle this disagreement?

**Answer:** I would acknowledge that the engineer's reasoning has some merit — absolute maximum ratings do include a safety margin, and the probability of the converter actually hitting 3.47V may be low. However, I would reframe the discussion around worst-case analysis and risk management. The datasheet specifies 3.47V as the maximum under worst-case conditions, which means there are credible scenarios where the output reaches that value: high input voltage, low load, component tolerance stacking, temperature extremes, and radiation-induced parameter shifts in the converter's feedback network. The FPGA's absolute maximum rating is not a recommended operating condition — it is the limit beyond which damage may occur, and operating near it, even briefly, reduces long-term reliability. I would also point out that the margin calculation should include the full error budget: the converter's output tolerance, the PCB trace resistance between the converter and the FPGA, any transient overshoot during load steps, and the possibility of radiation-induced shifts in the converter's reference voltage. When you add all of these, the 130 mV margin may shrink considerably. I would recommend either adding a post-regulator or a precision voltage supervisor that clamps or shuts down the rail if it exceeds a safe threshold, or selecting a converter with tighter output tolerance. The decision should be based on a documented worst-case analysis, not on an assumption about typical behavior.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you quantify the radiation-induced shift in the converter's output voltage? What if adding a post-regulator introduces its own reliability concerns?

## Q5: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant firmware update strategy for a space-deployed system where the application firmware resides in flash memory that is susceptible to single-event upsets, and a corrupted update could render the system unrecoverable?

**Answer:** The fundamental principle is that the system must always have a known-good boot path that cannot be corrupted by a failed update. I would structure the flash memory into at least two regions: a bootloader region that is write-protected after initial programming, and an application region that can be updated. The bootloader should be minimal, immutable, and capable of validating the application before jumping to it. For the update process itself, I would implement a multi-stage approach: first, the new firmware image is received and stored in a staging area, with a checksum or cryptographic hash computed over the entire image. The image is validated against the hash before it is committed to the active application region. The commit should be atomic — either the entire new image is written and verified, or the old image remains intact. I would also implement a fallback mechanism: if the new application fails to boot or fails its self-test within a timeout, the bootloader reverts to the previous known-good image. For radiation-induced corruption of the flash itself, I would consider using error-correcting code (ECC) if the flash supports it, or storing redundant copies of critical data structures. The bootloader should also verify the integrity of the application on every boot, not just after an update, because a single-event upset could corrupt the flash between updates. Finally, I would design the update protocol to be resumable — if the communication link drops mid-transfer, the system should be able to resume from the last complete block rather than restarting, and the staging area should be large enough to hold the complete image before commit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the case where the bootloader itself is corrupted? What if the system has only one flash device and no redundant boot path?
