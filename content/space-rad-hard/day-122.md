# space-rad-hard — Day 122

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The voltage reference is the accuracy anchor for the whole conversion chain, so a COTS reference with no radiation data is a real risk that has to be characterized rather than assumed. I'd start by treating the reference as a known unknown: its initial accuracy and temperature drift are specified, but TID-induced drift, long-term drift, and enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity (ELDRS) are not. The first step is to bound the risk — either get the part into a low-dose-rate TID test (ideally at a dose rate representative of the mission, since ELDRS can make low-dose-rate behavior much worse than high-dose-rate testing suggests), or select a reference that at least has published radiation data or a rad-tolerant equivalent.

If testing isn't feasible, I'd design around the uncertainty. That means: (1) provide a calibration path — a stable, known reference or a ratiometric measurement against a monitored reference — so drift can be corrected in firmware over mission life; (2) add margin in the error budget so that worst-case TID drift plus temperature drift plus long-term drift still leaves the measurement within spec; (3) consider a reference topology that is inherently more tolerant, such as a buried-Zener or a compensated bandgap with a known radiation response, rather than an uncharacterized part; and (4) monitor the reference in situ — measure it against a second, independent reference or against a known internal standard — so that drift is observable rather than silent.

The key discipline is to make the reference's behavior observable and correctable, not to assume the datasheet's initial accuracy holds for five years in a radiation environment.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish TID-induced drift from ordinary long-term drift in a reference you can only observe in flight? What would you do if the reference's ELDRS response turned out to be much worse than the high-dose-rate test suggested?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument conflates two different fault domains. TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in the logic that processes the measurement; it does nothing for a fault that corrupts the measurement before it ever reaches the digital domain. A single-event transient on the amplifier front-end, a drift in the sensor, or a corrupted sample-and-hold can produce a plausible-looking but wrong value that TMR will faithfully replicate three times — the voter has no way to know the input was bad. So the analog chain is not "covered" by digital TMR; it's a separate single point of failure.

I'd evaluate it by asking: what is the consequence of a wrong measurement reaching the control loop, and what is the probability of an SET or drift in that chain over the mission? If the measurement is safety-critical or drives an actuator, the analog chain needs its own mitigation — a second independent signal path with comparison, a plausibility check against a redundant sensor, a rate-of-change limit, or a hardware comparator that flags out-of-range values. The mitigation doesn't have to be full analog TMR; it has to be commensurate with the criticality.

I'd also point out that the digital TMR and the analog chain are complementary, not substitutes — the right framing is "what protects this measurement end to end," not "which side is covered."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a plausibility check on an analog measurement without adding a second full signal chain? Where would you draw the line between "needs redundancy" and "needs monitoring" for an analog channel?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload where several loads share a common upstream rail, and a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not collapse the rail for the others?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up in one load must be contained at the boundary of that load, so the shared rail never sees the fault. I'd start by mapping the loads by criticality and current draw, then decide where the "shared" vs. "isolated" boundary sits. Each load gets its own protection element — a current-limiting switch, an eFuse, or a latching current limiter — sized so that it trips on a latch-up event in that load but doesn't nuisance-trip on legitimate inrush. The upstream rail then only has to supply the sum of the protected branches, and a single branch fault is absorbed locally.

The design questions are: (1) how fast does the protection need to respond before the shared rail sags enough to disturb other loads — this drives whether you need a fast electronic limiter or a slower fuse; (2) how do you recover — a latching limiter needs a controlled retry or a command to reset, and you don't want an automatic retry loop hammering a persistently latched load; (3) how do you avoid the protection itself becoming a single point of failure — a failed-short protection element defeats the whole scheme, so consider whether the protection needs redundancy for the highest-criticality loads; and (4) how do you detect and report the fault so the system can shed the load and continue.

I'd also verify the scheme against inrush: capacitive loads draw large currents at power-up, and the limiter must ride through that without tripping, which usually means a timed or foldback characteristic rather than a simple hard current limit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a latching current limiter and a resettable fuse for a given load? What would you do if a load's legitimate inrush current were close to its latch-up current?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part of this test is that the failure mode is designed to defeat the obvious detection mechanisms — the processor looks alive (current draw normal, heartbeat toggling) but the control loop is dead. So the test plan has to verify recovery from a state that a naive watchdog would miss. I'd structure it in layers.

First, define the observable signature of the SEFI: the control loop's output stops updating, or the loop's own progress counter stops advancing, while the heartbeat continues. The test needs an independent way to detect that signature — not the heartbeat, but a "control loop alive" indicator that is generated by the loop itself, such as a counter that only increments when a control iteration completes, or a checksum over the loop's state that changes each cycle.

Second, since you can't inject real radiation on the ground, use fault injection: instrument the firmware with a test hook that deliberately freezes the control loop while leaving the heartbeat running, then verify that the recovery mechanism (a supervisory watchdog that monitors the loop-progress indicator, not the heartbeat) detects it and resets or restarts the loop within the required time. Vary the injection point — freeze at different stages of the loop, freeze the loop but not the scheduler, corrupt the loop's state — to cover the range of SEFI-like states.

Third, verify the recovery itself: after the reset, does the system return to a known-good state, re-initialize the loop, and resume control without a dangerous transient on the actuator? That last part is often overlooked — a reset that recovers the processor but glitches the output can be worse than the hang.

Finally, document the coverage: which SEFI states are detected, which are not, and what the residual risk is.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the loop-progress indicator so it can't itself be fooled by a partial hang? What recovery time would you specify, and what drives that number?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing is to separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be acknowledged before anything else — I'd start by asking them to walk through their margin calculation and the assumptions behind it, because the disagreement is almost always about an assumption, not about competence. If I can find the specific assumption I think is wrong — a dose rate, a derating factor, a part's radiation response, a mission duration — then the conversation becomes about that assumption rather than about "your design is wrong."

I'd frame it as a shared question: "What would have to be true for this margin to be adequate, and how confident are we that it's true?" That turns it into an investigation we do together. If the engineer's assumption is defensible, I should be open to being wrong — confidence plus real work is a signal to check my own reasoning, not just theirs. If it isn't defensible, the evidence usually makes the case better than I can; I'd ask for the data, the test result, or the reference that supports the margin, and if it doesn't exist, that's the gap to close.

I'd also make sure the decision is documented — the margin, the assumptions, the residual risk, and who accepted it. That protects the engineer and the project, and it makes the trade-off visible to the rest of the review rather than leaving it as a personal disagreement. And I'd keep the tone on the technical question throughout: the goal is the right decision, not winning the exchange.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's assumption turned out to be defensible and your concern was unfounded? How would you handle it if the engineer remained unconvinced after the evidence was presented?
