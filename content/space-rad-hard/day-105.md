# space-rad-hard — Day 105

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload where several loads share a common upstream rail, and a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not collapse the rail for the others?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up is a local fault and must be contained locally — the shared rail must never be the path that propagates it. I'd structure the design in tiers: a primary bus feed, then a per-load protection stage, then the load itself. Each load gets its own current-limiting element — a latch-up current limiter (LCL) or an eFuse with a defined trip threshold — sized so that a latched load draws a bounded current that the upstream rail can supply without drooping below the minimum input of the healthy loads. The key sizing question is: what is the worst-case sum of one latched load plus all healthy loads, and does the upstream converter and bus impedance hold regulation under that condition? If not, the protection thresholds need tightening or the converter needs headroom.

I'd also think about the failure mode of the protection element itself. A simple fuse is a one-shot device — after it clears, that load is dead until the mission can reset it, which may be never. An auto-retry eFuse or LCL that trips, waits, and retries gives the load a chance to recover if the latch-up clears (many SELs persist only while the ion is present, though some are destructive). The trade-off is that auto-retry can oscillate if the latch is permanent, so I'd want a retry counter that eventually latches off and reports the fault.

Beyond the per-load protection, I'd add a reverse-blocking element — an ideal-diode controller or a series FET — so a latched load can't back-feed the rail or drag it down through a shared ground path. Grounding deserves explicit attention: a single-point star ground per load, or at least careful return-path planning, so a large latch current doesn't develop a ground offset that corrupts the reference for the other loads. Finally, I'd instrument each load's current and report it in telemetry, so the ground team can see which load latched and command a targeted power cycle rather than a whole-payload reset.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the upstream converter's current limit relative to the sum of all per-load limits, and what happens if two loads latch simultaneously? How would you distinguish a latch-up from an inrush event when the protection trips?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage supervisor or reset IC for a space-deployed system, given that most commercial parts are not radiation-characterized?

**Answer:** A voltage supervisor is deceptively simple — it's a comparator with a reference and a delay — but that simplicity is exactly why it's easy to under-scrutinize. The failure modes that matter are: the internal reference drifting with TID (which shifts the trip threshold), single-event transients on the comparator output causing a spurious reset, and SEL in the output stage. So I'd start by asking what the supervisor actually protects against and how much threshold drift the system can tolerate. If the supervisor's job is to hold a processor in reset until the rail is valid, a threshold that drifts by a few percent over dose may be acceptable; if it's the last line of defense against an undervoltage that could corrupt flash, it isn't.

For qualification, I'd look first for any part with published TID and SEE data — even from a different vendor's family — as a starting point, then plan a targeted test campaign. A focused test on a handful of parts at a proxy dose rate and with heavy-ion or proton exposure at the expected LET is often affordable and far more informative than datasheet extrapolation. If testing isn't feasible, I'd derate hard: choose a part whose trip threshold has margin on both sides, add an external RC filter on the sense input to suppress SETs, and consider a redundant supervisor with a different topology so a common-mode failure doesn't take out both.

I'd also consider whether the supervisor needs to be a supervisor at all. Sometimes a discrete comparator plus a rad-tolerant reference, or a supervisor function folded into a rad-hard FPGA or a microcontroller's brown-out detector with an external divider, gives more control over the radiation response than a fixed-function commercial IC. The trade-off is board space and complexity versus predictability. Whatever the choice, I'd document the radiation rationale explicitly — what data exists, what was tested, what margin was assumed — because that's what a reviewer or a customer's reliability engineer will ask for.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you test a supervisor's SET response on a bench without a beam facility? What would make you reject a supervisor that passed TID but showed SETs on the output?

## Q3: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a category error in it: TMR protects against upsets in the digital logic that processes a value, but it does nothing for an error that enters the chain before the digital domain. If the sensor, amplifier, or ADC produces a wrong value — because of a single-event transient on the amplifier output, a reference glitch, or a corrupted sample — then all three TMR replicas receive the same wrong input and vote unanimously for it. TMR gives you immunity to internal digital faults, not to a common-mode input fault. So the analog chain is not "covered"; it's a single point of failure that happens to sit upstream of the redundancy.

How I'd evaluate it depends on what the measurement feeds. If it's a slow, non-safety-critical housekeeping value, single-ended may be entirely appropriate and the engineer's instinct to avoid over-engineering is right. If it feeds a control loop that can drive an actuator to a dangerous state, I'd want at least one of: a plausibility check on the measured value (rate-of-change limits, range checks, comparison against a redundant but dissimilar sensor), a second independent signal path with voting at the digital boundary, or a hold-last-good / fail-safe strategy when the value looks implausible. The cheapest effective mitigation is often the plausibility check, because it catches the SET-induced spike without duplicating hardware.

I'd frame the review conversation around the consequence, not the topology: "What happens to the actuator if this reading is wrong for one sample?" If the answer is benign, the design stands. If it isn't, we need a detection mechanism, and the engineer's TMR argument doesn't supply one. That keeps it a technical discussion about risk rather than a critique of the engineer's work.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a plausibility check that doesn't itself become a single point of failure? If you added a second sensor, would you vote the two, and what do you do on disagreement?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant boot sequence for a space-deployed system that uses an SRAM-based FPGA with external configuration memory (flash), where both the FPGA configuration bitstream and the flash memory are susceptible to single-event upsets?

**Answer:** The boot sequence is the most fragile moment in the mission because the system has no running fault handler yet — whatever goes wrong during configuration has to be caught by something that is itself already running. So I'd design it as a layered recovery ladder with an independent, radiation-tolerant sequencer at the bottom. That sequencer — a small rad-hard CPLD, a microcontroller, or a dedicated supervisor — owns the FPGA's PROGRAM and INIT pins and the flash chip select, and it is the only thing that decides when to (re)configure.

The ladder would look like this. First, the bitstream in flash is protected by a checksum or CRC that the sequencer verifies before releasing configuration; if the CRC fails, the sequencer doesn't even try — it goes straight to a recovery image. Second, I'd keep at least two bitstream images in physically separate flash regions, ideally in different devices, so a single upset or a stuck bit in one region doesn't take out both. Third, the FPGA's own configuration error signal (INIT_B going low after DONE, or a CRC error flag) feeds back to the sequencer, which retries configuration a bounded number of times before escalating to the backup image. Fourth, after configuration, the sequencer watches a heartbeat from the FPGA; if the FPGA configures but then hangs, the sequencer can force a reconfiguration.

The subtle part is scrubbing versus rebooting. If the FPGA supports configuration scrubbing from the sequencer, a single-bit upset in the fabric can be corrected without a full reconfiguration, which is much less disruptive. But scrubbing requires the sequencer to read back the configuration and compare, which costs time and bus bandwidth. I'd use scrubbing for the steady-state case and reserve full reconfiguration for when scrubbing can't keep up or the error is in a region that isn't scrubbable. And I'd make sure the sequencer's own state machine is simple enough to be trusted — a complex recovery controller is itself a liability.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide how many retries before switching to the backup image? What would you do if the backup image also fails CRC — is there a safe mode the system can enter?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be acknowledged before anything else — I'd start by asking them to walk through their assumptions and their margin calculation, and I'd listen for whether there's something I'm missing. Sometimes the "under-margined" impression comes from a different assumption about the environment or the duty cycle, and the engineer may have a defensible reason for a tighter margin than I'd choose.

If after that walkthrough I still believe the margin is insufficient, I'd move the conversation from opinion to criteria. I'd ask: what is the worst-case radiation environment we're designing to, what data supports the part's behavior in that environment, and what is the consequence if the margin is consumed? Putting those three on the whiteboard turns "I think it's too tight" into "here is the number we're comparing against and here is what happens if we're wrong." If the engineer's analysis holds up against those criteria, I should be persuaded — that's the point of the review. If it doesn't, the gap is now visible to everyone in the room, not just asserted by me.

If we still disagree, I'd avoid resolving it by seniority in the room. I'd propose a concrete next step that produces evidence: a targeted test, a worst-case simulation, a request for the vendor's radiation data, or a derating calculation done independently by a third person. That gives the engineer a path to prove the design and gives the project a defensible basis for the decision either way. And I'd be explicit that if the evidence comes back in the engineer's favor, I'll say so publicly — because the credibility of the review process depends on it being a search for the right answer, not a ratification of the lead's opinion. Throughout, I'd keep the language on the design and the data, never on the engineer's competence.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the schedule doesn't allow time for the test you proposed? How do you make sure a junior engineer feels able to push back on you in the next review?
