# space-rad-hard — Day 92

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** I'd start by treating the reference as the accuracy anchor of the whole chain, so its behavior under TID and SETs has to be bounded before anything downstream matters. With no radiation data, the honest position is that the part is unqualified, so I'd work through three layers. First, characterize or bound it: either fund a low-cost TID and heavy-ion screen at a facility, or, if budget doesn't allow, derive a worst-case drift budget from the datasheet's temperature coefficient and long-term stability and add a conservative radiation allowance — then check whether the resulting error still fits the measurement's error budget. If it doesn't, the part is out. Second, architect around it: a ratiometric measurement topology where the ADC converts relative to the same reference that excites the sensor cancels much of the reference drift, because the drift appears in both numerator and denominator. Where ratiometric isn't possible, I'd add a second, independent reference of a different technology and cross-check them periodically — divergence beyond a threshold flags a reference problem rather than silently corrupting data. Third, protect against SETs specifically: a transient on the reference is a fast event, so I'd add RC filtering at the reference output sized against the ADC's sampling current, plus digital plausibility checks — rate-of-change limits and comparison against a redundant channel — so a single spurious conversion can't drive a control action. Finally, I'd design in an in-flight calibration path: a stable internal standard or a commanded calibration against a known input, scheduled often enough that accumulated drift is always correctable, and I'd log reference health telemetry so degradation is visible on the ground rather than discovered as an anomaly.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish reference drift from sensor drift in flight, given both show up as a slow change in the reading? What would make you reject the ratiometric approach for a given sensor?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has proposed using a single commercial LDO with no radiation data to post-regulate a critical analog rail, arguing that the upstream DC-DC is already rad-tolerant so "the LDO doesn't matter." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a kernel of truth — the upstream converter sets the bulk of the rail's radiation behavior — but it inverts the risk logic. The LDO is the last element in the chain, so anything it does lands directly on the analog load with no further regulation to absorb it. Three failure modes matter. TID: the LDO's reference and error amplifier drift with dose, and because it's a closed-loop regulator, drift shows up as a slow output shift that the downstream ADC sees as gain error — the upstream converter being rad-tolerant does nothing about that. SETs: a transient on the LDO's internal reference or pass element propagates almost unattenuated, and the LDO's loop bandwidth may actually slow recovery compared to a passive filter. SEL: a COTS LDO is a classic latch-up candidate, and if it latches it can drag the analog rail down or crowbar the upstream converter. So my evaluation would be: what is the analog load's tolerance to a slow rail shift over mission life, what is its tolerance to a fast transient, and what happens to the rest of the system if this part latches? If the answers are tight, the LDO needs either radiation data, a rad-tolerant replacement, or architectural mitigation — a clamp, current limiting, and a monitored rail so a latched LDO can be power-cycled independently. I'd frame the review comment not as "you're wrong" but as "the upstream part doesn't cover the downstream part's failure modes — show me the analysis for each."

**Possible follow-ups:** If a rad-tolerant LDO isn't available in the package you need, what would you do instead? How would you test a candidate LDO's SET response without a full radiation campaign?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant I²C bus for a space-deployed system where multiple sensor nodes share the same bus, given that single-event upsets can corrupt data or cause bus lock-ups?

**Answer:** I²C is a poor fit for a fault-tolerant bus by nature — open-drain, clock-stretching, and a state machine in every node that can wedge — so the design has to assume corruption and lock-up will happen and make them recoverable rather than prevent them entirely. At the protocol level: every transaction carries a CRC or at least a checksum plus a sequence number, so a corrupted payload is detected rather than acted on; commands are idempotent or explicitly acknowledged so a retry is safe; and I'd avoid relying on clock stretching, which is a common lock-up source. At the electrical level: series resistors and bus capacitance kept within spec so edges stay clean, and a bus-recovery mechanism — a supervisor or the master itself that can clock out up to nine pulses and issue a STOP to free a slave holding SDA low, then re-initiate. At the architecture level: I'd segment the bus rather than hang everything on one pair of wires — a bus switch or multiplexer per group means a wedged node takes out its segment, not the whole system, and the master can isolate and power-cycle the offending segment. I'd also add a heartbeat or presence check per node so a silently dead node is detected, and I'd consider whether the critical path really needs to share a bus at all — a point-to-point link or a differential bus like RS485 or CAN-FD may be the better answer for anything control-critical, with I²C reserved for housekeeping.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide which nodes get their own segment versus sharing one? What's your recovery sequence if the master itself is the node that wedged?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** I'd decompose this into three problems — combining the feeds, isolating faults, and managing inrush — and solve each explicitly rather than letting one circuit try to do all three. For combining: ideal-diode ORing with controlled forward conduction, so either feed can carry the load alone and a failure of one feed doesn't back-drive the other; the ORing elements need to be radiation-characterized or at least derated with margin, since they sit in the main current path. For fault isolation: each downstream load gets its own current-limited switch or eFuse with a latch-up detection threshold set above legitimate inrush but below the feed's capability, so a latched load is disconnected locally and the rail survives — and the switch needs a retry policy, because a transient SEL may clear while a hard short won't, so I'd want counted retries with backoff and a telemetry flag rather than blind auto-retry. For hot-swap: inrush limiting on each card — a hot-swap controller with a controlled dV/dt ramp and a SOA-analyzed pass FET — sized so plugging a card doesn't droop the 28V bus below the other loads' minimum. I'd also add bus transient suppression at the input, because a 28V spacecraft bus carries inductive kickback from other loads, and I'd verify the whole thing against MIL-STD-461 EMI limits since the switching elements are noise sources. The key review discipline is a fault tree: for each single failure — feed shorted, load latched, card hot-plugged — trace what happens to every other load and confirm the answer is "nothing."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set the latch-up detection threshold when the load's inrush current is close to its fault current? What happens if both feeds are lost simultaneously — is there a hold-up requirement?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first move is to separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real analysis, so the review should start by acknowledging what's solid and asking them to walk through their margin calculation, not by announcing a verdict. Then I'd make the disagreement concrete and quantitative rather than a clash of opinions: what environment are we designing to, what's the worst-case number for this parameter, what's the load's limit, and what's the gap? Often the disagreement dissolves once both of us are looking at the same table — either I've missed something in their analysis, or the gap becomes visible to them. If it's still a genuine disagreement, I'd ask them to state the failure mode they're accepting and its consequence: "if this part drifts or upsets, what does the system do, and is that acceptable for this function?" That reframes it from "seniority says no" to "the risk owner has to own this specific outcome," which is a much better conversation. I'd also bring in evidence where I can — a datasheet limit, a prior test result, a standard's derating curve — because data persuades where assertion doesn't. If we still can't converge, I'd escalate the decision transparently rather than quietly overrule: document both positions, take it to whoever owns the risk, and make sure the engineer knows their analysis was heard and recorded. And I'd follow up afterward — a review that ends in a decision the engineer doesn't agree with still has to leave them willing to bring the next proposal forward.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's proposal turns out to be right and yours was the under-margined one? How do you keep a review from becoming adversarial when the schedule is tight and the disagreement is real?
