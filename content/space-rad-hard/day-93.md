# space-rad-hard — Day 93

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is often the weakest link in a precision measurement chain, because any drift in the reference appears directly as a gain error in every reading — it cannot be averaged or filtered out. With a COTS reference and no radiation data, I would treat the part as an unknown and design around that uncertainty rather than assume it behaves.

First, I would characterize what I can. Even without a beam line, I can measure initial accuracy, temperature coefficient, long-term drift, and — critically — low-dose-rate sensitivity if the budget allows a cobalt-60 exposure at a few representative dose points. ELDRS is the specific concern for bipolar references: some parts that look fine at high dose rate degrade badly at the low dose rates actually seen in orbit, so a high-dose-rate-only test can give false confidence.

Second, I would architect the measurement so the reference's absolute value matters less. Options include ratiometric measurement where the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, so drift cancels; periodic in-flight calibration against a more stable secondary standard (a buried-Zener or a rad-hard reference used only as a calibration check, not as the primary); and storing calibration coefficients in non-volatile memory that can be refreshed from the ground.

Third, I would add margin and monitoring. If the reference drifts, I want telemetry that reveals it — for example, a redundant reference on a separate die or package that can be compared periodically, with a divergence flag raised to the ground. If the two references agree, confidence is high; if they diverge beyond a threshold, the measurement is flagged as degraded rather than silently wrong.

Finally, I would document the residual risk explicitly in the radiation analysis: the part is uncharacterized, here is the mitigation, here is the monitoring, here is the worst-case error budget assuming the reference drifts by X. That way the decision is visible to the reviewer rather than buried.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish reference drift from sensor drift if both are uncharacterized? What would make you decide the COTS reference is simply unacceptable and switch to a rad-hard part?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a category error in it: TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in the digital logic, but it does nothing for a fault that originates in the analog front-end. If the amplifier latches up, or the ADC produces a spurious code, or the sensor connection opens, the TMR'd digital logic will faithfully vote on three copies of the same wrong input. Redundancy downstream of a single point of failure does not remove that failure.

I would walk through the failure modes of the analog chain and ask which ones the digital TMR actually catches. A single-event transient on the amplifier output that is shorter than the ADC sampling interval may be filtered out by the sampling itself — good. But a transient that lands in the sample-and-hold, or an SET that shifts the amplifier's bias point for milliseconds, will be captured as a valid-looking reading and passed into the control loop. TMR cannot tell a wrong-but-plausible value from a right one.

The practical mitigations depend on the criticality of the loop. For a measurement that feeds a safety-relevant control action, I would want at least one of: a plausibility check on the reading (rate-of-change limits, range checks, comparison against a redundant sensor on a different channel), a second independent signal path so the two can be compared, or a hold-last-good strategy with a timeout that flags the measurement as stale. For a truly critical loop, a fully redundant analog chain — separate amplifier, separate ADC channel, ideally separate reference — is the honest answer, and the cost has to be weighed against the consequence of a wrong control action.

I would also point out that "the digital side has TMR" is not a substitute for a failure modes analysis of the analog side. The right framing is: what is the probability and consequence of each analog failure mode, and what detection or mitigation covers it? That analysis may conclude the single-ended chain is acceptable for a non-critical housekeeping measurement — but that conclusion has to be reached deliberately, not by assuming digital redundancy covers it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a plausibility check without adding so much latency that the control loop becomes sluggish? If you added a second analog channel, how would you decide which one to trust when they disagree?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up in one load must be contained at the branch level, not allowed to propagate upstream and collapse the shared rail. That means each load — or each group of loads at the same criticality — gets its own protection element between it and the distribution bus, sized so that the protection trips on a latch-up current before the bus voltage sags enough to disturb the other loads.

The protection element is usually a current-limited switch or a fuse-plus-limiting combination. The design tension is that the trip threshold has to be above the load's legitimate worst-case inrush and operating current, but below the current at which the latch-up would drag the bus down. That window can be tight for high-inrush loads like capacitive input filters, so I would characterize the inrush carefully and consider active inrush limiting (a hot-swap controller with a controlled ramp) rather than relying on a slow-blow fuse that has to be sized generously.

Criticality tiers matter. Loads that are essential to the mission — the control processor, the telemetry transmitter — should be on a branch that is either unswitched or switched with a latch-up current limit that is generous enough never to nuisance-trip, and ideally fed from a separate regulator or a separate feed so a fault in a non-critical branch cannot affect them. Non-critical or high-risk loads (motors, high-power actuators, anything with a history of SEL susceptibility) go on branches with tighter protection and, where possible, the ability to be power-cycled independently to clear a latch.

I would also add monitoring: a current-sense signal per branch so the system can detect a latch-up event, log it, and command a power cycle of just that branch rather than the whole payload. And I would verify the scheme against the actual load characteristics — inrush, steady-state, and the expected latch current — rather than against datasheet typicals.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a load whose legitimate inrush current is close to its latch-up current? What would you do if a latch-up occurs in a load that shares a regulator with a critical load and cannot be independently switched?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part of this test is that the failure mode is defined by what the processor is *not* doing, while the obvious health signals — current draw, heartbeat toggle — still look normal. So the test plan has to be built around an independent observer that can tell the difference between "alive and working" and "alive but stuck," and around a way to induce the stuck state without a beam.

For fault injection, since I usually cannot put the system in a beam during ground test, I would use a debugger or a test harness to force the processor into representative stuck states: halt the core in a tight loop, disable the control-loop task while leaving the heartbeat task running, corrupt the control-loop state variable, or mask the interrupt that drives the loop. Each of these is a different flavor of SEFI and each should be tested separately, because the recovery mechanism may catch some and miss others.

The independent observer is the key design element. A heartbeat GPIO alone is insufficient — the whole point is that the heartbeat can keep toggling while the loop is dead. So the observer needs a signal that is causally tied to the control loop actually running: a counter that the loop increments, a watchdog that the loop must service (not the heartbeat task), or a challenge-response where the observer asks the processor to compute something derived from current sensor data and checks the answer against an independent model. The recovery logic — external watchdog, windowed watchdog, or a supervisor that checks the loop counter — must be tested against each injected fault to confirm it actually trips and actually recovers.

I would also test the recovery path itself: after the watchdog fires, does the system reinitialize cleanly, restore safe outputs, and resume the loop within the required time? And I would test the boundary cases — a fault that occurs during initialization, a fault that recurs immediately after recovery (does the system enter a reset loop?), and a fault that occurs while the system is already in a degraded state.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the watchdog so it catches a stuck control loop but does not nuisance-trip during legitimate long operations? What would you do if the recovery mechanism itself is susceptible to the same SEFI?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I would do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be taken seriously — dismissing it out of hand damages both the review and the engineer's willingness to bring forward work in future. So I would start by making sure I understand their reasoning: what margin did they assume, what radiation environment did they design to, what failure modes did they consider, and what data are they relying on. Sometimes the disagreement dissolves once I see the assumption they made that I did not.

If the disagreement is real, I would try to make it concrete rather than positional. "Under-margined" is an opinion until it is quantified. So I would ask: what is the worst-case condition, what is the margin to the limit, and what happens if the margin is consumed? If we can agree on the numbers, the decision often becomes obvious to both of us. If we cannot agree on the numbers, that itself is the finding — we need more data, and the action is to get it rather than to argue.

I would also be explicit about the consequence asymmetry. A design that is under-margined for a benign environment may be fine; the same design in a harsh environment may fail in a way that is expensive or impossible to recover from on orbit. Framing the decision in terms of consequence, not just probability, usually moves the conversation from "I think it's fine" to "what would we need to believe for it to be fine, and do we believe it?"

Finally, I would make sure the decision is documented — the concern, the engineer's reasoning, the resolution, and the residual risk — so that if the under-margin later causes a problem, the review record shows it was considered. And I would follow up with the engineer afterward to make sure the outcome did not feel like a verdict on their competence. The goal is a design that survives the environment, not a review that the engineer loses.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis is correct and it is *your* assumption that is wrong? How would you handle it if the engineer escalates the disagreement rather than accepting the review outcome?
