# space-rad-hard — Day 44

## Q1: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant analog multiplexing scheme for a space-deployed system where a single-event transient (SET) on the mux select lines could route the wrong sensor channel to a precision ADC, potentially causing an incorrect control action?

**Answer:** The core problem is that a single-bit upset in the mux address can silently redirect a measurement from the intended sensor to an entirely different one, and if that measurement feeds a control loop, the system could act on data that is valid but from the wrong source. My approach would start at the system level: classify which sensor channels are safety-critical and which are merely informational. For critical channels, I would not rely on a single mux stage. I would consider redundant measurement paths—for example, dedicating a separate ADC input or a second mux channel to the most critical sensors, so that even if the primary path is misrouted, the control loop can cross-check against the redundant path. At the circuit level, I would add error-detection coding on the mux address lines themselves. If the mux supports it, I would use a Gray-coded or Hamming-coded address so that a single-bit upset produces either a detectable invalid address or an adjacent-channel error that can be flagged. Alternatively, I would drive the select lines from a register that is protected with triple-modular redundancy (TMR) or ECC, and I would verify the register contents before each conversion. In firmware, I would not blindly trust a single sample. I would implement a plausibility check: before acting on a measurement, compare it against the previous value and against a model of the expected signal range and slew rate. If a sample is wildly inconsistent with the expected channel's characteristics, flag it as suspect and either re-sample or switch to a safe default. I would also periodically inject a known calibration voltage through the mux as a self-test, verifying that the channel routing is correct. Finally, I would consider the control action itself: for any safety-critical actuator command, require two independent measurements to agree before acting, or require a confirmation sample after a short delay. This prevents a single transient from causing a dangerous output.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a genuine sensor reading and a misrouted channel in firmware, without creating false alarms? What if the mux itself is radiation-hardened but the address decoder is internal to the part—how does that change your mitigation strategy?

## Q2: You are reviewing a design for a space-deployed system where a junior engineer has proposed using a single commercial voltage regulator with no radiation data for a critical analog rail, arguing that "the rail is only 5V and the load is only 50mA, so the risk is minimal." You've explained the risks of TID drift, SETs, and SEL, but the engineer pushes back, saying that the system has a calibration routine and the ADC can tolerate brief supply transients. How would you handle this continued disagreement, and how would you ensure the design review process remains constructive rather than adversarial?

**Answer:** When an engineer continues to push back after the technical risks have been explained, it usually means one of three things: they don't fully appreciate the failure mechanisms, they believe the system-level mitigations genuinely cover the risk, or they are concerned about schedule and part availability. My first step would be to move from abstract risk discussion to concrete analysis. I would ask the engineer to walk through the specific failure modes and their system-level consequences. For example, if the regulator experiences TID-induced output drift, what is the ADC's actual accuracy requirement, and how much drift can the calibration routine correct? Calibration typically corrects offset and gain errors at a point in time—it does not correct for drift that occurs between calibrations, nor does it correct for transient events that occur during a measurement. I would ask the engineer to quantify: what is the expected TID at the regulator's location, what is the part's estimated drift at that dose, and does the ADC's reference and signal chain have enough headroom to tolerate that drift while still meeting the mission's accuracy requirement? For SETs, I would ask: what is the expected transient magnitude and duration on the output, and does the ADC's power supply rejection ratio (PSRR) actually reject transients at that frequency, or only at DC and low frequency? For SEL, I would ask: what happens if the regulator latches up and draws excessive current—does the upstream power distribution have a current limit that will clear the fault, or will it take down the entire rail? If the engineer can answer these questions with data and analysis, then perhaps the risk is acceptable and we can document the rationale. If they cannot, then the part should not be used without additional testing or a qualified alternative. To keep the review constructive, I would frame this as a shared problem: "Help me understand the analysis that shows this is safe" rather than "You are wrong." I would also suggest a middle path: use the commercial part for a prototype or an engineering model, but qualify a radiation-tolerant alternative for the flight unit. This preserves schedule while managing risk. If the disagreement persists, I would escalate to the systems engineer or project lead with a written risk assessment, clearly stating the uncertainty and the potential mission impact, and let the program make an informed decision.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific radiation data would you require before accepting a COTS part for a critical analog rail? How would you structure a limited budget radiation test to get the most useful data from a single test campaign?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant firmware update strategy for a space-deployed system where the application firmware resides in flash memory that is susceptible to single-event upsets, and a corrupted update could render the system unrecoverable?

**Answer:** The fundamental requirement is that a failed or corrupted update must never leave the system in a state where it cannot boot and cannot retry. This drives three design principles: redundancy, atomicity, and validation. For redundancy, I would use at least two independent firmware images: a primary application image and a fallback or "golden" image that is known-good and immutable, or at least write-protected at the hardware level. The bootloader would be stored in a separate, more robust memory—ideally radiation-hardened or at least protected by strong ECC—and would be responsible for selecting which image to boot. For atomicity, the update process must never partially overwrite the active image. I would write the new image to the inactive bank first, verify it completely, and only then flip a boot flag to point to the new image. If the write or verification fails at any point, the boot flag remains unchanged and the system continues to boot the old, working image. For validation, the bootloader must verify the integrity of the image before executing it. This means a cryptographic signature (to prevent corruption and tampering) and a checksum or hash (to detect bit flips). The verification must be performed on every boot, not just after an update, because the flash can be corrupted by radiation at any time. If the primary image fails verification, the bootloader falls back to the golden image. If the golden image also fails—which would be a serious event—the bootloader should enter a minimal "safe mode" that can accept a new update over the command link. I would also consider the update transport itself. The uplink can corrupt the data, so the update protocol should include packet-level error detection and retransmission. The system should also be able to resume an interrupted update rather than restarting from scratch. Finally, I would add a watchdog at the bootloader level: if the newly updated application fails to start or crashes within a short window, the bootloader should automatically revert to the previous image. This "boot-once" flag mechanism is critical—it catches the case where the image passes static verification but fails dynamically.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the case where the flash memory itself is so degraded by total ionizing dose that it can no longer reliably store an image? What is the trade-off between storing the golden image in the same flash device versus a separate, more robust memory?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant clock distribution network for a space-deployed system that uses multiple FPGAs and ADCs requiring synchronized sampling?

**Answer:** Synchronized sampling across multiple ADCs and FPGAs requires that all devices share a common time base with controlled skew, and that a single-event upset in the clock path does not cause loss of synchronization or, worse, a metastable condition that corrupts data. My approach would start with the clock source. I would use a single, radiation-tolerant master oscillator—ideally a crystal oscillator or a TCXO with known radiation performance—rather than multiple independent oscillators that could drift apart. The master clock would be distributed through a fan-out buffer with low skew between outputs. The buffer itself should be radiation-tolerant, and I would consider using redundant buffers with a voting scheme at the receivers, so that a single buffer failure or upset does not take down the entire clock tree. For the distribution topology, I would prefer a star configuration over a daisy chain: each FPGA and ADC gets its own dedicated clock line from the buffer, which minimizes skew and prevents a single failed load from affecting others. On the PCB, the clock traces should be impedance-controlled and length-matched to meet the skew budget, and they should be routed with proper termination to avoid reflections. At the receiver side, each FPGA should have its own PLL or clock management unit to clean up the clock and generate internal timing. The PLLs should be monitored: if a PLL loses lock, the FPGA should flag an error and, if necessary, initiate a resynchronization sequence. For the ADCs, I would use a shared sample clock rather than deriving sampling from each ADC's internal oscillator. The sample clock should be distributed with matched delay to all ADCs so that they sample at the same instant. If the system requires even tighter synchronization, I would add a periodic sync pulse or frame-alignment signal that all devices use to reset their sample counters. This allows the system to detect and correct for any clock skew that accumulates over time. In firmware, I would implement a synchronization state machine: each FPGA monitors its sample counter and compares it to a known reference. If a discrepancy is detected, the system can issue a global sync pulse to realign all devices. Finally, I would consider the failure modes of the clock tree itself. A single-event transient on a clock line can cause a spurious edge that shifts the sample timing by one cycle. To detect this, I would add a parity or frame-check mechanism in the data stream: each sample frame includes a sequence number, and the receiver can detect if samples are missing or duplicated. If a glitch is detected, the system can flag the data as suspect and resynchronize.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the trade-off between a single master clock (single point of failure) and multiple redundant clocks (complexity and skew management)? What specific parameters would you specify for the clock buffer to ensure it meets the system's jitter and skew requirements?

## Q5: Imagine you are leading a design review for a space-deployed system where a junior engineer has proposed using a single commercial voltage regulator with no radiation data for a critical analog rail, arguing that "the rail is only 5V and the load is only 50mA, so the risk is minimal." You've explained the risks of TID drift, SETs, and SEL, but the engineer pushes back, saying that the system has a calibration routine and the ADC can tolerate brief supply transients. How would you handle this continued disagreement, and how would you ensure the design review process remains constructive rather than adversarial?

**Answer:** This scenario is fundamentally about risk ownership and decision quality, not just the technical merits of the part. When an engineer continues to argue after the risks have been explained, I would shift the conversation from "is this part acceptable?" to "what is the evidence that this part is acceptable for this specific application, and who is accountable if it fails?" I would ask the engineer to produce a written risk assessment that addresses each failure mechanism quantitatively. For TID, that means estimating the mission dose at the part's location and either finding test data for the specific part or bounding the worst-case drift from similar parts. For SETs, that means characterizing the expected transient on the output—amplitude and duration—and showing that the downstream ADC and signal chain can tolerate it without producing an out-of-spec measurement. For SEL, that means demonstrating that the upstream power distribution will clear the fault without damaging the part or taking down the rail. If the engineer cannot produce this analysis, then the part is not qualified for flight, regardless of how confident they feel. If they can produce the analysis and it shows the risk is acceptable, then I would accept it with documented rationale and a formal waiver. To keep the process constructive, I would avoid making it personal. I would frame the review as a shared effort to reduce mission risk: "Our job is not to prove each other wrong; it is to make sure this system survives the environment." I would also offer a concrete path forward: if the engineer believes the part is safe, they can propose a limited radiation test—even a single heavy-ion run or a TID test to a fraction of the mission dose—to gather data. This turns the disagreement into a testable hypothesis. If budget or schedule prevents testing, then the conservative choice is to use a qualified part. Finally, I would ensure the decision is documented in the design review minutes, including the dissenting opinion and the rationale for the final decision. This protects the team and the program if the part later fails.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific criteria would you use to decide whether a COTS part with no radiation data can be accepted with a waiver versus requiring a qualified part? How would you handle a situation where the only qualified alternative is significantly larger, heavier, and more expensive, and the program is mass-constrained?
