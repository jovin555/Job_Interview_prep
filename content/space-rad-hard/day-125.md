# space-rad-hard — Day 125

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is the accuracy anchor for the whole conversion chain, so a COTS reference with no radiation data is a real risk — not because it will fail outright, but because its output can drift under total ionizing dose and it can glitch under single-event transients. I'd approach it in layers. First, characterize or bound the risk: either get the part into a low-dose-rate and high-dose-rate test (to catch ELDRS behavior, which is often the dominant failure mode for references), or, if budget doesn't allow, treat the datasheet drift as unknown and design the architecture so the reference's absolute value matters less. That means ratiometric measurement where possible — measure the sensor against the same reference the ADC uses, so common-mode reference drift cancels — and a periodic calibration path against a more stable, characterized artifact (a rad-hard reference, or an on-board calibration standard measured at known intervals). Second, add margin: specify the ADC and signal chain so that the worst-case reference drift over the mission still leaves the measurement within tolerance, and derate the reference's initial accuracy and tempco budget to leave room for radiation drift. Third, mitigate transients: filter and buffer the reference output, and in firmware reject or flag samples that fall outside a plausibility window so a single SET spike doesn't propagate into a control action. Finally, plan for in-flight health monitoring — compare the reference against a second, independent reference or a bandgap-derived check, and trend the difference over time so drift is observable rather than silent. The key judgment is that "no radiation data" doesn't mean "unusable," but it does mean you must not rely on the reference's absolute accuracy as a given; you design around it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether low-dose-rate testing is worth the cost versus just adding a calibration channel? What would you do if the reference's drift turned out to be non-monotonic with dose?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has proposed using a single commercial LDO with no radiation data to post-regulate a critical analog rail, arguing that the upstream DC-DC is already rad-tolerant so "the LDO doesn't matter." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a kernel of truth — the upstream converter being rad-tolerant removes one failure mode — but it conflates "the input is clean" with "the regulator is safe." The LDO is a active device with its own radiation exposure, and it can fail in ways the upstream converter can't protect against: total-dose-induced output drift or dropout-voltage increase, single-event transients on the pass transistor or bandgap, and single-event latch-up drawing excessive current. If the LDO latches, it can drag the analog rail down regardless of how good the DC-DC is, and if it drifts, the analog measurement accuracy degrades silently. So I'd evaluate it on three axes. First, criticality: is this rail feeding a precision measurement or a control loop where a drift or glitch causes a wrong action? If yes, the "it doesn't matter" claim is weakest exactly where it matters most. Second, failure containment: does the design detect and recover from an LDO latch or drift — current limiting, a supervisor on the rail, a way to power-cycle just that regulator? Third, alternatives: is there a characterized rad-tolerant LDO or a discrete pass-element approach that fits the current and dropout budget? I'd frame the review not as "you're wrong" but as "show me the failure-mode analysis for this part under dose and SEE, and show me how the system detects and recovers if it drifts or latches." If the engineer can't produce that, the burden is on the design to add detection and margin, or to swap the part. The teaching point is that "upstream is protected" is not the same as "downstream is protected."

**Possible follow-ups:** What detection would you add around a COTS LDO on a critical analog rail? How would you decide between a rad-tolerant LDO and a discrete regulator built from characterized parts?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant I²C bus for a space-deployed system where multiple sensor nodes share the same bus, given that single-event upsets can corrupt data or cause bus lock-ups?

**Answer:** I²C is a shared, open-drain bus with a clock and data line, and its failure modes under radiation are well known: a bit flip in a transaction corrupts a reading, and a stuck or glitched line can hold SCL or SDA low, locking the bus so no node can communicate. I'd design in layers. At the protocol level, add integrity: CRC or checksum on every payload, sequence numbers or transaction IDs so a corrupted or duplicated message is detectable, and timeouts so a node that doesn't respond is treated as failed rather than waited on forever. At the electrical level, add bus recovery: a dedicated recovery mechanism that detects a stuck line and issues the standard I²C bus-clear sequence (clock pulses on SCL until SDA releases, then a STOP), driven either by the master firmware or by a small supervisor. At the topology level, consider segmenting the bus — an I²C mux or switch — so a fault on one segment doesn't take down the whole bus, and so a locked node can be isolated. At the node level, make each sensor's interface tolerant: series resistors and filtering to limit glitch energy, and a watchdog on each node so a hung node resets itself rather than holding the bus. Finally, decide the recovery policy: how many retries before declaring a node failed, and whether the system can operate degraded with a subset of sensors. The design principle is that a shared bus is a single point of failure unless you add detection, isolation, and recovery — you can't make I²C immune to SEUs, but you can make the system survive them.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement the bus-clear sequence if the master itself is the node that's hung? What are the trade-offs of adding an I²C mux versus keeping a single flat bus?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part of SEFI testing is that the failure is invisible to the usual health signals — the processor is alive, the heartbeat is toggling, current draw looks normal, but the control loop has stopped. So the test plan has to detect "not executing the loop" rather than "not powered" or "not toggling." I'd structure it around fault injection rather than waiting for real radiation. First, define the observable that proves the loop is running: a counter or sequence number that only advances when the control loop completes an iteration, distinct from the heartbeat GPIO. Second, build injection hooks in firmware — a debug or test mode that can deliberately halt the loop, corrupt the program counter, or stall a task — so you can reproduce the SEFI state on the bench without a beam. Third, define the recovery mechanism under test: an external watchdog that monitors the loop-completion counter (not just the heartbeat), a supervisor that can reset the processor, and a boot path that restores safe state. Fourth, write the test cases: inject the stall, verify the watchdog fires within the specified window, verify the reset brings the system back to a known-safe state, verify the control loop resumes and the counter advances, and verify no unsafe output occurred during the recovery window. Fifth, run it repeatedly and across conditions — cold, hot, low voltage — because recovery timing can vary. Finally, if a beam is available, use it to confirm the injected-fault results correlate with real SEFI behavior, but the bench injection is what gives you repeatable coverage. The principle is: test the detection and recovery path, not just the failure.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the watchdog so it catches a stalled loop but doesn't false-trigger during normal long operations? What would you do if the recovery itself could produce a transient unsafe output?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing is to separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves respect, because dismissing it outright shuts down the exact behavior you want on a team. I'd start by asking them to walk through their margin calculation and the assumptions behind it, and I'd listen for where the disagreement actually lives: is it the radiation environment spec, the part data, the derating policy, or the failure-mode coverage? Often the gap is a missing assumption rather than a wrong conclusion. Then I'd make the concern concrete and shared: put the numbers on the board — what dose, what SEE rate, what the part's characterized or uncharacterized behavior is, what the worst-case stack-up looks like — so the discussion is about the data, not about who's right. If the part has no radiation data, I'd frame it as "we can't claim margin we can't substantiate," and ask what evidence would change either of our minds — that turns it into a joint investigation rather than a verdict. I'd also separate "this is wrong" from "this needs a documented risk decision": sometimes the right outcome is to accept the risk with a written rationale and a mitigation, and that's a legitimate engineering answer if it's made explicitly rather than by omission. Throughout, I'd keep the review's job clear — the review's purpose is to surface risk, not to win. If we still disagree after the data is on the table, I'd escalate to the documented risk-acceptance process rather than pulling rank in the room, and I'd follow up with the engineer afterward so the relationship isn't collateral damage. The measure of success is that the right decision gets made and the engineer is still engaged.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis was actually correct and your concern was the one that was under-informed? How do you make sure a "risk accepted with rationale" decision doesn't become a habit that erodes margin over time?
