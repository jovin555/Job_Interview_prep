# space-rad-hard — Day 114

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** I'd start by treating the two feeds as genuinely independent sources that are only combined at the point of load, not upstream. Each feed gets its own input protection stage — reverse-polarity protection, a fuse or eFuse with current limiting, and transient suppression — so a fault on one feed can't propagate back onto the other bus or onto the shared load rail. The two conditioned feeds then feed an ORing stage: either ideal-diode ORing controllers or a diode-OR with low-forward-drop devices, so the load draws from whichever feed is healthy and the transition is transparent. The key design decision is where to put the current limit: it needs to be fast enough to catch a hard short before the bus sags, but not so aggressive that it trips on legitimate inrush.

For hot-swap, I'd use a hot-swap controller with programmable dV/dt ramp on the gate of the pass FET, plus a sense resistor for current-limit and a timer for fault blanking. The ramp controls inrush into the downstream bulk capacitance so the 28V bus doesn't see a step; the blanking timer lets the controller ride through a legitimate startup surge without latching off. I'd also add a soft-start on the downstream DC-DC so the two inrush events don't stack. On the ORing side, I'd want the controller to distinguish a latched load (persistent overcurrent) from a transient, so it can isolate the faulted feed and keep the other one serving the load rather than both shutting down. Finally, I'd verify the whole thing at the bench with a real short applied to one feed under load, and with a hot-plug event on the other, watching the bus for droop and ringing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a fuse and an active eFuse for the input protection, given the reliability and single-point-failure implications? What would you do about the ORing diode's reverse-recovery behavior during a feed dropout?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage supervisor or reset IC for a space-deployed system, given that most commercial parts are not radiation-characterized?

**Answer:** I'd treat the supervisor as a small but safety-relevant part and reason about it in three layers: what it must do functionally, what radiation can do to it, and how much I can verify within budget. Functionally, the supervisor has to hold its threshold accuracy over temperature and life, assert reset reliably at power-up, and not false-trigger on normal supply ripple. That's a datasheet exercise first — threshold tolerance, hysteresis, and reset timeout.

The radiation question is harder because most commercial supervisors have no TID or SEE data. I'd start by looking for any part with published TID data, even if it's not QML — a part with characterized behavior is worth more than an uncharacterized one with better specs. If nothing qualified exists, I'd consider a discrete implementation: a precision reference plus a comparator plus a delay, where each element can be individually characterized or chosen from parts with known radiation behavior. That trades integration for transparency. For the SEE side, the concern is a transient on the reset output or a threshold shift causing a spurious reset — so I'd want the supervisor's output to be filtered or debounced, and I'd want the system to tolerate a spurious reset gracefully (safe state, fast recovery) rather than treat reset as catastrophic.

For qualification within a limited budget, I'd prioritize a TID step-stress test at a few doses to see where the threshold moves, and a heavy-ion or proton run at a single fluence to look for destructive or functional events on the reset line. If budget doesn't allow even that, I'd document the risk explicitly, add margin to the threshold window, and design the downstream logic so a supervisor failure is detectable — for example, a heartbeat check that catches a stuck reset line.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the test matrix if you could only afford one beam run? What's your fallback if the supervisor's threshold drifts enough at end-of-life to sit inside the supply's normal ripple band?

## Q3: You are reviewing a design for a space-deployed system that uses a radiation-hardened FPGA with internal block RAM for critical telemetry storage. The designer has implemented SEC-DED ECC on the block RAM but has not implemented scrubbing. How would you evaluate this approach, and what would you recommend?

**Answer:** SEC-DED corrects single-bit errors and detects double-bit errors, which is a solid baseline, but without scrubbing the correction is only as good as the read rate. The problem is error accumulation: a corrected single-bit error is fixed in the read data path but the stored word still holds the flipped bit unless it's written back. If that same word takes a second hit before it's next read, you now have an uncorrectable double-bit error — and the probability of that grows with mission duration and with how rarely each word is touched. Telemetry storage is exactly the kind of data that sits idle for long stretches, so it's the worst case for unscrubbed ECC.

I'd recommend adding a background scrubber that walks the block RAM continuously, reads each word, and writes back corrected data. The design questions are the scrub rate and the arbitration. The scrub rate should be set so the mean time between hits on a given word is much longer than the scrub period — you want to sweep the whole array well inside the expected upset interval. Arbitration matters because the scrubber competes with functional reads and writes; I'd give functional access priority and let the scrubber use idle cycles, or time-slice it. I'd also want the scrubber to log corrected-error counts, because a rising count is an early warning of a degrading cell or a higher-than-expected environment. And I'd want the double-bit-error path to be a real, tested response — flag the word, mark the data as suspect, and have a defined recovery — not just an interrupt that nobody handles.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the scrub period relative to the expected upset rate and the array size? What's the trade-off between scrubbing frequency and the power and bandwidth cost of the scrubber itself?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant I²C bus for a space-deployed system where multiple sensor nodes share the same bus, given that single-event upsets can corrupt data or cause bus lock-ups?

**Answer:** I²C is a deceptively fragile bus in a radiation environment because it has no built-in error detection and a well-known failure mode: a slave that gets interrupted mid-byte can hold SDA low and lock the whole bus. So I'd attack it at three levels — protocol, electrical, and recovery.

At the protocol level, I'd add a checksum or CRC to every transaction payload, because the bus itself won't catch a corrupted byte. I'd also use a transaction ID or sequence number so a node can detect a dropped or duplicated message, and I'd keep messages short so the window for a mid-message upset is small. At the electrical level, I'd make sure the pull-ups and bus capacitance are within spec at the worst-case node count, and I'd consider a bus buffer or segmenter so one stuck node can be isolated rather than dragging the whole bus down.

The recovery piece is the important one. I'd implement the standard bus-clear sequence: detect SDA held low, then clock SCL manually for up to nine pulses until SDA releases, then issue a STOP. That has to live in the master's fault handler, not in the normal driver path, because the normal path may itself be blocked. I'd also add a per-node watchdog or heartbeat so a node that stops responding can be power-cycled independently — which means each node needs its own switchable supply, not a shared rail. And I'd want the master to distinguish "no response" from "bus stuck" so it takes the right recovery action. Finally, I'd test the recovery path deliberately by forcing a slave to hold SDA low and confirming the master recovers without a full system reset.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to segment the bus with a buffer versus giving each node its own bus? What's the risk of the bus-clear routine itself being interrupted by an upset?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, so the review should start by acknowledging that and asking them to walk through their assumptions — specifically the radiation environment they sized against, the part data they used, and where the margin sits. Often the disagreement isn't about the conclusion but about an input: they may have used a typical upset rate where I'd use a worst-case one, or assumed a part behaves like its commercial cousin. Getting the assumptions on the table turns it from "I think you're wrong" into "let's check the inputs together."

If the gap is real, I'd frame it in terms of consequence rather than authority: what happens to the system if the margin is consumed, and is that failure detectable and recoverable or silent and fatal? That reframes the conversation around risk the whole team can reason about. I'd also invite a third perspective — a reliability or systems engineer — so it's not just my judgment against theirs. If we still disagree, I'd want the decision documented with both positions and the rationale, so it's a traceable engineering decision rather than a personal one, and I'd be explicit about what evidence would change my mind. The goal is that the engineer leaves the review feeling heard and knowing exactly what would close the gap, not feeling overruled.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis is actually correct and your concern was based on a stale assumption? How do you keep this kind of review from becoming adversarial when the schedule is tight?
