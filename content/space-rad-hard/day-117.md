# space-rad-hard — Day 117

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The voltage reference is the single most accuracy-critical part in the chain, so I'd start by treating "no radiation data" as a risk to be quantified rather than a reason to reject the part outright. First, I'd characterize what the mission actually needs: what accuracy budget is allocated to the reference versus the ADC, the amplifier, and the sensor itself. That tells me how much drift I can tolerate before the measurement is no longer valid.

Then I'd attack the problem on three fronts. On the part-selection side, I'd look for references with any published TID data — even a manufacturer's internal report or a similar-die family — and prefer a topology known to be relatively ELDRS-insensitive, such as a buried-Zener or a bandgap with careful layout, over one with no pedigree at all. If nothing exists, I'd budget for a small TID screening run at a facility rather than assume. On the architecture side, I'd avoid depending on absolute reference accuracy: use a ratiometric measurement where the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, so drift common to both cancels. I'd also add a stable, radiation-tolerant calibration reference on the board — even a simple precision divider off a known-good source — that the firmware can use to periodically re-baseline the ADC reading and detect reference drift over time. On the firmware side, I'd log the reference-derived calibration coefficient and its history so ground can trend it; a slow monotonic drift is recoverable through recalibration, whereas an abrupt step suggests an SET and should be filtered.

The key point I'd make in a review is that "no radiation data" doesn't mean "no plan" — it means the plan has to include characterization, architectural immunity, and in-flight observability.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine reference drift from a single-event transient on the reference during a calibration read? What would you do if the screening run showed the reference drifting outside your budget at end-of-life?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating two different failure domains. TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in the logic that processes the measurement — it does nothing for an upset that corrupts the measurement before it ever reaches the logic. A single-ended analog chain has no way to detect that the amplifier output was hit by a single-event transient, that the ADC sample-and-hold was disturbed, or that the sensor itself produced a spurious value. TMR downstream will faithfully vote on three copies of a corrupted input, and all three will agree — the redundancy is blind to a common-mode fault upstream.

So the argument conflates "the digital logic is protected" with "the measurement is protected." I'd walk the engineer through the fault path: where can a single event inject an error, and does any downstream mechanism detect it? For a control loop, a corrupted measurement that passes silently is arguably worse than a detected fault, because it drives an incorrect actuator command with no alarm.

Then I'd talk about proportionate mitigation rather than demanding full triple redundancy on the analog side, which is often impractical. Options include: a second, independent signal path with a plausibility check or voting at the ADC; a reference channel or built-in test injection to validate the chain periodically; range and rate-of-change sanity checks in firmware so an implausible sample is rejected rather than acted on; and, for time-critical loops, a hold-last-good or safe-state policy when a sample fails plausibility. The right answer depends on the criticality of the loop, but the principle is that the analog chain needs its own detection mechanism, not a borrowed one from the digital side.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a plausibility check that doesn't reject legitimate fast transients in the real signal? If full dual-path redundancy is too costly, what's the minimum you'd accept for a safety-critical loop?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core idea is that the boundary between "shared" and "isolated" should be drawn by criticality and by the energy a latch-up can draw, not by convenience of layout. I'd start by classifying loads: mission-critical (must never lose power), mission-essential (can be briefly interrupted and recovered), and non-critical (can be shed). Then I'd design the distribution tree so that no single load's fault can collapse a rail that feeds a higher-criticality load.

Concretely, each load gets its own current-limited switch or eFuse with a latch-up detection and trip threshold set above its legitimate inrush and operating current but below the point where it would drag the upstream rail down. The trip should latch off rather than auto-retry blindly, so a persistent SEL doesn't cause repeated brownouts; recovery is either a commanded retry or a timed retry with a limited count. Critical loads sit on a rail that is either dedicated or protected by an upstream current limit that the downstream fault can't reach. I'd also consider a separate always-on housekeeping rail for the controller that manages the switches, so that even a fault on a payload rail doesn't take out the intelligence that's supposed to recover it.

The other half is the upstream side: the primary bus feed needs its own current limiting and, ideally, a way to isolate a faulted branch before it pulls the bus below the undervoltage threshold for the other branches. That's where the "shared vs. isolated" decision really bites — you want the shared bus to be robust enough that one branch's fault is contained at the branch, and the isolation components themselves to be rated for the fault current they'll see.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set the trip threshold for a load whose legitimate inrush current is close to its latch-up current? What recovery policy would you use for a critical load that trips — automatic retry, or wait for ground command?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The tricky part of a SEFI like this is that the usual "is it alive?" indicators lie — the processor is drawing current and the heartbeat GPIO is still toggling, so a naive watchdog that just checks the heartbeat would never fire. The test plan has to prove that the recovery mechanism detects loss of *functional* execution, not just loss of *electrical* activity.

I'd structure the plan around fault injection rather than waiting for real radiation. On the bench, I'd use a debugger or a test harness to force the processor into the exact failure state: halt the control loop while leaving a timer or DMA still toggling the heartbeat line, and leave the core drawing current. Then I'd observe whether the recovery path fires. That means the design must have a functional heartbeat — something only the control loop can produce, such as a counter the loop increments and a supervisor that checks it's advancing, or a challenge-response where the supervisor asks the loop a question and expects a fresh answer. The test verifies that this functional check, not the raw GPIO, is what triggers recovery.

I'd also test the recovery itself: does the reset or re-initialization bring the loop back to a known-good state, does it re-establish any shared state, and does it avoid a reset loop if the fault persists? I'd inject the fault repeatedly and at different points in the loop cycle to catch state-dependent recovery bugs. And I'd verify the detection latency is within the system's safe-operating window — a recovery that works but takes too long is a failure for a control loop. Finally, I'd document the injection method and the pass criteria so the same test can be rerun on the flight unit and, where possible, correlated with beam-test results.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the functional heartbeat so it can't itself be fooled by a stuck-but-toggling peripheral? What would you do if the recovery mechanism's own supervisor is the thing that gets hit by the SEFI?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is make sure the disagreement is about the technical facts, not about whose judgment wins. The engineer has done real work, so I'd start by acknowledging that and asking them to walk the review through their margin analysis — what environment they assumed, what the worst-case part behavior is, and where the margin comes from. Often the gap is an unstated assumption: they assumed a typical dose rate, or a part behavior that isn't characterized, or a derating that doesn't hold at end-of-life. Surfacing the assumption is more productive than asserting "it's under-margined."

If, after that, I still believe the margin is insufficient, I'd frame it as a question the design has to answer rather than a verdict: "What happens to this node at the worst-case dose and temperature, and how would we know?" I'd ask for the analysis to be written down and reviewed, because a documented margin calculation is something the whole team can check, and it also protects the engineer if the design turns out to be fine. If the analysis confirms my concern, the decision is grounded in evidence, not seniority.

I'd also be careful to separate the person from the proposal — critique the margin, not the engineer — and to leave room for the possibility that I'm wrong. If the engineer's analysis holds up, I should say so clearly. The goal is a defensible design decision and a team that trusts the review process, not a win. Where the schedule allows, I'd bring in a second opinion from someone with radiation experience rather than making it a two-person standoff.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis is correct but the schedule doesn't allow the mitigation you'd prefer? How do you make sure a junior engineer doesn't feel shut down by a review like this, especially if the concern turns out to be valid?
