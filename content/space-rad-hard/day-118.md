# space-rad-hard — Day 118

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The voltage reference is the accuracy anchor for the whole conversion chain, so a COTS reference with no radiation data is a real risk, not a formality. I'd start by characterizing what I actually need: what accuracy budget the measurement allocates to the reference, and over what conditions. Then I'd treat the reference as a component with three unknown failure modes — TID-induced drift (including ELDRS, which can make low-dose-rate behavior much worse than high-dose-rate test data suggests), single-event transients on the reference output, and long-term drift that compounds with radiation drift.

Mitigation would be layered. First, part selection: even without full radiation data, I'd look for references with any published TID/SEE characterization, or a manufacturer willing to share test reports, and prefer a topology (e.g., buried Zener or a bandgap with known behavior) that has a track record in space. Second, architecture: rather than trusting a single reference, I'd consider a ratiometric measurement where the ADC converts relative to the same reference that excites the sensor, so reference drift largely cancels in the ratio — this is often the single biggest accuracy win. Third, if ratiometric isn't possible, I'd add periodic in-flight calibration against a stable secondary reference or a known internal standard, and design the firmware to detect and flag drift beyond a threshold rather than silently absorbing it. Fourth, I'd add filtering and transient protection on the reference output so SETs don't propagate into conversions, and consider a redundant reference with voting or a switchover if the measurement is safety-critical.

The honest answer is that without radiation data you're managing an unknown, so I'd also budget for a targeted radiation test on the reference alone — it's a cheap, small part to test and the data de-risks the whole analog chain.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether ratiometric measurement is feasible given the sensor topology? What would you do if the reference drifts slowly but monotonically — how would your calibration scheme distinguish that from a real sensor change?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a category error in it: TMR protects against upsets in the digital logic, but it can't protect against a fault that originates upstream of the digital domain. If the analog front-end produces a wrong value — because of an SET on the amplifier, a reference glitch, or a corrupted sample — TMR will faithfully replicate and vote on the wrong number. Redundancy only helps if the redundant copies are independent, and a single analog chain is a single point of failure feeding all three digital lanes.

I'd walk through the failure modes with the engineer: an SET on the amplifier output that lands during the ADC's sample window produces a bad conversion; a single-ended chain has no common-mode rejection, so ground bounce or a transient on the reference couples straight in; and there's no way to detect a stuck or drifted sensor because there's no second opinion. Then I'd talk about what "covered" should mean — the analog chain needs its own integrity mechanism, whether that's a differential front-end for common-mode rejection, a second independent channel with plausibility checking or voting, or at minimum a sanity check in firmware (rate-of-change limits, range checks, comparison against a redundant sensor) that can flag or reject a suspect reading before it drives the control loop.

I'd also acknowledge the engineer's real constraint — analog redundancy costs board area, power, and channels — and frame the decision as risk-based: how critical is this measurement, what happens if it's wrong, and what's the cheapest mechanism that gives an independent check. That keeps the review collaborative rather than just overruling.

**Possible follow-ups:** If full analog redundancy isn't affordable, what's the minimum viable integrity check you'd accept? How would you verify in test that the plausibility checking actually catches an SET-induced bad sample?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up in one load must be contained at the boundary of that load, so the architecture has to make the boundary explicit. I'd start by classifying loads by criticality and current draw, then design the distribution as a tree: a primary bus feed, a protected upstream rail, and per-load or per-group branch protection with current limiting and latch-up detection. The key decision is where to draw the shared/isolated boundary — put too much on a shared rail and one SEL collapses it for everyone; isolate everything and you pay in mass, complexity, and single points of failure in the protection itself.

For each branch I'd use a current-limiting switch or e-fuse with a defined trip characteristic, sized so normal inrush (capacitive loads at power-up) doesn't nuisance-trip but a latch-up overcurrent does. Latch-up detection is typically an overcurrent event that persists beyond a timeout — the protection then either current-limits indefinitely or, better, cycles the branch off and retries, so a transient SEL clears and the load recovers without operator intervention. Critical loads get priority: they should be on their own protected branches, possibly with redundant feeds, so a non-critical load's fault can't drag them down. I'd also make sure the protection itself is radiation-tolerant and that its failure mode is safe — a protection switch that fails short defeats the whole scheme.

Finally, I'd instrument it: per-branch current telemetry so the system can report which branch latched and how often, which feeds both fault management and the reliability case.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the trip threshold and timeout so you don't trip on legitimate load transients? What's your approach if the protection switch itself is a COTS part with no radiation data?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part of this scenario is that the usual recovery mechanisms are blind to it: the processor looks alive to a simple heartbeat monitor, and it's still consuming normal current, so neither a current monitor nor a naive watchdog catches it. The test plan has to prove that the system detects a "zombie" state and recovers, not just that it recovers from a clean reset.

Since I can't inject real radiation on the bench, I'd build a fault-injection harness in firmware or via debugger: force the control loop to stop executing while a separate low-priority task or interrupt keeps toggling the heartbeat GPIO and the core keeps running. Then I'd verify the detection mechanism — which should not be the heartbeat alone. Options include a watchdog that requires the control loop itself to check in (a "windowed" or challenge-response watchdog where the correct response depends on control-loop state, so a stuck loop can't satisfy it), a supervisor that monitors a control-loop-specific output (e.g., a PWM update or a sequence counter that must change), or an external monitor comparing commanded vs. actual actuator behavior.

The test matrix would cover: detection latency (how long until the fault is caught), recovery action (reset, re-init, safe-state entry), and post-recovery correctness (does the control loop resume with valid state, and is any critical state preserved or safely re-established). I'd repeat across power cycles, temperature corners, and with the fault injected at different points in the loop to make sure detection isn't dependent on where it hangs. I'd also test the failure of the detector itself — what happens if the watchdog doesn't fire — and confirm the system ends in a safe state rather than an unsafe one.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a watchdog challenge that a stuck control loop genuinely cannot satisfy? What safe state would you define for a life-support-type actuator if recovery fails?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** First, I'd separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves respect and engagement, not a flat "no." I'd start by asking them to walk through their margin calculation and the assumptions behind it, because the disagreement is usually about an assumption, not the arithmetic. Often the gap is that the analysis used typical or best-case radiation data, or didn't account for a failure mode (ELDRS, SET during a critical window, TID drift over mission life), or treated an uncharacterized part as if it were characterized.

Then I'd make the risk explicit and shared: what's the consequence if the margin is wrong, and what's the cost of adding margin? Framing it as a risk trade rather than a correctness contest keeps it collaborative. If the engineer still disagrees, I'd propose a way to resolve it with evidence rather than authority — a targeted test, a worst-case analysis with agreed assumptions, or a second opinion from another engineer — and commit to being persuaded if the evidence supports them. That's important: the review has to be a genuine technical forum, not a veto.

If we can't converge and the decision is mine, I'd explain the reasoning, document the dissent and the rationale in the review record, and make sure the engineer understands it's a risk decision, not a judgment on their work. I'd also follow up afterward so the relationship isn't damaged and they feel heard. The goal is the right decision *and* a team that keeps bringing proposals forward.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the review record so a documented dissent is useful later? What would you do if the engineer's proposal later turned out to be right — how does that change how you run the next review?
