# space-rad-hard — Day 4

## Q1: How would you approach designing a watchdog timer circuit for a radiation-hardened system, and what failure modes specific to the space environment should it protect against?

**Answer:** A watchdog timer in a radiation-hardened system needs to be designed with the understanding that radiation can cause failures in both the monitored processor and the watchdog circuit itself. I would start by selecting a watchdog IC that is either a rad-hard qualified part or a COTS part that has been characterized for TID and SEL immunity. The watchdog should be independent of the main processor—separate power rail, separate clock source (preferably a radiation-tolerant crystal oscillator or RC oscillator), and physically separated on the PCB to avoid common-mode failures from a single ion strike.

Key failure modes to address include: single-event functional interrupts (SEFI) that cause the processor to stop servicing the watchdog, single-event latch-up (SEL) that could cause the watchdog itself to latch and fail to reset, and total ionizing dose (TID) degradation that could shift timing thresholds. I would implement a windowed watchdog rather than a simple timeout watchdog, because radiation-induced glitches could cause premature or delayed watchdog kicks. The windowed approach ensures the processor must kick within a specific time window—too early or too late triggers a reset.

For the reset signal itself, I would use a pulsed reset rather than a static level, and include a reset pulse stretcher (monostable multivibrator) to ensure the reset pulse is long enough to reliably reset the processor even if the watchdog's output is degraded by TID. I would also add a secondary, independent watchdog—perhaps a simple RC-based timer with a comparator—as a diverse backup, operating on a different principle so a single failure mode cannot disable both.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you test that your watchdog circuit is immune to single-event transients during radiation testing? What considerations would you have for the watchdog's timeout period in a system that may experience temporary slowdowns due to radiation-induced error correction?

## Q2: You are designing a multi-layer PCB for a space-deployed system that uses both analog sensor inputs and high-speed digital communication. How would you approach the layer stack-up and grounding strategy to minimize radiation-induced noise coupling?

**Answer:** The stack-up and grounding strategy must address two distinct challenges: first, the normal EMI/EMC concerns of mixed-signal design, and second, the unique problem of radiation-induced charge injection creating transient currents in the substrate. I would use a minimum of six layers, with the following stack-up from top to bottom: top signal (analog), ground plane, power plane, ground plane, bottom signal (digital), and a second ground plane. This provides a low-impedance return path for both analog and digital signals while keeping them separated by ground planes.

For grounding, I would use a single solid ground plane across the entire board rather than splitting analog and digital grounds, because split grounds can create large loop areas that are more susceptible to single-event transients. Instead, I would physically partition the board: analog circuitry on one side, digital on the other, with the ground plane continuous underneath both. The analog and digital sections would each have their own local power islands on the power plane, connected through ferrite beads or pi-filters to the main power rail. This prevents digital switching noise from coupling into analog supplies while maintaining a low-impedance ground return.

For radiation-specific concerns, I would add guard rings around sensitive analog nodes (like high-impedance sensor inputs) to collect any substrate currents generated by ion strikes. These guard rings should be tied to a clean analog ground at a single point. I would also place decoupling capacitors as close as possible to every IC power pin—using both bulk tantalum and low-ESR ceramic capacitors—because radiation-induced transients can cause momentary voltage drops that need to be supplied locally. For the most sensitive analog paths, I would consider using differential routing to reject common-mode noise from substrate currents.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the return path for a high-speed digital signal that must cross from the digital section to the analog section? What role does via stitching play in radiation-hardened PCB design?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage reference for a precision ADC in a space application that must maintain accuracy over a 5-year mission?

**Answer:** Voltage reference selection for space is particularly challenging because radiation degrades the very parameters that make a reference accurate—bandgap voltage stability, temperature coefficient, and long-term drift. I would start by reviewing the mission's radiation environment: total ionizing dose expected, whether the orbit passes through the proton belts, and whether there are any high-energy heavy ion concerns. For a 5-year mission in LEO, TID might be 10-50 krad, while a geostationary orbit could see 100+ krad.

I would prioritize a bandgap reference over a Zener-based reference, because bandgap references generally have better TID tolerance and lower temperature coefficients. However, bandgap references can suffer from significant TID-induced voltage shift—often 0.5-2% over mission life—so I would look for parts that have been characterized for this shift, ideally with published radiation test data. If using a COTS part, I would require lot-specific radiation testing to understand the part's specific degradation curve.

For qualification, I would follow a derating approach: select a reference with an initial accuracy at least 2-3x better than the system requirement, to budget for radiation-induced drift. I would also characterize the reference's temperature coefficient before and after irradiation, because TID can change the curvature of the temperature response. The reference should be tested at the system level with the ADC to verify that the combined radiation-induced errors stay within the ADC's linearity budget.

For the circuit design, I would add output buffering with a radiation-tolerant op-amp to prevent the ADC's sampling current from disturbing the reference voltage. I would also include a low-pass filter on the reference output to filter out single-event transients that could corrupt a single conversion. If the mission allows, I would implement periodic calibration: the system measures a known internal voltage (like a precision resistor divider from the reference itself) and adjusts the ADC's offset and gain in firmware to compensate for long-term drift.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the case where the voltage reference's TID-induced drift is monotonic and predictable versus random? What alternative would you consider if no radiation-qualified reference meets your accuracy requirements?

## Q4: Imagine you are leading a design review for a space-deployed system that uses a COTS FPGA for data processing. A junior engineer has implemented triple modular redundancy (TMR) on all flip-flops in the design, but has not applied TMR to the configuration memory or the block RAM. How would you address this gap?

**Answer:** I would first acknowledge that the engineer has taken a good step by protecting the flip-flops, but explain that TMR on flip-flops alone is insufficient for an SRAM-based FPGA in a radiation environment. The configuration memory (CRAM) and block RAM (BRAM) are actually more vulnerable to single-event upsets (SEUs) than the flip-flops, because they represent a much larger total number of bits and because a single upset in configuration memory can change the logic function itself—not just a data value.

For the configuration memory, TMR at the logic level doesn't help because the configuration bits are not accessible to the user logic. Instead, I would recommend using configuration memory scrubbing: periodically reading back the configuration frames, comparing them to a golden copy stored in radiation-hardened memory (like MRAM or a rad-hard PROM), and correcting any mismatches. The scrub rate should be fast enough that the mean time between uncorrectable errors exceeds the mission lifetime. Many modern FPGAs have built-in configuration scrubbers that can be enabled.

For block RAM, I would implement either TMR with majority voting at the output, or error-correcting code (ECC) such as single-error correction, double-error detection (SECDED). TMR for BRAM requires triplicating the memory and voting on reads, which triples the memory usage but provides immediate correction. ECC is more area-efficient but introduces latency for correction. The choice depends on the application: for high-speed data buffers where latency matters, TMR may be preferred; for large lookup tables where area is constrained, ECC may be better. I would also ensure that the BRAM is scrubbed periodically, either by a dedicated scrubber or by the configuration scrubber if the FPGA supports it.

Finally, I would ask the engineer to perform a single-event upset analysis using tools like the FPGA vendor's soft error rate calculator, to quantify the expected upset rate for the unprotected configuration and block RAM. This would help the team make an informed decision about whether additional mitigation is needed, and would also identify any other unprotected state elements like SRLs or shift registers that might have been overlooked.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between using the FPGA's built-in configuration scrubber versus implementing a custom scrubber in the FPGA fabric? What are the failure modes of the scrubber itself that you would need to protect against?

## Q5: You are the lead engineer for a space-deployed system that uses an STM32 microcontroller with external DAC and ADC for precision control of a fiber amplifier. During integration testing, you discover that the system occasionally outputs incorrect control voltages to the amplifier, causing brief power spikes. The errors are rare and non-deterministic. How would you approach debugging this issue?

**Answer:** This sounds like a single-event effect (SEE) manifesting as a transient in the control loop. I would approach the debugging systematically, starting with characterization of the failure to narrow down the root cause.

First, I would instrument the system to capture more data around each failure. I would add a circular buffer in firmware that logs the last N samples of the ADC reading, the DAC output value, and the control loop state variables. When a power spike is detected (either by an over-power monitor circuit or by the system's own telemetry), the buffer is frozen and transmitted for analysis. This would show whether the incorrect DAC output was caused by a corrupted ADC reading, a corrupted control algorithm state, or a direct corruption of the DAC's input register.

Second, I would analyze the timing and pattern of failures. Are they correlated with specific operating conditions (temperature, power supply voltage, vibration)? Do they occur more frequently during certain phases of the control loop? If the failures cluster around specific events (like a telemetry transmission or a mode change), that points to a timing or synchronization issue rather than a random radiation event.

Third, I would examine the SPI or I2C bus between the STM32 and the external DAC/ADC. A single-event transient on the clock or data lines could corrupt a single transaction, causing the DAC to latch an incorrect value. I would add CRC or checksum protection to all transactions, and verify that the DAC's data sheet specifies its susceptibility to single-event transients on its digital inputs. If the DAC is a COTS part, I might need to perform focused radiation testing on the digital interface.

Fourth, I would review the firmware's control loop architecture. Is there a single point of failure where a corrupted variable could directly set the DAC output? I would implement sanity checks: the DAC output should be bounded to a safe range, and any value outside that range should trigger a safe-state shutdown rather than being applied. I would also add a "heartbeat" mechanism where the DAC output is periodically verified by reading it back through an analog multiplexer and the ADC, comparing the actual output to the commanded value.

If the failures persist after these mitigations, I would consider hardware changes: adding a windowed comparator that monitors the DAC output and forces a safe state if it exceeds limits, or switching to a rad-hard DAC that has been characterized for single-event transients. The key is to make the system fail safely rather than trying to prevent all possible upsets.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a single-event upset in the STM32's core versus a transient on the SPI bus? What role would a hardware-in-the-loop radiation test play in your debugging approach?
