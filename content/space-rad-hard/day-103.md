# space-rad-hard — Day 103

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload where several loads share a common upstream rail, and a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not collapse the rail for the others?

**Answer:** The core principle is that the shared rail must be protected from any single load's fault, so the first decision is where to place current limiting. I'd put an independent current-limiting switch — ideally a latch-up protection device (LPD) or a current-limited load switch with a defined trip threshold — in series with each load branch, sized so that its trip point sits above that load's worst-case inrush and operating current but well below the upstream supply's capability. That way an SEL in one branch is contained locally: the switch either current-limits or trips and latches off, and the upstream rail sees only a bounded transient rather than a hard short.

The upstream supply itself needs headroom analysis: total worst-case load current plus margin must stay within the converter's rating, and the converter should have its own current limit and, ideally, hiccup or foldback behavior so a sustained fault doesn't cook it. I'd also consider whether the loads have different criticality — if so, a tiered approach makes sense, where the most critical loads sit on a branch with the tightest protection and possibly a redundant feed, while non-critical loads can be shed entirely on a fault.

Fault reporting matters too: each protection switch should expose a status/fault flag so the controller can log which branch latched and command a retry or leave it off. Retry policy is a design decision — automatic retry risks re-triggering a persistent latch, so I'd typically do a bounded number of retries with a cooldown, then latch off and report. Finally, I'd verify the whole scheme under fault injection on the bench: short each branch, confirm the rail stays within spec, and confirm the other loads ride through without reset.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the current-limit trip point when a load's inrush current is comparable to its steady-state current? What would you do if a load's SEL current is below the protection switch's minimum guaranteed trip point?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is the accuracy anchor for the whole chain, so an uncharacterized part there is a real risk — TID-induced drift and long-term stability are exactly the parameters that matter, and neither is specified. My first move would be to treat the reference as a known-unknown and design around it rather than trusting it.

Concretely, I'd look for three layers of mitigation. First, part selection: even if the exact part has no radiation data, I'd check whether the manufacturer or literature has TID data on a similar process or package, and whether a pin-compatible or functionally equivalent reference with published data exists — sometimes the cheapest fix is a different reference. Second, architecture: if the measurement is ratiometric, I can reference the ADC to the same supply that excites the sensor, which cancels much of the reference drift; if it's absolute, I'd add a stable, radiation-characterized secondary reference or a calibration channel that the system can switch in periodically to measure the primary reference's actual value and correct for drift in firmware. Third, in-mission calibration: a known internal or external standard measured at intervals lets the system track slow drift, provided the calibration standard itself is trustworthy.

I'd also derate the reference's initial accuracy budget aggressively — assume worst-case TID drift and long-term drift consume most of the error budget, and verify the remaining margin against the measurement requirement. If the numbers don't close, that's the signal to change the part or the architecture, not to hope.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you validate a firmware calibration scheme on the ground when you can't reproduce years of TID drift in a short test? What failure mode would make a ratiometric architecture worse rather than better?

## Q3: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a category error in it: TMR protects against upsets in the digital logic, but it does nothing for a fault that originates upstream of the ADC. If a single-event transient hits the amplifier or corrupts a sample, all three TMR replicas receive the same bad input and vote unanimously on the wrong answer — TMR gives you no coverage against common-mode input faults. So "the digital side has TMR" is not an answer to "the analog side is single-point."

That said, I wouldn't reflexively demand full analog redundancy — that's expensive and often unnecessary. The right response is to characterize the actual risk: what's the consequence of a bad measurement reaching the control loop, and how long does it persist? If the control loop has a fast enough update rate and the actuator has inertia, a single corrupted sample may be filtered out by the loop itself. If the measurement directly commands something dangerous, it needs protection.

Practical mitigations short of duplicating the whole chain: plausibility checks and rate-of-change limits in firmware so an implausible sample is rejected rather than acted on; a second, cheaper measurement path (even a coarse one) for cross-checking; median filtering across samples; and hardware-level measures like input clamping and filtering to reduce SET susceptibility. I'd ask the engineer to show the fault-injection analysis — inject a transient at the amplifier output and demonstrate the control loop's response is bounded. If it isn't, we add redundancy where it's cheapest, which is often at the sensor or ADC level rather than the whole chain.

**Possible follow-ups:** Where in the chain would you place a redundancy boundary to catch the most likely SET, and why? How would you distinguish a genuine fast transient in the measured quantity from an SET, given both look like a sudden step?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part is that a naive external watchdog won't catch this — the processor is alive enough to keep the heartbeat going, so the watchdog sees a healthy system. The test plan has to specifically reproduce that "zombie" state and prove the recovery mechanism catches it.

On the ground, without a beam, I'd build a fault-injection harness. The cleanest approach is firmware-instrumented: add a test hook that deliberately halts the control loop while leaving the heartbeat task running, then observe whether the system recovers. That directly exercises the detection logic. I'd also want a hardware-level injection path — for example, forcing the processor into a known bad state via debug interface or a test pin — so the test isn't only exercising a software simulation of the fault.

The detection mechanism itself needs to be designed for this case: a heartbeat alone is insufficient, so I'd add a "liveness" check that's tied to actual control-loop progress — a counter or sequence number the control loop must update, checked by an independent supervisor (external watchdog with a windowed or challenge-response input, or a separate small MCU). The test then verifies that when the control loop stops but the heartbeat continues, the supervisor still fires and resets the system within the required time.

I'd structure the plan around: (1) verify detection latency against the requirement, (2) verify the reset actually clears the fault and the system re-enters the control loop correctly, (3) verify no unsafe actuator state occurs during the detection window, and (4) repeat across power/temperature corners. If beam time is available, I'd add a beam run to confirm the injected-fault model matches real SEFI behavior, but the ground harness is what gives repeatable coverage.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the supervisor so it can't itself be fooled by a corrupted heartbeat signal? What's the trade-off between detection latency and false-positive resets, and how would you set the threshold?

## Q5: How would you approach derating and part selection for a space-deployed board, and how does derating interact with the radiation tolerance you're trying to achieve?

**Answer:** Derating and radiation tolerance are related but distinct margins, and the trap is treating them as substitutes. Derating buys electrical margin — voltage, current, power, junction temperature — against the part's datasheet limits so that normal variation and transients don't push it over the edge. Radiation tolerance is about how the part's parameters shift under TID and how it behaves under SEE. A part can be comfortably derated electrically and still fail functionally because TID has degraded a parameter the derating didn't account for.

So my approach is to derate against post-radiation parameters, not just the fresh datasheet. If I have TID data, I use the end-of-life (post-irradiation) values as the basis for derating — for example, if a regulator's output drifts or its dropout voltage increases with dose, the thermal and voltage margins must be computed on the degraded numbers. If I don't have radiation data, I either add margin to cover the unknown or treat the part as a risk item and look for an alternative.

For part selection, the hierarchy is: prefer parts with published radiation data and QML or equivalent qualification; where COTS parts are necessary, prefer those with a well-understood process and some radiation heritage, and derate them more conservatively. I'd also apply derating to the SEE-relevant parameters — for instance, keeping operating voltage and current well inside limits reduces SEL susceptibility, and keeping junction temperature low reduces both thermal stress and some radiation-induced leakage effects.

The interaction I'd flag explicitly in a design review: derating that assumes a parameter stays at its nominal value is invalid if TID moves that parameter. The derating table and the radiation analysis have to be reviewed together, not in separate silos.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set derating margins for a part with no radiation data at all — what's a defensible approach? Where does derating fail to help against SEE, and what has to be handled by architecture instead?
