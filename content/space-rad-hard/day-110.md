# space-rad-hard — Day 110

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is the accuracy anchor for the whole chain, so I'd start by treating "no radiation data" as an unknown risk rather than a zero risk. First, I'd characterize what the mission actually needs: the total ionizing dose at the reference's location (behind shielding), the expected single-event rate, and the accuracy budget allocated to the reference versus the ADC and front-end. That tells me how much drift I can tolerate before the measurement is out of spec.

Then I'd structure the design so the reference's behavior is observable and correctable. That means: (1) periodically measure the reference against a second, independent reference of a different technology — ideally a rad-hard or at least radiation-characterized part — so drift is detectable rather than silent; (2) include a calibration path with a known internal standard or a ratiometric measurement that cancels reference drift; (3) design the front-end so the reference is used ratiometrically where possible, so gain errors from reference drift cancel in the ratio. If the measurement is absolute, I'd add margin in the error budget for TID-induced drift and ELDRS, and I'd plan for a calibration cadence over the mission.

I'd also look hard at the reference's topology: bandgap references are generally more susceptible to TID and ELDRS than buried-zener or other topologies, and some parts have known low-dose-rate sensitivity that only shows up in ELDRS testing, not in high-dose-rate tests. If the part has no data at all, I'd either fund a small radiation test campaign (a few samples, a few dose points, plus a low-dose-rate leg) or qualify a characterized alternative. The decision is a trade between test cost and the cost of an unquantified accuracy risk over a multi-year mission — and for a critical measurement, I'd lean toward testing or substituting rather than assuming.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the periodic cross-check between two references so that a single-event transient on one doesn't corrupt the comparison? What would you do if the only radiation-characterized reference available has worse initial accuracy than the COTS part?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has proposed using a single commercial LDO with no radiation data to post-regulate a critical analog rail, arguing that the upstream DC-DC is already rad-tolerant so "the LDO doesn't matter." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a kernel of truth — the upstream converter sets the bulk of the rail's behavior — but it misses that the LDO is now the last element in the chain and its failure modes land directly on the load. I'd evaluate it on three axes.

First, TID and ELDRS: an uncharacterized LDO can drift in output voltage, reference, and dropout over dose, and low-dose-rate sensitivity can be much worse than a high-dose-rate test would suggest. If the analog rail feeds a precision measurement, even tens of millivolts of drift may blow the error budget. Second, single-event effects: an LDO can experience SETs on its internal reference or error amplifier that propagate as output glitches, and it can latch up if it's a bipolar or CMOS part without adequate layout and current limiting. Third, the "upstream is rad-tolerant" claim doesn't transfer — the LDO's own pass element, reference, and control loop are separate silicon with separate failure modes.

My recommendation would be to either (a) replace the LDO with a radiation-characterized part, (b) if that's not feasible, add mitigation: current limiting and a clamp on the LDO output, a downstream filter to attenuate SET glitches, and a monitoring path so drift is observable, plus a radiation test campaign on the specific part if the budget allows. I'd also ask whether the LDO is even necessary — sometimes a well-filtered tap off a rad-tolerant converter, or a different post-regulation topology, removes the uncharacterized element entirely. The key point in review is to make the risk explicit and quantified rather than dismissed by the "upstream covers it" argument.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you quantify the SET glitch attenuation you'd need from a downstream filter? If the LDO must stay for schedule reasons, what monitoring would you add so drift is caught before it affects the measurement?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant clock distribution network for a space-deployed system that uses multiple FPGAs and ADCs requiring synchronized sampling?

**Answer:** Synchronization across multiple devices is really a skew and single-point-of-failure problem, and radiation adds transient clock disturbances on top. I'd start by deciding the synchronization budget: how much sample-to-sample skew the signal processing can tolerate, and whether the ADCs need sample-synchronous operation or just a common time base with timestamping. That drives whether I need a single distributed clock, a clock per device with a synchronization pulse, or a recovered clock scheme.

For the distribution itself, I'd use a fan-out buffer tree with matched-length, impedance-controlled traces and proper termination, and I'd keep the clock source and buffers in a radiation-tolerant or characterized family. A single crystal or oscillator driving everything is the simplest for skew but is a single point of failure, so I'd consider a primary/secondary clock source with a glitch-free switchover, or a clock monitor that detects loss and fails over. For SETs on the clock net, the concern is a transient that causes a synchronous logic failure across multiple chips at once — so I'd add glitch filtering where the clock enters each device, and I'd make the synchronization scheme tolerant of a missed or corrupted sync pulse (e.g., re-sync on the next valid pulse rather than hanging).

I'd also think about the failure-detection side: a clock monitor per device that flags loss of lock or out-of-window frequency, feeding the housekeeping so the system can re-synchronize or reset a device that's drifted. And I'd verify the whole scheme with a skew measurement across temperature and a fault-injection test that pulls or glitches the clock and confirms the system recovers coherently.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a clock source that fails in a "stuck high" or "stuck low" state rather than stopping cleanly? What's the trade-off between a single distributed clock and per-device clocks with a sync pulse?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part is that the processor looks alive — current draw is normal, the heartbeat GPIO is still toggling — so a naive watchdog that just checks "is the heartbeat moving" won't catch it. The test plan has to verify that the recovery mechanism detects a *functional* failure, not just a *dead* processor.

I'd structure the plan around fault injection rather than waiting for real radiation. On the bench, I'd inject SEFI-like states by: halting the control loop in firmware while leaving the heartbeat task running (a deliberate software fault injection), corrupting the program counter or a critical state variable via debugger, and using a hardware fault injector to flip bits in the processor's state. For each injection, I'd verify the detection path — a watchdog that requires the control loop to "check in" with a specific sequence or a computed token, not just a toggle — fires within the required time, and that the recovery (reset, re-init, safe state) completes and the control loop resumes correctly.

I'd also test the boundary cases: what if the SEFI happens during recovery, what if the heartbeat task itself is corrupted, and what if the fault occurs while the system is in a safe state. And I'd run the injection at temperature extremes and across the operating voltage range, because recovery timing margins shrink at corners. Where possible, I'd correlate with beam time — a proton or heavy-ion run — to confirm the injected faults resemble real SEFI signatures, but the bench injection is what gives repeatable coverage.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the watchdog check-in so it can't be satisfied by a corrupted-but-still-running task? What recovery action would you choose if a reset isn't acceptable because the control loop must not be interrupted?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be acknowledged before I challenge the conclusion. I'd start by asking them to walk through their margin calculation and the assumptions behind it, because often the disagreement is really about an unstated assumption (e.g., they assumed a dose level, a shielding thickness, or a derating factor that I'd question). Making the assumptions explicit turns it from "I think you're wrong" into "let's check this input together."

Then I'd bring the discussion back to the requirements and the risk framework: what does the mission spec actually require, what's the consequence of the margin being insufficient, and what's the cost of adding margin versus the cost of a failure. If the engineer's analysis holds up against those, I should be open to being wrong — confidence backed by work is worth taking seriously. If it doesn't, I'd frame the gap in terms of the specific number or assumption that's off, not a general judgment, and propose a concrete path: add margin here, or run a test to resolve the uncertainty, or document the accepted risk with a sign-off.

I'd also make sure the decision gets recorded — the rationale, the dissenting view, and who owns the risk — so it's not relitigated later and so the engineer sees their concern was heard. The goal in a review is a defensible decision, not a winner, and keeping it that way is what makes people willing to bring real analysis next time.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis is sound but the schedule doesn't allow the margin fix before the design freeze? How do you make sure a junior engineer doesn't feel personally overruled in front of the team?
