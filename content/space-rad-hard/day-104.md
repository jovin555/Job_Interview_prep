# space-rad-hard — Day 104

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** I'd break this into three concerns: fault isolation between the two feeds, inrush/hot-swap control, and single-event latch-up (SEL) containment on the downstream loads.

For isolation, the two 28V feeds should be combined through an ORing scheme — either ideal-diode controllers or ORing FETs — so that a fault on one feed cannot back-drive or collapse the other. The key design question is whether the ORing is truly independent per feed or whether a shared controller creates a common-mode failure path. I'd want each feed's ORing element to be independently controlled and monitored, with the ability to isolate a feed entirely if its input voltage or current goes out of bounds. A shorted or latched load downstream of the ORing should be caught by a per-load current-limiting/protection stage, not by the ORing itself, so that one bad load doesn't force the whole feed offline.

For hot-swap, the concern is inrush current into the bulk capacitance when a board is inserted or a feed is switched in. I'd use a hot-swap controller with programmable slew-rate control on the pass FET, plus a current-limit threshold set above the steady-state load but below what the bus can tolerate. The hot-swap controller also needs to ride through the transient without nuisance tripping, so the current-limit response time and the fault timer have to be tuned together. During a hot-swap event, the ORing and downstream regulation should not see a disturbance large enough to glitch the loads — that means adequate bulk capacitance on the downstream side and a hot-swap controller that limits di/dt rather than just clamping current.

For SEL containment, each downstream load branch should have its own current-limited switch with a latch-up detection threshold. If a load latches, the branch current rises, the switch limits it, and a fault timer either retries or latches the branch off. The critical point is that the protection is per-branch, not shared, so a single latched load cannot drag the common rail down. I'd also add a bus voltage monitor that can command a full feed isolation if the common rail sags below a threshold, as a last-resort backstop.

The trade-off I'd flag is between protection speed and nuisance tripping: too-fast current limiting can trip on legitimate inrush or transient load steps, while too-slow limiting lets a latch-up persist long enough to damage the part or disturb the rail. That's a tuning exercise backed by worst-case load-step and inrush analysis, not a one-size-fits-all number.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the fault isolation behavior on the bench without actually shorting a live 28V bus? What would you monitor to distinguish a genuine latch-up from a transient load step?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage supervisor or reset IC for a space-deployed system, given that most commercial parts are not radiation-characterized?

**Answer:** I'd start by being honest about what the part actually has to do and what failure of that function means for the mission. A voltage supervisor that holds the processor in reset during power-up is a fairly benign function if it fails safe (asserts reset); it's dangerous if it fails in a way that releases reset early or never asserts at all. That framing determines how much qualification effort is justified.

The selection process: first, look for any part with published radiation data — total ionizing dose (TID) and single-event effects (SEE), specifically single-event transient (SET) and single-event upset (SEU) on the output. If a rad-tolerant or rad-hard supervisor exists that meets the threshold accuracy and timing requirements, that's the path of least resistance and I'd take it even at a cost premium, because the qualification burden drops dramatically.

If no characterized part exists, I'd evaluate candidates on architecture and process. Bipolar or older-process parts tend to be more TID-tolerant than fine-geometry CMOS. I'd look at the datasheet's absolute maximum ratings and operating range and derate aggressively — a supervisor running well below its rated supply and temperature stress will have more TID margin. I'd also look at whether the part's output stage is open-drain or push-pull; open-drain with an external pull-up gives more flexibility to define the fail-safe state.

Then the qualification plan. With limited budget, I'd prioritize: a TID test to the mission dose plus margin (typically 2x or the mission dose plus a factor, depending on the program's derating philosophy), and a heavy-ion or proton test focused on SET/SEU at the output, because a spurious reset or a missed reset is the failure mode that matters. If full beam time is unaffordable, I'd consider a cheaper screening approach — proton testing at a facility with lower cost per hour, or using published data from a similar process node as a starting point — but I'd be explicit that this is a risk-accepted position, not a qualification.

I'd also design around the uncertainty: add an independent reset path (a second supervisor or a discrete RC-plus-comparator circuit) so that a single uncharacterized supervisor failing doesn't leave the system without reset coverage. And I'd make the supervisor's behavior observable in telemetry — log reset cause — so that on-orbit anomalies can be attributed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the TID test dose level and what margin philosophy would you apply? If the supervisor passes TID but shows SETs at the output during heavy-ion test, how would you mitigate that in the circuit?

## Q3: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a category error in it: TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in the digital logic, but it does nothing for a single-event transient (SET) that corrupts the analog measurement before it ever reaches the ADC. If the sensor, amplifier, or ADC front-end produces a wrong value, TMR on the downstream digital logic will faithfully process and vote on that wrong value — all three redundant copies see the same corrupted input. Redundancy only helps if the redundant paths are independent at the point where the fault occurs.

So I'd evaluate the analog chain on its own terms. What's the failure mode of each stage under radiation? An SET on the amplifier output is a transient voltage spike that the ADC may sample; an SET on the ADC's sample-and-hold or reference can corrupt a conversion; a TID-induced drift in the amplifier's offset or gain shifts the measurement slowly over mission life. None of these are addressed by digital TMR.

Then I'd ask: what does the control loop actually need? If the measurement is time-sensitive and a single corrupted sample could drive a dangerous actuator command, then some form of analog-side mitigation is warranted. Options include: a second independent signal chain with comparison/voting at the ADC output (true analog redundancy, expensive in board area and power); a plausibility check on the digitized value (rate-of-change limiting, band checking) that rejects implausible samples and holds the last good value; or a sample-and-hold with a longer integration time that averages out short SETs, at the cost of bandwidth. The right choice depends on the SET duration relative to the sampling rate and the control loop's tolerance for a held value.

I'd also push back on the framing that the analog side needs to be "covered" in the same way as the digital side. The goal isn't symmetry — it's that every credible fault path has a mitigation proportional to its consequence. A single-ended analog chain may be perfectly acceptable if the control loop has a downstream plausibility check and the consequence of a bad sample is bounded. It's not acceptable if the bad sample goes straight to an actuator with no gate. So my response to the engineer would be: show me the fault path from sensor to actuator and the mitigation at each step, and if the only mitigation is "the digital side has TMR," that's a gap.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a plausibility check on a digitized measurement without introducing unacceptable latency into the control loop? If you added a second analog chain for voting, how would you handle the case where the two chains disagree because of a slow TID-induced drift rather than an SET?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part of this question is that the failure mode is specifically designed to defeat the naive recovery mechanism — a watchdog that just checks "is the heartbeat toggling" will be fooled. So the test plan has to verify recovery from a fault that looks alive but isn't functioning.

First, I'd define what "recovered" means concretely: the control loop resumes executing within a bounded time, the outputs return to a safe and then correct state, and the event is logged with enough information to diagnose it. That definition drives the test.

To inject the fault without a particle beam, I'd use a debug hook or a test-only firmware path that deliberately halts the control loop task while leaving the heartbeat toggle running — essentially simulating the SEFI state. On a system with an RTOS, that could be suspending the control task while a lower-priority task keeps toggling the GPIO. On a bare-metal system, it could be a jump to a tight loop that toggles the heartbeat but skips the control update. The injection should be triggerable externally (a command over the debug interface or a test pin) so the test is repeatable and doesn't require rebuilding firmware for each run.

Then I'd verify the recovery mechanism actually catches it. If the design uses a watchdog that only monitors the heartbeat, this test will fail — which is the point. The recovery mechanism needs to be something that detects loss of control-loop execution specifically: a watchdog that the control loop itself must service (so a hung control loop starves it), a cross-check where a supervisor task verifies that control outputs are updating within expected bounds, or a hardware timer that the control loop must reset and that the heartbeat task cannot. The test plan should exercise each layer of that mechanism independently and then together.

I'd also test the boundaries: how long does recovery take, and is that within the control loop's safe operating window? What state do the outputs go to during the fault and during recovery — do they fail safe or do they hold a stale value that could be dangerous? Does the event log capture enough to distinguish this SEFI from a genuine watchdog reset or a power glitch? And does the system recover repeatedly, or does a second injection during recovery cause a lock-up?

Finally, I'd run the test across the operating envelope — temperature extremes, supply voltage margins, and with the processor loaded — because recovery timing and watchdog behavior can shift with clock and voltage, and a mechanism that works at nominal may not work at the corner.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the watchdog so that it detects a hung control loop but does not false-trigger during legitimate long-running operations? If the recovery mechanism itself is susceptible to radiation, how would you test that it still works when the supervisor is also disturbed?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the technical question from the interpersonal one. The engineer has done real work, which means they've thought about the problem and deserve to have that work taken seriously — dismissing it out of hand damages the review and the relationship. But "confident and has done real work" doesn't make a design adequate, and my job is to make sure the margin question gets resolved on evidence, not on confidence.

I'd start by making the concern concrete and falsifiable rather than asserting "it's under-margined." What specifically is the margin, against what stress, and what's the consequence if it's exceeded? For example: "The regulator's worst-case output is X, the load's absolute max is Y, and the gap is Z millivolts — here's the datasheet line and here's the radiation-induced drift that could close that gap." Putting numbers on the table turns it from a difference of opinion into a question the engineer can engage with. If I can't articulate the concern that precisely, that's a signal I need to do more homework before pushing back.

Then I'd invite the engineer to walk through their margin analysis and specifically ask how they accounted for the radiation effects in question — TID drift, SET, SEL, whatever applies. Often the gap is that the engineer analyzed the nominal electrical case thoroughly but didn't extend the analysis to the radiation-degraded case. Framing it as "help me understand how you handled this" rather than "you missed this" keeps it collaborative and gives them a chance to either show me something I missed or discover the gap themselves.

If after that discussion we still disagree, I'd escalate to data: propose a specific test, analysis, or worst-case calculation that would settle it, and agree in the review to resolve it that way rather than by seniority. If the schedule doesn't allow a test, I'd document the risk explicitly — what the margin is, what the consequence of exceeding it is, and what the mitigation or acceptance rationale is — so the decision is visible and owned rather than buried. And if the engineer's position turns out to be right, I'd say so clearly in the review; that's how you keep the next disagreement constructive.

The one thing I wouldn't do is let it become a contest of confidence. The review's job is to surface the risk and make a defensible decision, and a junior engineer who feels heard and is argued with on the merits is more likely to internalize the margin discipline than one who's simply overruled.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis is technically correct but you still believe the design is too risky for program reasons the engineer isn't privy to? How would you make sure the documented risk acceptance actually gets reviewed by someone with the authority to accept it?
