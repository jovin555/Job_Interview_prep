# space-rad-hard — Day 99

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The voltage reference is often the accuracy bottleneck in a precision measurement chain, so I'd treat an uncharacterized reference as a top-level risk rather than a detail. My approach would start with characterization: even a limited test campaign — a few TID steps at low dose rate to catch ELDRS behavior, plus heavy-ion or proton runs to look for single-event transients on the reference output — gives far more confidence than a datasheet with no radiation data. If budget doesn't allow full characterization, I'd bound the risk analytically: estimate the worst-case drift from the reference's temperature coefficient and long-term stability specs, then add a TID allowance based on published data for similar topologies, and check whether the resulting error budget still meets the measurement requirement. Architecturally, I'd avoid depending on a single uncharacterized reference for a critical measurement. Options include a rad-tolerant reference with published data if one fits the accuracy target, a ratiometric measurement scheme where the reference and the sensor excitation share the same source so drift largely cancels, or periodic in-flight calibration against a stable secondary standard. I'd also add SET mitigation at the reference output — filtering and, if the reference feeds a sample-and-hold, a transient-detection scheme so a momentary spike doesn't get latched into a control decision. Finally, I'd document the residual risk explicitly and make sure the system-level error budget and the mission's accuracy requirement are reconciled before committing to the part.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the in-flight calibration scheme so it doesn't itself depend on an uncharacterized part? What test data would you insist on before accepting the reference for a critical rail?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** I'd start by pointing out that TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in the logic, but it does nothing for a fault that originates upstream of the ADC — a SET on the amplifier, a corrupted reference, or a wrong channel selected at a mux. TMR replicates the computation, not the input. If the analog front-end produces a bad sample, all three TMR lanes will faithfully agree on the bad value, and the voter will happily pass it through. So the argument conflates "the digital logic is protected" with "the measurement is protected," and those are different claims. I'd then work through the actual failure modes: what happens if a SET hits the amplifier mid-conversion, what happens if the reference glitches, what happens if the mux select lines are disturbed. For each, I'd ask whether the existing design detects or masks it. Often the answer is "neither," which means the control loop can act on a corrupted measurement. Mitigations don't have to mean full analog redundancy — they can be cheaper: a plausibility check or rate-of-change limit on the sampled value before it enters the control loop, a duplicate measurement on a second channel for critical parameters, or a comparison against a redundant sensor. I'd frame the discussion around the consequence of a bad measurement reaching the actuator, not around "analog vs digital," so the engineer sees the gap in terms of system risk rather than a turf argument.

**Possible follow-ups:** Where would you put the plausibility check — in the ADC driver, in the control loop, or in a separate monitor task? How would you decide which analog parameters justify full redundancy versus a sanity check?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a SEL is a local fault and must be contained locally — the distribution architecture should never let one load's overcurrent event propagate upstream and collapse a shared rail. I'd start by tiering the loads by criticality and by their SEL sensitivity, then design the protection per tier. Each load, or each small group of similar loads, gets its own current-limited switch with a latch-up detection threshold set above its normal inrush but below the point where it would drag the upstream rail down. The switch needs to distinguish a genuine overcurrent from a legitimate capacitive inrush — typically with a timed blanking window or an active inrush-limiting scheme — so it doesn't nuisance-trip at power-up. For the most critical loads, I'd consider a redundant feed path or a cross-strapped arrangement so a single switch failure doesn't remove the load. Upstream, I'd make sure the primary bus has its own protection and that the distribution point can isolate a faulted branch without disturbing the others — that usually means a star or branch topology rather than a single shared bus with everything hanging off it. I'd also think about recovery: after a latch-up trip, does the switch retry automatically, and if so with what backoff? For a critical load you may want an autonomous retry with a limited count, then a latched-off state that requires a command, so the system doesn't oscillate. Finally, I'd verify the whole scheme against the actual load current profiles and inrush characteristics, because the protection thresholds are only as good as the load data behind them.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set the latch-up detection threshold when a load's normal current varies widely with operating mode? What would you do if a critical load's redundant feed shares a common upstream point with a non-critical load?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part here is that the failure mode is specifically designed to defeat a naive watchdog — the processor looks alive because the heartbeat is still toggling, so a simple "did the GPIO change?" monitor won't catch it. The test plan has to verify that the recovery mechanism detects loss of *functional* liveness, not just loss of *electrical* liveness. Since I can't inject real radiation on the bench, I'd build a fault-injection harness in firmware: a debug hook or a test build that deliberately wedges the control loop while leaving the heartbeat task running, so I can reproduce the exact SEFI signature on demand. Then I'd verify each layer of the recovery chain independently. First, does the functional monitor — a task that checks the control loop is actually producing fresh outputs, not just that the CPU is running — detect the wedge within its specified window? Second, does the recovery action (reset, task restart, or full power cycle) actually clear the wedged state? Third, does the system come back to a safe, known state after recovery, not just "running"? I'd also test the boundary conditions: what if the wedge happens during a critical write, what if the heartbeat task itself is the one that's corrupted, what if the recovery mechanism is triggered repeatedly. And I'd verify the monitor's own liveness — a watchdog that depends on the same wedged subsystem is useless. The output of the plan is evidence that the recovery chain works for the specific failure signature, plus a documented residual risk for SEFI modes the harness can't reproduce.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the functional-liveness monitor so it can't be fooled by a partially-wedged system? What would you do if the fault-injection harness can't reproduce a particular SEFI mode you're worried about?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be acknowledged before I challenge the conclusion. I'd ask them to walk me through their margin calculation and the assumptions behind it, because often the disagreement isn't about the conclusion but about an input: what radiation environment they assumed, what derating they applied, whether they accounted for TID drift over the mission life, whether the part's datasheet numbers are typical or worst-case. If the gap is an assumption, we can resolve it with data rather than opinion. If the gap is a judgment call about acceptable risk, I'd reframe it in terms the whole review can evaluate: what's the consequence if the margin is wrong, and what does the mission's risk posture say about that consequence? That moves it from "I think you're wrong" to "here's the risk we'd be accepting, and here's who owns that decision." I'd also make sure the engineer has a real path to defend their position — if they can show the margin holds under a defensible worst case, I should be persuadable, and saying so explicitly keeps the review honest. If we still disagree after that, I'd document both positions and escalate the risk decision to whoever owns it, rather than letting it become a personal standoff. The goal is a decision the team understands and can stand behind, not a decision I win.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer's analysis turns out to be correct and your concern was based on a wrong assumption? What would you do if the risk owner overrules you and accepts the under-margined design?
