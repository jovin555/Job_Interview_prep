# space-rad-hard — Day 126

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** The core idea is to treat each 28V feed as an independent, isolated source and to make the downstream load-side protection fast enough that a fault on one feed never propagates to the other or to the shared load. I'd structure it in layers.

First, input-side: each feed gets its own reverse-polarity and overvoltage protection, plus an ideal-diode-OR (or a redundant ORing controller) so that either feed alone can carry the full load. The ORing element must be rated for the full fault current and must not fail short — a MOSFET-based ORing controller with a controlled gate drive is preferable to a simple Schottky OR, because it limits dissipation and gives you a defined failure mode. Critically, the two ORing paths must be independent: no shared control node, no shared bias supply that a fault on one feed could drag down.

Second, hot-swap: an inrush-limiting controller on each feed with a defined dV/dt ramp and a current-limit foldback. The hot-swap FET must be sized for the worst-case inrush energy (capacitive load charging) plus the fault energy, and the controller should latch off on a sustained overcurrent rather than repeatedly retrying into a short. I'd also add a transient-voltage suppressor and a small series inductance on each input to keep hot-swap di/dt from coupling onto the bus.

Third, load-side: a per-load current-limiting switch or eFuse so that a latched load trips its own protection without collapsing the upstream rail. The trip threshold and response time need to be coordinated with the upstream ORing and hot-swap stages — a fast load-side trip protects the rail, but too-fast a trip can nuisance-trip on legitimate inrush.

Fourth, the radiation angle: the ORing controllers, hot-swap controllers, and current-sense amplifiers are all candidates for SET-induced false trips. I'd want SET-tolerant filtering on the current-sense path (a short blanking window so a transient spike doesn't latch the protection off), and I'd derate the FETs generously on Vds and current so that TID-induced parameter drift doesn't erode the margin. Any control logic that decides "trip and latch" should be designed so that a single upset can't permanently disable a healthy feed — either a voting scheme or a resettable latch with a supervisory path.

Finally, I'd verify the whole thing with fault-injection testing: short each feed, latch each load, hot-swap into a fully charged load, and confirm the other feed and the shared load ride through.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you coordinate the trip thresholds between the load-side eFuse and the upstream hot-swap controller so you don't get cascading trips? What failure mode would you accept in the ORing controller if it were hit by an SET at the worst possible moment?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage supervisor or reset IC for a space-deployed system, given that most commercial parts are not radiation-characterized?

**Answer:** I'd start by being honest about what the part actually has to do and what failure looks like if it gets it wrong. A voltage supervisor's job is to hold the processor in reset until the rail is valid and to assert reset if the rail droops — so the failure modes that matter are (a) failing to release reset (system never boots), (b) releasing reset too early (processor runs on an invalid rail), and (c) nuisance resets from SET-induced threshold glitches. Each of those has a different radiation sensitivity.

Selection-wise, I'd look first for a part with any published SEE/TID data, even from a manufacturer's internal report or a sister part in the same family — sometimes a rad-hard supervisor exists but isn't obvious from the top-level catalog. If nothing qualified exists, I'd consider building the function from discretes or from a rad-hard comparator plus a reference, because then I control the topology and can add hysteresis and filtering explicitly.

For a COTS candidate, the qualification approach depends on budget. With limited budget, I'd do a two-step: first, a physics-of-failure review of the die (bipolar vs CMOS, presence of a bandgap reference, latch-up structure) to estimate SEL susceptibility and TID drift; second, a focused test campaign — TID to the mission dose plus margin on a small sample, and heavy-ion or proton SEE testing at the relevant LET if the part is in a critical path. If full testing isn't affordable, I'd bound the risk by design: add external hysteresis so threshold drift doesn't cause nuisance resets, add an RC filter on the sense input so SETs are averaged out, and put the supervisor's output through a redundant path (e.g., a second supervisor with a slightly different threshold, ORed) so a single failed supervisor can't hold the system down.

I'd also document the residual risk explicitly — what dose the part was tested to, what LET, what the untested corner is — so the program can make an informed accept/reject decision rather than pretending the part is qualified.

**Possible follow-ups:** If you build the supervisor from a rad-hard comparator, how do you guarantee the reference is stable over dose? What would you do if TID testing showed the part's threshold drifted outside the datasheet window but the part still functioned?

## Q3: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a category error in it: TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in the digital logic, but it does nothing for a fault that originates in the analog front-end. If the amplifier's gain drifts under TID, or an SET on the ADC reference produces a spurious sample, the digital TMR will faithfully vote on three copies of the same wrong number. Redundancy downstream of a single point of failure doesn't remove the single point of failure.

So I'd evaluate the chain by asking where the single points of failure are and what the consequence of each is. The sensor itself, the amplifier, the ADC, and the reference are all candidates. For each, I'd ask: what does a TID-induced drift look like over mission life, and can calibration catch it? What does an SET look like at the output, and can the control loop tolerate a single bad sample? If the control loop has a time constant much longer than the SET duration, a median filter or a plausibility check on the sample may be enough. If the loop acts on every sample, a single SET can drive a wrong actuator command, and that's a real hazard.

Mitigations I'd consider, in order of cost: (1) plausibility/range checking and rate-of-change limiting on the ADC output, so a single wild sample is rejected; (2) a second, independent signal path — even a lower-grade one — so the two can be compared and a disagreement flagged; (3) a redundant reference or a ratiometric measurement so reference drift cancels; (4) full dual-channel with voting if the measurement is safety-critical. The right answer depends on the hazard analysis, not on a blanket "add redundancy everywhere."

I'd also want the engineer to show me the FMEA for the analog chain specifically — if the analog chain isn't in the FMEA at all, that's the real gap to close.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a plausibility check is sufficient versus needing a second physical channel? If you add a second channel, how do you handle the case where the two channels disagree and you can't tell which is right?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a memory scrubbing strategy for an SRAM-based FPGA in a space application, and what trade-offs would you weigh?

**Answer:** Scrubbing is about preventing the accumulation of single-bit upsets into uncorrectable multi-bit errors. The strategy has to answer three questions: what gets scrubbed, how often, and what happens when scrubbing finds an error it can't fix.

What gets scrubbed: configuration memory is the big one for SRAM FPGAs, because an upset there can change the logic function itself, not just data. Block RAM and any external memory holding critical state also need coverage. I'd separate these because the mechanisms and cadence differ — configuration scrubbing is typically a full-device readback-and-compare against a golden image, while data-memory scrubbing is usually ECC-driven.

How often: the scrub interval is set by the expected upset rate and the correction capability. If the memory has SEC-DED, a single upset is correctable, so the scrub interval just needs to be short enough that the probability of a second upset landing in the same codeword before the first is scrubbed stays acceptably low. That's a reliability calculation, not a guess — I'd want the predicted upset rate per bit, the codeword size, and the mission duration, and then choose an interval that keeps the uncorrectable-error probability below the program's target. There's a trade-off: scrubbing faster costs more power and more FPGA readback bandwidth, and readback itself can contend with the functional logic.

What happens on an uncorrectable error: this is where a lot of designs are weak. If scrubbing finds a multi-bit error it can't correct, the system needs a defined response — partial reconfiguration from the golden image, a full reload, or a reset of the affected subsystem. That response has to be tested, because an untested recovery path is not a recovery path.

Trade-offs I'd weigh: scrub granularity (full-device vs partial) against recovery latency; scrub frequency against power and bandwidth; whether to scrub during critical operations or only in idle windows; and whether the scrubber itself needs protection, since a scrubber that gets upset can either miss errors or, worse, write bad data back. I'd also consider whether the golden image needs its own integrity check, because scrubbing against a corrupted golden image is worse than not scrubbing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you protect the scrubber logic itself from upsets? If readback bandwidth is limited, how would you prioritize which regions get scrubbed most often?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real work, and that deserves acknowledgment before any critique — I'd start by having them walk through their analysis and the assumptions behind it, because the disagreement is almost always about an assumption, not about competence.

Then I'd make the disagreement concrete and testable rather than a matter of opinion. "I think this is under-margined" is not a review comment; "your analysis assumes the part's threshold doesn't drift under TID, and here's the data suggesting it can drift by X over the mission dose" is. If I can't articulate the specific assumption I think is wrong, I need to go find out before I push back — otherwise I'm just asserting seniority.

I'd also invite the engineer to stress-test their own design: what would have to be true for this to fail, and how would we know? Often the engineer will find the gap themselves once the question is framed that way, which is a much better outcome than me winning the argument. If they still disagree, I'd propose a way to resolve it with evidence — a worst-case analysis, a bench measurement, a literature check on the part's radiation behavior — and agree in the review on who does what and when.

If it comes down to a judgment call that can't be resolved with data in the time available, I'd make the call and own it, but I'd document the dissent and the reasoning so the decision is traceable. What I would not do is let the review become adversarial or let the engineer feel their work was dismissed. The goal is the right technical decision, and the way to get there is to make the engineer a partner in finding it, not an opponent to be overruled.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis turns out to be right and yours was wrong? How do you make sure a junior engineer feels able to push back on you in the next review?
