# space-rad-hard — Day 116

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** I'd start by treating the reference as the weakest link and quantifying what "accuracy over the mission" actually means — separating initial accuracy, temperature drift, long-term drift, and radiation-induced drift, because each has a different mitigation path. With no radiation data, the honest position is that the reference's behavior under TID and ELDRS is unknown, so the design must either (a) remove the reference from the accuracy-critical path, or (b) make its drift observable and correctable.

For (a), the strongest move is ratiometric measurement: if the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, reference drift largely cancels in the ratio, converting an absolute-accuracy problem into a matching problem. Where that isn't possible, I'd consider a reference topology with inherent margin — for example, a buried-Zener or bandgap device with a documented low-dose-rate sensitivity trend from the literature, even if not formally qualified — and then design the surrounding circuit so that reference drift is a slow, monotonic term rather than a step.

For (b), I'd add a calibration anchor: a stable, radiation-insensitive physical quantity (a precision resistor divider from a characterized part, or an on-board transfer standard measured at known intervals) that lets firmware track reference drift over the mission and apply a correction. This requires the reference to be monotonic and slow-drifting, which is exactly what ELDRS can violate — so I'd want at least a low-dose-rate screen or a small sample irradiated at low dose rate to confirm monotonicity, even if full qualification is out of budget.

Finally, I'd bound the risk with a fault-detection layer: monitor the reference against its expected window, flag out-of-family behavior, and degrade gracefully (e.g., fall back to a lower-accuracy mode with wider error bars in telemetry) rather than silently reporting bad data. The key discipline is being explicit in the requirements and FMEA about what is *known*, *assumed*, and *mitigated* — an uncharacterized reference is acceptable only if the architecture makes its failure observable and its drift correctable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the low-dose-rate screen if you can only irradiate a handful of samples? What changes if the reference also supplies the DAC in a closed-loop control path, so drift affects actuation as well as measurement?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument conflates two different fault domains. TMR protects against a single logic upset producing a wrong digital result; it does nothing about a wrong *input* arriving at the voter. If the analog front-end produces a corrupted sample — an SET on the amplifier, a mux glitch, a reference transient — all three TMR lanes receive the same bad value and vote unanimously for it. TMR gives you agreement, not truth. So the analog chain is not "covered"; it's a single point of failure upstream of the redundancy.

I'd walk through the failure modes explicitly: an SET on the amplifier output that lasts longer than the ADC sampling window; a stuck or drifted sensor; a reference transient that shifts the conversion; a single-ended ground offset that couples digital return currents into the measurement. Each of these produces a plausible-looking but wrong sample, and none is caught by digital TMR.

Then I'd discuss proportionate mitigation rather than reflexively demanding full analog redundancy, which is often impractical. Options in increasing cost: differential signal paths with common-mode rejection; a second, independent sensor or a diverse measurement principle for cross-checking; plausibility limits and rate-of-change checks in firmware so an out-of-family sample is rejected before it reaches the control loop; a redundant ADC channel or a reference cross-check; and finally full analog channel redundancy with mid-value selection or median voting. The right level depends on the criticality of the control loop and the consequence of a bad actuation.

The framing I'd use with the engineer is: redundancy has to be *diverse and independent* to be meaningful. Two identical channels fed by the same front-end aren't redundant — they're correlated. The design review question isn't "is there TMR?" but "what is the fault containment boundary, and does every credible fault get detected before it can cause harm?"

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement plausibility checking without adding so much latency that the control loop becomes unstable? If you add a second sensor, how do you decide which one to believe when they disagree?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a watchdog and reset scheme for a processor that can suffer a SEFI leaving it still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The core problem is that a naive watchdog trusts a signal that a SEFI can fake. If the heartbeat is a simple GPIO toggle driven by a timer interrupt, a processor stuck in a state where the timer still fires but the main loop is dead will keep the watchdog happy forever. So the first design principle is that the watchdog must be fed by evidence of *correct* execution, not evidence of *any* execution.

Concretely, I'd make the heartbeat a computed token rather than a fixed toggle: the main loop performs a defined sequence of checks — control loop ran, sensor values in range, state machine advanced — and writes a result the watchdog expects to see change in a specific way. A stuck processor that only runs the timer ISR can't produce the correct evolving token. This is essentially a challenge-response watchdog rather than a window watchdog.

Second, I'd separate the watchdog from the processor's clock domain where possible. An external watchdog with its own oscillator is immune to a clock fault that takes out the processor's timing. If the processor's internal watchdog is the only option, I'd at least verify it's clocked independently of the main PLL and that its timeout is shorter than the time to reach a hazardous state.

Third, I'd design the reset response, not just the detection. A reset that returns the system to a safe state and logs the event is worth more than a reset that silently reboots into the same fault. I'd include a reset cause register, a boot counter to catch reset loops, and a defined safe-state entry (actuators to known-safe, outputs disabled) before normal operation resumes.

Finally, I'd test it adversarially: inject a fault that halts the main loop while leaving the timer running, and confirm the watchdog fires. If it doesn't, the scheme is wrong regardless of how it looks on paper.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you prevent a reset loop where the system reboots, re-enters the fault, and resets again indefinitely? What's the trade-off between a shorter watchdog timeout and false resets during legitimate long operations?

## Q4: How would you approach thermal design for a high-power DC-DC converter in a vacuum, where the only heat path is conduction to a chassis, and how does that interact with radiation derating?

**Answer:** In vacuum there's no convection, so the thermal design is a conduction problem from junction to chassis, and every interface in that path is a resistance I have to account for: junction-to-case, case-to-heatsink or case-to-chassis through the mounting interface, and the thermal interface material itself. I'd build the path as a series thermal resistance budget and check that the junction temperature stays under the derated limit at worst-case dissipation — which for a DC-DC is usually at maximum load and minimum input voltage, not nominal.

The practical levers: maximize copper and thermal via density under the converter, use a thermal interface material with a specified, vacuum-stable conductivity (and be careful — some gap fillers outgas or degrade in vacuum), and ensure the mounting provides real contact pressure rather than relying on a few screws. Radiative cooling is usually a minor term at these temperatures but shouldn't be ignored for surfaces with a view to cold space.

The radiation interaction is the part people miss. Radiation derating and thermal derating compound: a part run close to its junction limit has less margin for the parametric drift that TID causes, and some failure mechanisms are accelerated by temperature. So I'd derate junction temperature more aggressively than a terrestrial design would, and I'd check that the converter's control loop and reference — which set output accuracy — are evaluated at the *hot* end of the range under end-of-life drift, not just at room temperature. A converter that meets its output tolerance cold but drifts hot, combined with TID drift, can walk out of the load's absolute-maximum window.

I'd also verify the thermal design survives a single-event transient that briefly increases dissipation, and that any thermal protection (shutdown on over-temperature) doesn't itself become a single point of failure that takes down a critical rail.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you validate the thermal model before flight — what test would give you confidence in the junction temperature estimate? What happens to your derating budget if the chassis temperature is specified at its maximum rather than typical?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** First, I'd separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be acknowledged explicitly — the disagreement is about margin, not about competence, and if the review feels like an attack the engineer will defend rather than reconsider.

Second, I'd get to the shared facts before arguing about conclusions. The productive question isn't "is this enough margin?" but "what environment are we designing to, what does the part's data actually say, and what's the consequence if we're wrong?" Often the disagreement dissolves once both of us are looking at the same numbers — sometimes the engineer has data I don't, sometimes I have a requirement they haven't seen. I'd ask them to walk me through their margin calculation and where the numbers came from, and I'd do the same for mine.

Third, I'd make the risk explicit and owned rather than personal. If we still disagree, I'd frame it as: here is the failure mode, here is its likelihood and consequence, here is the mitigation cost, and here is who accepts the residual risk. That moves the conversation from "you're wrong" to "here's the decision and its basis," which is what a design review is for. If the engineer's position is defensible and the risk is acceptable and documented, I should be willing to be persuaded — being the lead doesn't mean winning.

Fourth, if the margin genuinely isn't there, I'd make the call and explain the reasoning, and I'd give the engineer a path to contribute to the fix rather than just overruling them — ask them to propose the mitigation, or to define the test that would prove the margin is adequate. That keeps their ownership and often produces a better answer than either of us started with.

Finally, I'd record the decision and its rationale in the review minutes, so the reasoning is visible later and the engineer sees their concern was heard even if the outcome went the other way.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis turns out to be right and yours was wrong — how do you handle that in front of the team? How do you keep a review constructive when the schedule pressure makes everyone want to just approve and move on?
