# space-rad-hard — Day 107

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in one load must be contained without collapsing the shared upstream rail — and how would you decide where to draw the boundary between "shared" and "isolated"?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up is a low-impedance fault: the affected device stops behaving like a load and starts behaving like a short. So the design question is really "how much current can this fault draw before something upstream gives, and what else is on that same node?" I'd start by partitioning loads by criticality and by fault energy. Anything that can latch gets its own current-limited branch — a dedicated eFuse or latching-current-limiter with a defined trip threshold and a latch-off or retry policy — so the fault is confined to that branch and the upstream rail only sees a bounded, predictable transient. The shared rail itself gets sized and filtered so that the worst-case sum of branch transients stays within its regulation envelope, and I'd add bulk capacitance at the rail to ride through the response time of the limiters. The boundary decision comes down to three things: how much fault current the upstream source can tolerate without dropping out of regulation, how fast the limiter responds versus how fast the rail sags, and whether a load's failure mode is "annoying" (retry is fine) or "must never recur without operator intervention" (latch-off). I'd also think about fault propagation backwards — a latched load can inject noise or a voltage transient onto the shared rail, so the limiter needs to block reverse current, not just forward overcurrent. Finally, I'd want the containment to be observable: each branch should report its fault state to telemetry, otherwise you've contained the fault but lost the diagnostic.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you choose between a latch-off and an auto-retry policy for a limiter protecting a load you can't physically reset? What happens to your containment scheme if two loads latch simultaneously?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has proposed using a COTS crystal oscillator with no radiation data as the primary clock source, arguing that "crystals don't have logic, so they can't have single-event upsets." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The premise is partly right and the conclusion is wrong. It's true that a quartz resonator itself doesn't hold state the way a flip-flop does, so you won't get a classic SEU in the crystal. But the argument ignores everything around it. First, the oscillator is an active circuit — the driver/inverter inside the can is a transistor circuit and is susceptible to single-event transients, which can manifest as a momentary frequency excursion, a missed cycle, or in the worst case a startup failure or a stuck output. Second, TID and displacement damage can shift the oscillator's frequency and degrade its startup margin over the mission, and that drift is not something a calibration routine fixes if the clock is the timebase everything else is referenced to. Third, and most important for a system, the clock is a single point of failure with system-wide blast radius: a transient on the clock net can cause a synchronous logic failure across multiple chips simultaneously, which is exactly the kind of correlated fault that redundancy elsewhere doesn't catch. So I'd evaluate it on three axes: does the part have any radiation characterization at all (even a vendor report or published test data), what is the failure mode and how does the system detect it (clock monitor, loss-of-lock, frequency plausibility check), and is there a fallback timebase. If there's genuinely no data, I'd want either a characterized alternative, a derated/qualified COTS part with a documented test campaign, or a mitigation architecture — a redundant clock source with a monitor and switchover — so that a clock fault is detectable and recoverable rather than silently corrupting the whole board.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the clock monitor so it catches a frequency excursion without false-tripping on legitimate jitter? If you keep the COTS oscillator, what would you want to see from a radiation test to sign off on it?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant I²C bus for a space-deployed system where multiple sensor nodes share the bus, given that single-event upsets can corrupt data or cause bus lock-ups?

**Answer:** I²C is a deceptively fragile bus in a radiation environment because it's open-drain, has no built-in error detection, and a single stuck node can hold SDA or SCL low and take the whole bus down. I'd attack it at three layers. At the protocol layer: every transaction gets a CRC or checksum in the payload, plus a sequence number or transaction ID so a corrupted or duplicated read is detectable; reads are treated as untrusted until validated, and critical values are cross-checked against plausibility limits or a redundant read. At the electrical/bus-management layer: the master needs a bus-recovery routine — detect a stuck line, clock out up to nine pulses to let the offending slave release, issue a STOP, and re-initiate; if that fails, isolate the offending node. That implies the bus should be segmentable, either with an I²C buffer/switch per branch or with each node's connection gated, so one locked slave can be electrically removed rather than dragging the bus. At the node layer: each sensor node should have a local watchdog and a way to reset its own I²C state machine, and ideally the ability to be power-cycled independently. I'd also think about bus speed and rise times — slower, well-terminated buses with margin are more forgiving of a transient glitch than a bus running at the edge of its timing. And I'd want the recovery events counted and telemetered, because a bus that recovers silently every hour is telling you something about the environment.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to isolate a node permanently after repeated lock-ups versus retrying it? What's the trade-off between adding a bus switch per node and just accepting occasional bus resets?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage supervisor or reset IC for a space-deployed system, given that most commercial parts are not radiation-characterized?

**Answer:** A voltage supervisor is a small part with an outsized role — it's the thing that decides whether the processor is allowed to run — so I'd treat the lack of radiation data as a risk to be quantified and mitigated rather than a checkbox. First, I'd define what the supervisor actually has to do: threshold accuracy over temperature and life, reset pulse width, and behavior during and after a supply transient. Then I'd look for any radiation data at all — vendor reports, published SEE/TID test results, or heritage from similar missions — because "no data" often means "no one has published," not "no one has tested." If there's genuinely nothing, I'd consider a few paths: a rad-hard or rad-tolerant supervisor if one exists at the needed threshold; a discrete implementation built from characterized parts (a reference, a comparator, an RC delay) where I control and can analyze each element; or a COTS part with a documented test campaign if the budget allows. For the COTS path, I'd want to know the failure modes: does a TID-induced threshold shift cause a false reset (annoying but recoverable) or a failure to reset (dangerous)? Does an SET on the output cause a spurious reset pulse that the system can ride through? I'd also design the surrounding circuit to be tolerant of supervisor misbehavior — e.g., a reset that's asserted by more than one source, and a processor that can distinguish a real brownout from a spurious reset via a status register. Finally, derating and lot qualification matter: a part that's fine at one lot may not be at another, so I'd want burn-in and any available SEE data before committing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you evaluate a supervisor whose threshold shifts negative under TID — is that safer or more dangerous than a positive shift? What would you put in the reset path to make a spurious reset non-catastrophic?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be engaged with on its merits, not overruled by seniority. I'd start by asking them to walk through their margin calculation and the assumptions behind it, because often the disagreement is really about an unstated assumption (what environment, what part behavior, what worst case) rather than about the conclusion. If I think the margin is thin, I'd try to make the concern concrete and testable rather than asserting it: "here's the failure mode I'm worried about, here's the condition under which it occurs, can we quantify the probability or the consequence?" That reframes it from a disagreement into a shared analysis problem. I'd also bring in the consequence side — in a radiation context, margin isn't just about whether it works nominally, it's about what happens when it doesn't, and whether the system can detect and recover. If after that we still disagree, I'd want a decision that's documented with the rationale and the residual risk, and I'd be explicit about who owns that risk. And I'd keep the door open: if the engineer's analysis turns out to be right, say so clearly — that builds the trust that makes the next hard review easier. The goal isn't to win the review, it's to make sure the design that ships is the one we can defend.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis is technically correct but you still believe the consequence is unacceptable? How do you make sure a "documented residual risk" decision doesn't just become a way to avoid fixing the design?
