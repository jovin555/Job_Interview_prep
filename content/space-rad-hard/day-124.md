# space-rad-hard — Day 124

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is the accuracy anchor for the whole conversion chain, so an uncharacterized reference is a first-order risk, not a detail. I'd start by decomposing the error budget into three buckets: initial accuracy, environmental drift (temperature, time), and radiation-induced drift (TID and ELDRS). The first two are usually specified; the third is the unknown, and the design has to be robust to it rather than assume it away.

My first move is to avoid betting the measurement on a single uncharacterized reference. Options, roughly in order of preference: (1) select a reference that *does* have radiation data — even a modestly characterized part beats a precision part with a blank datasheet, because you can budget the drift; (2) if a COTS reference is unavoidable, characterize it myself with a low-cost TID screen (cobalt-60 or proton) at a few dose points, including a low-dose-rate leg to expose ELDRS, and use that data to set the error budget; (3) design the architecture so reference drift is observable — e.g., periodically measure the reference against a second, independent reference (even a lower-grade one) or against a ratiometric on-board standard, so drift becomes a detectable, calibratable quantity rather than a silent bias.

Architecturally, I'd also make the conversion ratiometric where possible: if the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, reference drift largely cancels in the ratio. That converts a hard absolute-accuracy problem into a ratio problem, which is far more forgiving of an uncharacterized reference. Where absolute accuracy is genuinely required, I'd add a calibration path — a known internal standard switched into the ADC input periodically — so the system can correct for reference movement over mission life. And I'd derate the reference's operating point (lower current, lower junction temperature) because TID and ELDRS damage are both temperature- and bias-sensitive; running the reference cool and lightly loaded buys margin.

Finally, I'd document the residual risk explicitly: what accuracy the mission actually needs, what the worst-case uncharacterized drift could be, and what the calibration cadence has to be to stay inside it. If the numbers don't close, that's a finding for the design review, not something to paper over.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the periodic calibration path so it doesn't itself become a single point of failure? If you can only afford one radiation test campaign, would you spend it on the reference or on the ADC itself, and why?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument conflates two different fault domains. TMR protects against a single logic upset *downstream* of the ADC — it does nothing for an error that enters the chain before the digital domain, because TMR faithfully replicates a wrong input three times and votes it through. Redundancy only helps if the redundant copies can fail independently; three copies of the same corrupted sample are not redundancy, they're agreement on a wrong answer.

So I'd reframe the review around where errors can originate and whether the architecture can detect them. In a single-ended chain, the failure modes are: sensor drift or open/short, amplifier offset drift or SET-induced transient on the front-end, ADC reference movement, and coupling of digital switching noise into the analog path. None of these are caught by downstream TMR. The question becomes: what detection exists *at the analog boundary*?

Practical mitigations I'd discuss with the engineer: (1) plausibility/range checks and rate-of-change limits on the digitized value before it reaches the control loop — cheap, catches gross faults and many SET-induced spikes; (2) a second, independent measurement path where the criticality justifies it — even a lower-grade redundant sensor with a different physical principle, so common-cause failure is reduced; (3) differential signal routing and front-end filtering to reduce SET and noise susceptibility; (4) a known-good reference injected periodically to verify the chain end-to-end. The right answer depends on the criticality of the loop: for a monitoring channel, plausibility checks may be enough; for a control loop that actuates something, I'd want genuine analog-side diversity or at least a cross-check against a second sensor.

I'd also make the point that "the digital side has TMR" is itself worth scrutinizing — TMR on the fabric doesn't protect the input conditioning, and if the analog chain is single-ended, the whole measurement has a single point of failure that the TMR gives a false sense of security about. The review outcome should be a documented decision: either accept the single-ended chain with explicit detection and a stated criticality rationale, or add diversity. What I wouldn't accept is "TMR covers it" as the justification.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a rate-of-change limit without masking a genuine fast transient the control loop needs to see? If you add a second sensor for diversity, how do you decide which one to trust when they disagree?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant I²C bus for a space-deployed system where multiple sensor nodes share the same bus, given that single-event upsets can corrupt data or cause bus lock-ups?

**Answer:** I²C is a deceptively fragile choice for a shared bus in a radiation environment because it has no built-in error detection, no arbitration recovery, and a well-known failure mode where a slave holds SDA low and wedges the bus. So I'd treat the bus as a system with three layers of protection: protocol, electrical, and recovery.

At the protocol layer: every transaction gets a CRC or checksum in the payload, because I²C's ACK only confirms a byte was received, not that it was correct. I'd also add sequence numbers or a transaction ID so a corrupted or replayed read is detectable, and I'd design the master to treat any malformed response as a failed transaction rather than trusting partial data. Where the sensor supports it, I'd use a register-read-back pattern — write a config, read it back, verify — for anything safety-relevant.

At the electrical layer: bus capacitance and pull-up sizing matter more in a redundant/multi-node design; I'd keep segments short, consider a bus buffer/expander with fault isolation so one wedged segment doesn't take down the whole bus, and add series protection so a failed node can't hold the bus. If the criticality justifies it, I'd consider a second, independent bus (or a different protocol entirely for the most critical node) so a single bus fault doesn't remove all sensing.

At the recovery layer: the classic I²C lock-up fix is clock pulsing — the master toggles SCL up to nine times to let the stuck slave shift out its pending bit, then issues a STOP. I'd build that into the master's error handler as an automatic recovery routine, with a bounded retry count, and escalate to a bus reset (power-cycle the segment via a load switch) if clock pulsing fails. The recovery logic itself needs to be robust to the master being the upset victim, so a watchdog on the master and a defined re-initialization sequence are part of the design.

I'd also think about failure containment: a single sensor node that latches up shouldn't be able to hold the bus. Segmenting the bus with isolators, or giving each critical node its own bus, trades pin count and complexity for fault isolation — a trade I'd make explicitly based on criticality.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you detect that a sensor node is wedged versus simply not responding, and does the recovery differ? What's the risk of the clock-pulsing recovery itself disturbing other nodes on the bus, and how would you bound it?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a watchdog and reset scheme for a processor that can suffer a SEFI leaving it still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** This is the case that defeats a naive watchdog, and it's worth being precise about why: a simple external watchdog that just checks "is the heartbeat toggling?" will be satisfied by a processor that's stuck in a loop toggling the heartbeat GPIO but has stopped running the control task. The watchdog is measuring liveness, not correctness. So the design has to make the heartbeat a *proof of correct execution*, not just a sign of life.

The standard technique is a windowed watchdog with a challenge-response: the external watchdog issues a changing challenge (a nonce or a sequence the processor must transform in a way that requires actually running the control code), and the processor must return the correct response within a time window. A stuck processor can't produce the right answer to a fresh challenge, so the watchdog fires. The window (both minimum and maximum) also catches a processor that's running too fast or too slow, which a simple timeout misses. The response computation should be tied to the control loop's own state so that "the loop ran" is what's being attested.

I'd also add a second, independent liveness check that doesn't depend on the processor at all: a hardware monitor on a signal the control loop must produce — e.g., the actual actuator command update, or a dedicated GPIO the control task toggles only after completing a full loop iteration with valid data. If that signal stops changing, the monitor resets the processor regardless of what the heartbeat is doing. Diversity matters here: the heartbeat and the loop-completion signal should be generated by different code paths so a single fault can't fake both.

On reset: the reset itself must be robust. I'd use an external supervisor with a defined reset pulse width, ensure the reset is asserted long enough for the processor and its peripherals to fully re-initialize, and log the reset cause (watchdog vs. power-on vs. brown-out) in non-volatile memory so the ground can see the pattern. If the processor can latch up (SEL), the reset may not clear it — that needs a power-cycle path via a load switch, with the supervisor commanding a full power cycle after a reset that doesn't restore healthy operation. And the recovery should be bounded: N resets, then fall back to a safe state and wait for ground command rather than reset-looping forever.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the challenge-response so it doesn't add unacceptable latency to the control loop? If the processor is in a reset loop, how does the system decide when to stop retrying and go to a safe state?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves respect — the fastest way to lose a good engineer is to make a review feel like a verdict on their competence rather than on the design. So I'd open by acknowledging the work and restating what I understand their reasoning to be, to confirm I've actually understood it before I critique it.

Then I'd move the conversation from opinion to evidence. "Under-margined" is my judgment; the review needs a shared basis. I'd ask the engineer to walk through their margin calculation explicitly — what radiation environment they assumed, what the part's worst-case behavior is, what derating they applied, and where the numbers came from. Often the disagreement resolves itself at this step: either they've used an assumption I disagree with (e.g., a typical rather than worst-case TID number, or no ELDRS allowance), or I've missed something they accounted for. Either way, the discussion is now about assumptions, not about who's right.

If we still disagree, I'd try to make the disagreement concrete and testable: what would have to be true for their margin to be adequate, and can we check it? That might mean pulling the datasheet, requesting radiation data, running a quick calculation together, or flagging it as an open action with an owner and a date. Framing it as "here's the specific number I'm worried about, can we close it?" keeps it collaborative and gives the engineer a path to defend or revise their position with dignity.

I'd also be explicit about the decision rule: for a radiation-critical item, the default is to design to worst-case with documented margin, and if we can't demonstrate the margin, we either add mitigation or escalate the risk with a named owner. That takes the decision out of a personality contest and puts it on a documented standard. If the engineer still disagrees after the evidence is on the table, I'd make the call, document the rationale and the dissent, and make clear the decision is about the risk posture, not about them. And I'd follow up afterward — a short conversation after the review to make sure the engineer feels heard and knows what's expected next goes a long way toward keeping the relationship and the review culture healthy.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's manager escalated your decision after the review? How do you keep a review constructive when the under-margined proposal came from someone more senior than you?
