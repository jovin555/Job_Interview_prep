# space-rad-hard — Day 59

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant clock tree for a board that mixes a rad-hard FPGA with several COTS clocked devices, where a single-event transient on the clock net could cause a synchronous logic failure across multiple chips?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the problem into two parts: the clock *source* and the clock *distribution*. On the source side, I'd want a clock generator that either has radiation data or can be made tolerant through architecture — for example, a PLL whose loop filter is slow enough that a single-event transient on the reference gets attenuated rather than propagated, or a source that can be re-locked and re-validated after an upset. If the source itself has no radiation data, I'd treat it as a single point of failure and either duplicate it with a glitchless switchover, or accept it only if the downstream logic can tolerate a bounded frequency excursion.

For distribution, the key insight is that a clock net is a *shared* resource, so an SET on it is a common-mode fault — it can flip many flip-flops at once, which defeats the assumption behind TMR that faults are independent. That pushes me toward a few strategies. One is to keep the clock tree short and point-to-point where possible, so a transient on one branch doesn't reach every load. Another is to use differential clocking (LVDS or similar) with a receiver that has good common-mode rejection, since a single-ended net is more susceptible to induced transients. A third is to add a clock monitor — a small independent oscillator or a divided copy of the clock — that the FPGA can compare against; if the monitored clock deviates outside a window, the logic can hold reset or switch to a backup source.

I'd also think about whether the clock needs to be synchronous at all across every device. If some COTS parts only need a clock for housekeeping, I'd consider giving them their own local oscillator rather than fanning out one master clock, so an upset on the housekeeping clock can't corrupt the control path. The trade-off is more components and more potential for drift between domains, which matters if you need synchronized sampling.

Finally, I'd validate the design with fault injection — not just radiation testing, but injecting glitches on the clock net during bench testing to see how the logic responds. If a single glitch can cause an unrecoverable state, that tells me the clock architecture needs more than just a tolerant source; it needs a recovery path.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If you duplicate the clock source for redundancy, how do you handle the switchover without introducing a glitch that's worse than the transient you're protecting against?
- How would you decide whether a given COTS clocked device needs its own local oscillator versus sharing the main clock?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design for a space-deployed system where a junior engineer has proposed using a COTS crystal oscillator with no radiation data as the primary clock source, arguing that "crystals don't have logic, so they can't have single-event upsets." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument is partially right but incomplete, and the gap is where the risk lives. A bare quartz crystal is a passive resonator — it doesn't have the junctions that make a semiconductor susceptible to SEU, so the *resonator itself* is relatively robust. But a crystal oscillator is not just a crystal; it's a crystal plus an active driver circuit, usually an inverting amplifier or a dedicated oscillator IC, and that active circuitry absolutely can experience single-event transients, single-event functional interrupts, and over the mission, total ionizing dose degradation. So the claim "crystals can't have SEUs" is true of the resonator and false of the oscillator.

The practical concerns I'd raise are: first, does the oscillator's active circuit have any radiation characterization at all? If not, I'd want to know whether the topology is one where an SET on the driver causes a momentary frequency excursion that the downstream PLL can ride through, or whether it can cause the oscillator to stop or start at a harmonic. Second, TID and displacement damage can shift the oscillator's frequency or degrade its startup margin over the mission — a part that starts reliably at beginning of life may not start reliably after years of accumulated dose, especially at cold temperatures. Third, if the oscillator is the single clock source for the whole system, a functional interrupt there is a common-mode fault.

What I'd recommend depends on criticality. If the clock is only used for housekeeping, I might accept a COTS oscillator with a documented startup and frequency-stability spec, plus a monitor that detects loss of clock and triggers a reset or switchover. If the clock drives a control loop or synchronized sampling, I'd want either a rad-hard or radiation-tested oscillator, or a redundant pair with a glitchless switchover, and I'd want the switchover logic itself to be tolerant. I'd also want the design to specify a startup test at end-of-life dose and temperature extremes, because "it worked on the bench" is not the same as "it starts after five years in orbit."

The broader point I'd make to the junior engineer is that "no logic" is not the same as "no active circuitry," and the radiation risk is almost always in the active circuitry, not the passive element.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you design a loss-of-clock detector that is itself tolerant to the radiation environment?
- If you use a redundant oscillator pair, how do you decide which one is "good" without trusting a potentially upset comparison circuit?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** I'd break this into three concerns: input ORing, per-load protection, and hot-swap behavior.

For input ORing, the goal is that either 28V feed can power the payload alone, and a fault on one feed doesn't drag down the other. The classic approach is diode-ORing, but a plain diode drops voltage and dissipates heat, so for a 28V bus I'd lean toward an ideal-diode controller or an ORing FET arrangement with a controller that quickly turns off the FET if it detects reverse current. The key spec is reverse-recovery speed: if a feed shorts, the ORing element on that feed has to turn off fast enough that the other feed doesn't sag below the payload's minimum operating voltage. I'd also want the ORing controller itself to be tolerant — if it's a COTS part with no radiation data, I'd want to understand its failure modes and possibly add a passive backup path.

For per-load protection, the requirement is that a shorted or latched load on one branch doesn't take down the whole rail. That means each load gets its own current-limiting or e-fuse element, sized so that a hard short on that branch trips the protection without pulling the common rail below the undervoltage threshold of the other loads. I'd also think about whether the protection should latch off or auto-retry. For a latched load, auto-retry can be dangerous if the latch is persistent — it can cause repeated current surges. A better approach is often a timed retry with a limited number of attempts, then a latched-off state that the system can report and recover from on command. That gives the system a chance to recover from a transient latch without oscillating indefinitely.

For hot-swap, the concern is inrush current when a load is plugged in or when a feed is switched on. A capacitive load looks like a short at the instant of connection, so without inrush control you can get a current spike that trips the upstream protection or disturbs the bus. The standard solution is a hot-swap controller with a controlled slew rate on the pass FET, plus a timer that allows the inrush to complete before the overcurrent protection arms. I'd also want to make sure the hot-swap controller's behavior during a radiation event is understood — if an SET causes it to momentarily mis-compare, does it glitch the output, or does it ride through?

The overall architecture I'd aim for is: two feeds, each with an ORing element and its own protection; a common intermediate rail; and then per-load e-fuses with controlled inrush and timed retry. I'd want to test the whole thing under fault injection — short a load, short a feed, hot-swap a load — and verify that the other feed and the other loads stay within spec. And I'd want the fault reporting to be independent of the faulted path, so the system can tell the ground what happened even if the faulted branch is latched off.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you size the ORing FET and its controller so that a feed short doesn't cause the surviving feed to sag below the payload's minimum voltage?
- If a load latches off after repeated retries, how would you design the recovery path so the system can bring it back without a full power cycle?

## Q4: How would you approach derating and part selection for a space-deployed board, and how does derating interact with the radiation tolerance you're trying to achieve?

**Answer:** Derating and radiation tolerance are related but distinct, and the interaction between them is where a lot of designs get into trouble. Derating is about keeping a part's applied stress — voltage, current, power, junction temperature, frequency — below its rated maximum by some margin, so that the part has margin against manufacturing variation, aging, and the environment. Radiation tolerance is about how the part behaves when it's exposed to ionizing particles and accumulated dose. The interaction is that radiation can *consume* the margin you thought you had.

Take voltage derating on a capacitor. You might derate to 50% of rated voltage for reliability. But TID can cause leakage current to increase, and in some dielectrics it can reduce breakdown voltage. So a part that had 2x margin at beginning of life might have much less margin at end of life after accumulated dose. If you only derated for reliability and didn't account for TID-induced degradation, you could be fine on the bench and fail in orbit. The same logic applies to transistors: TID causes threshold voltage shifts, which can reduce noise margin, increase leakage, and change switching behavior. A logic part that's derated for speed at beginning of life might be too slow at end of life after threshold shifts.

So my approach would be: first, select parts that either have radiation data or are in a family with known behavior. For parts with data, I'd look at the end-of-life parameters — not just the beginning-of-life specs — and derate from those. For parts without data, I'd either avoid them in critical paths or add enough margin and monitoring that I can tolerate degradation. Second, I'd apply derating on top of the radiation-adjusted parameters, not on the datasheet beginning-of-life numbers. That means if a part's datasheet says 5V max and radiation data shows the effective max drops to 4.5V at end of life, I derate from 4.5V, not 5V. Third, I'd think about derating for the *environment*, not just the part. In vacuum, there's no convection, so thermal derating is more about conduction paths and interface materials than about ambient temperature. A part that's fine at 25°C ambient on the bench might be at 100°C junction in vacuum with the same power dissipation.

The other interaction is that derating can sometimes *reduce* radiation sensitivity. Running a part at lower voltage and lower current generally reduces its susceptibility to single-event latch-up, because latch-up is driven by the parasitic thyristor turning on, and that's easier when the part is at higher voltage and higher temperature. So there's a happy alignment there: derating for reliability often also improves radiation tolerance. But it's not a substitute for radiation data — a part with no data is still a risk even if it's heavily derated, because you don't know the failure mode.

Finally, I'd document the derating rationale in the design file, including the radiation assumptions, so that a reviewer can see not just *what* margin was applied but *why* it's sufficient for the mission dose and environment.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a part where the radiation data shows end-of-life degradation but the datasheet doesn't specify the degraded parameters?
- If derating reduces SEL susceptibility, how would you decide how much derating is enough versus how much is over-design?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the *person* from the *proposal*. The engineer has done real work, and that deserves respect — if I come in and just say "this is wrong," I lose the room and I lose the chance to actually improve the design. So I'd start by acknowledging the work and asking questions rather than making assertions. "Walk me through how you arrived at this margin" is a better opening than "this margin is too small." Often the engineer has a rationale I haven't considered, and sometimes the disagreement is about assumptions rather than conclusions.

The second thing is to make the disagreement *specific and testable*. "Under-margined" is vague. What I want to get to is: what is the worst-case condition, what is the part's specified behavior under that condition, and what is the margin between them? If we can agree on the worst-case condition and the part's behavior, the margin question often answers itself. If we disagree on the worst-case condition — say, I think the radiation environment is harsher than the engineer assumed — then the disagreement is about the environment spec, and we can resolve that by going back to the mission requirements or the radiation environment document. If we disagree on the part's behavior, that's a question for the datasheet or for radiation test data, and we can resolve it by looking at the data together.

The third thing is to bring in *evidence* rather than authority. If I have radiation data that shows the part degrades more than the engineer assumed, I'd show it. If I don't have data and I'm arguing from experience, I'd say so — "I don't have data on this specific part, but I've seen similar topologies fail in this environment, and I'd like us to either get data or add margin." That's a different conversation than "I'm the lead and I say so." It also gives the engineer a path to resolve the disagreement: go get the data, or add the margin, or propose a test that would settle it.

The fourth thing is to keep the review *forward-looking*. The goal is not to win the argument; it's to make the design robust. So I'd try to reframe from "your design is wrong" to "here's the risk I'm worried about, and here's what I'd like to see to close it." That might be a test, a derating change, a different part, or a documented rationale for accepting the risk. Any of those can be a valid outcome, as long as it's explicit and documented. What I don't want is for the risk to be silently accepted because the engineer was confident and I didn't want to push back.

Finally, if we can't resolve it in the review, I'd take it offline and bring in a third party — a radiation specialist, a systems engineer, or the customer — rather than letting it become a standoff in the meeting. The review's job is to surface the disagreement, not necessarily to resolve it on the spot. And I'd follow up with the engineer afterward to make sure they understand that the pushback was about the design, not about them, and that I want them to keep bringing proposals forward.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if the engineer's proposal is actually correct and your concern was based on an outdated assumption?
- How would you handle a situation where the engineer agrees to add margin but the schedule doesn't allow for the rework?
