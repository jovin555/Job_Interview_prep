# space-rad-hard — Day 95

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** I'd start by treating the reference as an uncharacterized risk and bounding that risk before deciding whether it can stay in the design. The first step is to understand what the reference actually contributes to the error budget: initial accuracy, temperature coefficient, long-term drift, and noise are all specified, but TID-induced drift and ELDRS are not. Without data, I have to assume the worst — that the reference could drift by some unknown amount over the mission — and ask whether the measurement architecture can tolerate that.

There are a few architectural levers. One is to make the reference observable: route it into a spare ADC channel through a resistive divider so the system can measure its own reference against a second, independent reference (or against a ratiometric measurement) and detect drift in flight. If the reference drifts slowly, a calibration routine that periodically re-derives the transfer function can compensate — but only if the drift is monotonic and slow, not a step change from a single event. Another lever is to use a ratiometric measurement topology where the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, so drift in the reference cancels in the ratio. That's often the most robust approach for bridge or RTD-style sensors.

For single-event transients on the reference, the concern is a momentary spike or dip that corrupts one conversion. Mitigations include a well-bypassed reference output with a low-ESR capacitor close to the ADC, a sample-and-hold that rejects short transients, and firmware that takes multiple samples and applies a median or consistency check before accepting a reading. If the measurement is time-critical and can't retry, I'd consider a second reference and a voting or plausibility scheme.

Ultimately, if the mission accuracy requirement is tight and the reference is truly uncharacterized, I'd push to either qualify the part with a targeted TID and heavy-ion test at a facility, or replace it with a reference that has published radiation data. The decision comes down to whether the error budget can absorb an unknown drift term — and if I can't bound it, I can't sign off on it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the in-flight calibration routine so it doesn't mask a genuine sensor fault as reference drift? What test would you run first if you had budget for only one radiation test on this part?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument conflates two different fault domains. TMR on the digital side protects against logic upsets after the signal has been digitized — it does nothing for a fault that occurs before the ADC. A single-event transient on the amplifier, a drift in the sensor excitation, or an upset in the ADC itself produces a wrong digital value that TMR will faithfully replicate three times. Redundancy downstream of a corrupted input just gives you three copies of the same wrong answer.

So the first thing I'd do is map the analog chain's failure modes against the control loop's criticality. If this measurement feeds a safety-critical or mission-critical control action, a single non-redundant analog path is a single point of failure regardless of what the digital side does. The question becomes: what's the probability and consequence of an analog SET or drift, and can the control loop tolerate a transient wrong value?

There are cheaper mitigations than full triple redundancy. A plausibility check in firmware — rate-of-change limits, range checks, comparison against a redundant but lower-grade sensor — can catch many transient errors. A second, dissimilar sensor channel with a voting or "best-of-two with disagreement flag" scheme gives diversity without triplicating the whole chain. For the ADC itself, taking two conversions through different input paths (or a mux'd spare channel) and comparing them catches converter-level upsets. If the measurement is truly critical, I'd argue for a genuinely independent second channel — different amplifier, different ADC, ideally different reference — because common-cause failures in a shared front-end won't be caught by any amount of downstream voting.

I'd frame the review conversation around the control loop's failure budget rather than "analog vs digital." The engineer isn't wrong that TMR is valuable; the point is that it doesn't extend coverage across the ADC boundary. Once we agree on where the uncovered single points of failure are, the mitigation choice becomes an engineering trade rather than a philosophical one.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set the plausibility-check thresholds so they catch SETs without rejecting legitimate fast transients in the measured signal? If you add a second sensor channel, how do you handle the case where the two channels disagree and you can't tell which is right?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up in one load must be contained at that load's boundary — it must not be allowed to drag down a shared rail or propagate back to the primary bus. That means each load needs its own protection element between it and the shared distribution point, and the protection has to be fast enough and sized correctly for that load's current profile.

I'd start by tiering the loads by criticality. A mission-critical load (the main processor, the control loop) gets a dedicated protected branch with its own current limit and, ideally, its own upstream regulation so a fault in a non-critical load can't perturb its supply. Non-critical or high-risk loads (motors, actuators, anything with a history of latch-up sensitivity) get current-limited branches with latch-up detection — either a current-sense comparator that trips a disconnect, or a foldback-limited regulator that limits fault current without latching off. The disconnect should be resettable so the system can attempt recovery rather than permanently losing a load.

The protection topology matters. A simple series fuse is too slow for SEL — latch-up currents can rise in microseconds and a fuse may not clear before the rail collapses or the part is damaged. An active current-limit switch or a hot-swap controller with fast overcurrent response is better. For the highest-criticality branches, I'd consider an eFuse or a dedicated load switch with independent current sensing and a latch-off-and-retry policy. The retry policy is important: a hard latch-off means a transient SEL permanently kills the load, while infinite retry into a hard fault can cook the part. A bounded retry count with a cooldown is usually the right compromise.

I'd also think about the shared rail itself. If several loads share an upstream regulator, a fault in one load can pull the rail down before its branch protection trips. Decoupling and bulk capacitance on the shared rail buy time for the branch protection to act, but the real fix is to make sure the branch protection responds faster than the rail can sag below the other loads' minimum operating voltage. That's a timing analysis I'd want numbers for: fault current rise time, branch trip time, rail capacitance, and the other loads' undervoltage thresholds.

Finally, I'd want telemetry on each branch — current, trip status, retry count — so the system can report which load faulted and the ground can distinguish a transient SEL from a hard failure. That telemetry is what makes the fault-tolerant architecture actually operable rather than just theoretically survivable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between latch-off and retry for a given load, and what would make you change that policy? How would you verify the branch protection timing on the bench without actually inducing a latch-up?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting and qualifying a voltage supervisor or reset IC for a space-deployed system, given that most commercial parts are not radiation-characterized?

**Answer:** Voltage supervisors are deceptively simple parts, which is exactly why they get overlooked — but a supervisor that falsely trips or fails to trip can reset a healthy system or leave a faulted one running. The selection process has to treat the supervisor as a real radiation-risk component, not a passive afterthought.

I'd start with the function it performs and how much error the system can tolerate. A supervisor's job is to assert reset when a rail is out of spec; the key parameters are threshold accuracy, hysteresis, and response time. Under radiation, the concerns are TID-induced threshold shift (which could make it trip at the wrong voltage), SETs on the comparator (a momentary false trip), and SEL (which could latch the output in the wrong state). Without radiation data, I have to assume the threshold could shift by an unknown amount over mission life, which means the margin between the supervisor threshold and the load's minimum operating voltage has to absorb that shift.

For qualification, I'd look for any published radiation test data first — sometimes a part has been tested by a third party even if the datasheet doesn't say so. If not, and the part is critical, I'd budget for a targeted TID test (to characterize threshold drift) and a heavy-ion test (to look for SETs and SEL). If budget doesn't allow, I'd derate hard: choose a supervisor with a threshold well below the load's minimum operating voltage so that even a worst-case upward drift still leaves margin, and add an independent second supervisor or a firmware-based rail check so a single supervisor failure doesn't go undetected.

Architecturally, I'd avoid relying on a single supervisor for a critical rail. A common approach is a supervisor plus a firmware ADC check on the rail, or two supervisors with slightly different thresholds feeding a voting or OR-ing scheme. The firmware check catches a supervisor that's stuck good; the supervisor catches a processor that's hung. Diversity of mechanism is more valuable than redundancy of the same part.

I'd also consider the supervisor's output stage — an open-drain output with a pull-up is generally more robust than a push-pull, because a stuck output can be overridden by another driver. And I'd make sure the reset line has a defined state during power-up and power-down, since a supervisor that glitches during ramp can cause spurious resets.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you test a supervisor's SET response on the bench without a particle beam? What would make you decide a supervisor is critical enough to warrant its own radiation test versus derating and diversity?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real work, and that deserves acknowledgment before any critique — otherwise the conversation becomes about defending the work rather than improving the design. I'd open by saying what's good about the approach and what problem it's solving well, then frame my concern as a question about margin rather than a verdict.

The substance of the disagreement is usually about assumptions. If I think the design is under-margined, I should be able to point to the specific assumption I think is optimistic — a radiation level, a derating factor, a worst-case corner — and ask the engineer to walk me through their reasoning on that assumption. Often the engineer has a justification I hadn't considered, and the conversation resolves itself. If they haven't considered the corner I'm worried about, the question surfaces it without me having to assert authority.

I'd try to make the disagreement concrete and testable. "I'm worried the threshold shift under TID could eat the margin here — can we put a number on the worst case and see if it still holds?" is a much more productive framing than "this isn't rad-hard enough." If we can't resolve it with the data in the room, I'd propose an action: the engineer does a worst-case analysis or finds a radiation test report, and we reconvene. That keeps the review moving and gives the engineer ownership of the resolution.

If after that the design still looks under-margined and the engineer disagrees, I'd escalate the decision transparently — bring in a second reviewer or the systems lead, document both positions, and make the call at the right level. What I wouldn't do is override in the room without giving the engineer a fair hearing, because that erodes the review culture and discourages people from bringing well-developed proposals. The goal isn't to win the argument; it's to make sure the design that ships is the right one, and that the engineer trusts the process enough to keep doing rigorous work.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis turns out to be correct and your concern was unfounded — how do you make that a positive outcome? How do you document a design-review disagreement so the decision is traceable later?

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you keep the review constructive if the engineer becomes defensive? What's your threshold for escalating a technical disagreement versus letting the engineer own the risk?
