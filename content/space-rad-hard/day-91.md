# space-rad-hard — Day 91

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is often the weakest link in a precision measurement chain, so I'd treat this as a risk-management problem rather than a simple part-selection one. First, I'd characterize what I actually need: the required absolute accuracy at end of life, the drift budget allocated to the reference, and how much of that budget the system's calibration scheme can absorb. A reference with no radiation data isn't automatically disqualified — but it does mean I have to either bound its behavior or design around it.

The first lever is architecture. If the measurement is ratiometric, I can drive the ADC from the same reference that excites the sensor, so reference drift largely cancels in the ratio. That converts an absolute-accuracy problem into a stability problem, which is much easier to defend. If the measurement is absolute, I'd consider a periodic in-flight calibration against a known internal standard — a stable bandgap or a zener-based reference that has at least some radiation pedigree — and treat the COTS reference as a short-term-stable element that gets re-anchored. I'd also add a second, independent reference path and compare them; divergence beyond a threshold flags the reference as suspect and lets the system fall back or flag the data.

Second, I'd bound the unknown. Even without a full radiation test campaign, I can do a low-cost TID step-stress on a handful of samples to get a coarse drift curve, and I can review the die process — a buried-zener or a well-characterized bandgap topology has more predictable TID behavior than an uncharacterized CMOS reference. ELDRS is the specific worry for bipolar references, so I'd want at least a low-dose-rate data point or a conservative derating assumption.

Third, I'd design the board so the reference sees as little dose as practical — spot-shielding over the reference and its support circuitry is cheap and often buys a meaningful reduction — and I'd keep the reference thermally stable, since TID drift and temperature drift interact and I don't want to confuse the two during calibration.

Finally, I'd write the requirement and the verification plan together: state the assumed reference drift bound, show how the calibration cadence and the comparison channel keep the measurement inside its accuracy budget even at the worst-case bound, and define the fault response if the two references disagree. That way the "no radiation data" gap is an explicit, owned risk rather than an unexamined assumption.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish reference drift from sensor drift during in-flight calibration? What would make you decide the COTS reference is simply unacceptable and force a rad-hard part?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up is a local fault and must be contained locally — the distribution architecture's job is to make sure the fault's blast radius stops at the load that latched. I'd start by classifying loads by criticality and by their SEL sensitivity, then design the protection tier to match.

For each load, or each small group of loads, I'd put an independent current-limited switch — a rad-tolerant high-side switch or a latching-current-limiter — with a trip threshold set above the load's worst-case inrush and steady-state draw but below the level at which the upstream rail would sag. The key design decision is the trip response: a hard current limit that holds the load in a limited state can keep a latched device from dragging the rail down, but it also dissipates power in the switch, so I'd pair it with a timed latch-off — if the overcurrent persists beyond a defined window, the switch opens and the load is cycled. That gives the latched device a chance to clear on power removal, which is the standard SEL recovery mechanism.

Criticality drives the redundancy. For the highest-criticality loads, I'd want independent feeds from separate upstream converters or separate bus feeds, so a fault in one distribution branch can't remove power from the critical function. For lower-criticality loads, a single protected branch is fine — the requirement is only that their failure doesn't propagate.

I'd also think hard about the upstream side. The primary bus should have its own current limit and, ideally, a fuse-like or electronic breaker element that isolates a distribution branch fault from the bus itself. And I'd make sure the protection coordination is actually coordinated: the downstream switch must trip before the upstream breaker, or a single load fault takes out a whole branch. That means checking trip curves and timing across the tiers, not just picking parts that each look reasonable in isolation.

Telemetry matters too. Each protected branch should report its state and current so the fault is observable — a latch-up that's silently cleared by a power cycle is a latent reliability problem, and I want the ground or the onboard fault manager to see the event count and decide whether the load is degrading.

Finally, I'd verify the scheme against the actual latch-up current the parts can draw, not just their datasheet maximum operating current — SEL currents can be well above normal operating current, and the protection has to be sized for the fault, not the function.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a load whose inrush current is close to its latch-up current, so the protection can't easily distinguish them? What's your approach to coordinating trip timing between a downstream switch and an upstream electronic breaker?

## Q3: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating two different things the engineer is conflating: redundancy of computation and integrity of the input. TMR on the digital side protects against a corrupted computation given a correct input. It does nothing for a corrupted input — if the analog chain hands the voter three copies of the same wrong number, TMR faithfully votes for the wrong answer. So the argument only holds if the analog chain's failure modes are genuinely benign, and for a control-loop measurement that's rarely true.

I'd walk through the analog chain's failure modes explicitly. An SET on the amplifier or the ADC sample-and-hold can produce a transient that looks like a real measurement for one sample; a single-ended topology is more vulnerable to ground-referenced noise and to common-mode shifts than a differential one; and a single amplifier is a single point of failure for both gain and offset. Then I'd ask: what does the control loop do with a single bad sample? If the loop has filtering or rate limiting that makes a one-sample excursion harmless, the exposure is smaller. If the loop acts on every sample, a single SET can drive an actuator.

From there I'd propose proportionate fixes rather than reflexively demanding full triple redundancy on the analog side, which is often impractical. The cheapest high-value change is usually differential signaling from sensor to ADC, which buys common-mode rejection and makes the chain less sensitive to ground disturbance. Next is plausibility checking in firmware — range limits, rate-of-change limits, and a comparison against a redundant or dissimilar measurement where one exists — so a single anomalous sample is rejected rather than acted on. Where the measurement is truly safety-critical, I'd consider a second, independent signal path with a dissimilar amplifier or a different ADC channel, and vote or cross-check at the sample level, not just at the logic level.

I'd also point out that the review question isn't "is the analog side redundant?" but "is the residual risk of an undetected bad measurement acceptable for this function?" Framing it that way keeps the discussion technical and moves it away from a redundancy checklist. And I'd want the engineer to own the analysis — have them enumerate the analog failure modes and show which ones the digital TMR actually covers, which usually makes the gap self-evident without me having to argue it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a rate-of-change plausibility check without adding so much latency that the control loop suffers? When would a dissimilar redundant analog path be worth the cost versus simply accepting and monitoring the risk?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** This is really three problems layered together — feed redundancy, fault isolation, and inrush management — and the design has to satisfy all three without letting any one compromise the others.

For feed redundancy, the two 28V inputs need to be combined in a way that a fault on one feed can't back-feed or drag down the other. The classic approach is ideal-diode ORing — either discrete pass elements with ORing controllers or a dedicated ORing FET controller — so each feed can only source current, never sink it. That gives me reverse-current blocking, which is what protects the healthy feed when the other one shorts. I'd also put independent current limiting and overvoltage protection on each feed ahead of the ORing point, so a fault on one input is caught before it reaches the combining node.

For fault isolation, the ORing stage plus per-feed protection means a shorted or latched load downstream is fed by both inputs in parallel — so the protection has to be sized to clear the fault without either feed collapsing. I'd think about where the fault is: a fault on the load side is handled by the downstream load switch; a fault on one input feed is handled by that feed's own protection and the ORing diode action. The key is that the two protection domains are independent, so a fault in one doesn't consume the other's margin.

Hot-swap is where a lot of designs get into trouble, because the inrush of a large capacitive load looks electrically similar to a fault. I'd use a hot-swap controller with a controlled slew rate on the pass FET gate, so the load capacitance charges at a defined dI/dt rather than a step. That keeps the inrush below the current-limit threshold and avoids disturbing the bus. I'd also add a soft-start ramp on the downstream converters so the bulk capacitance charges progressively. The subtlety is coordinating the hot-swap current limit with the steady-state and fault currents: the limit has to be high enough to charge the capacitance in a reasonable time but low enough that a genuine short trips it. That's a timing-and-threshold trade I'd work out explicitly and verify with a transient load test.

I'd also make sure the ORing and hot-swap stages are rated for the transient energy of a bus hot-swap event — the inductive kick from the harness can be significant — with appropriate TVS clamping and layout that keeps the transient loop small. And I'd want telemetry on each feed's current and fault state so a feed loss is visible rather than silent.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set the hot-swap current limit when the load capacitance is large enough that charging it approaches the fault threshold? What failure mode would you worry about most in the ORing stage itself, and how would you mitigate it?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is make sure I'm actually right before I make it a confrontation — confidence plus real work deserves a real check, not a reflexive veto. I'd ask the engineer to walk me through their margin analysis: what environment they assumed, what the worst-case part behavior is, and how they bounded the parts with no radiation data. Often the disagreement turns out to be about an unstated assumption rather than the conclusion, and once that's on the table it resolves itself. If I still think the margin is thin, I want to be able to point to the specific number or failure mode, not just a general unease.

Then I'd reframe the conversation from "your design is wrong" to "here's the risk I need us to own explicitly." That keeps it technical and non-adversarial. I'd ask the engineer to quantify the exposure — what happens if the part drifts or upsets at the rate we're worried about, and what the consequence is at the system level. If the consequence is a recoverable glitch, maybe the design is fine and I was over-cautious. If the consequence is a loss of a critical function, then the margin question becomes a requirement question, and we can talk about it in those terms rather than as a personal judgment.

I'd also bring the decision into the open rather than settling it one-on-one. In a design review, the right move is to record the concern, the engineer's rationale, and the disposition — accepted with rationale, or action assigned — so the decision is documented and owned by the review, not by me. That protects the engineer's work and protects the project if the assumption later turns out wrong.

If we still disagree after that, I'd escalate on technical grounds, not authority: get a second opinion from someone with radiation experience, or propose a cheap test or analysis that would settle it. A small TID step-stress or a worst-case analysis is often faster than an argument. And I'd be explicit that if the engineer turns out to be right, that's a good outcome and I'll say so — the goal is the right decision, not being the one who made it. The worst thing I can do is win the argument and lose the engineer's willingness to bring me their real analysis next time.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer's analysis is sound but you still believe the risk is unacceptable on judgment grounds? What would you do if the schedule pressure made a redesign impractical and the under-margined design was the only path to delivery?
