# space-rad-hard — Day 102

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The voltage reference is often the weakest link in a precision measurement chain, because its drift maps directly into every conversion result and cannot be distinguished from sensor signal. With no radiation data, I would treat the reference as an unknown and design around that uncertainty rather than assume it behaves. First, I would characterize what I can: initial accuracy, temperature coefficient, long-term drift, and — critically — whether the part's topology is known to be susceptible to enhanced low-dose-rate sensitivity (ELDRS), since bipolar bandgap references are a classic ELDRS risk while buried-Zener or FET-based topologies tend to be more benign. Second, I would build in a calibration path that does not depend on the reference being stable: a ratiometric measurement against a second, independent reference, or periodic calibration against a known on-board standard, so that slow TID-induced drift is observable and correctable rather than silently accumulating. Third, I would add margin in the error budget — allocate the reference a generous drift allowance and verify the end-to-end accuracy still meets the requirement even if the reference drifts to the edge of that allowance. Fourth, I would consider a radiation-tolerant alternative if the budget allows, or a redundant reference with voting/comparison so a large excursion is flagged. Finally, I would plan a low-dose-rate or at least a stepped-dose test on a small sample if any budget exists, because a few data points are far better than none for bounding the risk. The key mindset is: don't let an uncharacterized part sit in the critical path without either characterizing it, calibrating around it, or bounding it with margin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish reference drift from sensor drift in the calibration scheme? What would you do if the reference also exhibits single-event transients that momentarily spike the conversion?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument conflates two different fault domains. TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in registers and logic after the signal has been digitized; it does nothing for a fault that occurs before the ADC, because a corrupted analog value gets faithfully digitized and then faithfully replicated three times by the TMR logic. In fact, TMR can make the situation worse by giving false confidence — all three lanes agree on the wrong number. So I would separate the question into: what failure modes exist in the analog chain (sensor drift, amplifier offset shift, SET on the amplifier output, reference glitch, mux mis-selection), and which of those are credible in the radiation environment. Then I would ask what detection exists for each. A single-ended chain with no comparison point has no way to detect a stuck or shifted analog value. Mitigations range from a second independent signal path with comparison/voting, to a plausibility check in firmware (rate-of-change limits, cross-checks against a redundant sensor or a model), to dithering/injection of a known test signal to verify the chain is alive. I would also point out that "covered" is the wrong framing — the right question is whether the residual risk after mitigation is acceptable for that measurement's criticality. If the measurement feeds a safety-relevant control loop, a single non-redundant path is hard to justify regardless of what the digital side does.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a plausibility check that doesn't itself become a single point of failure? Would you accept a dual-redundant analog path with comparison instead of full TMR, and why?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a fault-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload with multiple loads of different criticality, where a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not take down the rest of the system?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up is a local fault and must be contained locally — the distribution architecture should make it structurally impossible for one load's fault to propagate upstream. I would start by tiering the loads by criticality and assigning each tier its own protection strategy. Every load gets a current-limited switch (e-fuse or load switch with programmable current limit and fast overcurrent response) so that an SEL-induced current surge is interrupted before it can sag the shared rail. The current limit must be set above the load's worst-case inrush but below the level that would disturb the bus — that window is the design tension, and it's why inrush control (soft-start, slew-rate limiting) matters: it lets you set a tighter current limit without nuisance trips. For the most critical loads, I would consider independent feeds or a dedicated regulator per load so there is no shared impedance path at all. I would also add per-load fault reporting so the controller knows which load latched and can attempt a power-cycle recovery rather than blindly resetting everything. Finally, I would verify the architecture by fault injection — crowbar a load and confirm the bus stays within spec and other loads ride through — because the analysis only counts if it's been demonstrated.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the current limit for a load whose inrush is comparable to its latch-up current? What recovery policy would you implement after a load is power-cycled — automatic retry, or latch off until commanded?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** This is really two problems layered: input redundancy and downstream fault containment. For the input side, I would use ideal-diode ORing (or a redundant ORing controller) so either 28V feed can carry the load alone, with the ORing elements sized for the full load current plus margin and arranged so a short on one feed is blocked from pulling down the other — that means the ORing device on each feed must be able to withstand reverse current and the controller must respond fast enough to isolate a faulted input. I would add per-feed current sensing and telemetry so the controller can tell which feed is faulted and report it. For hot-swap, the concern is inrush into the downstream bulk capacitance: I would use a hot-swap controller with programmable slew-rate/dv-dt control and a safe operating area (SOA)-protected pass FET, so plugging in doesn't collapse the bus or stress the FET. For downstream faults, each load branch gets its own current-limited switch as in any SEL-tolerant distribution, so a latched load trips locally. The whole thing needs to be analyzed as a system: simulate or test the fault cases (short one feed, hot-plug, latch a load) and confirm the surviving feed stays within regulation and no upstream protection trips. I would also pay attention to the ORing and hot-swap FETs' radiation tolerance, since they're in the power path and a single-event-induced false turn-on there would be serious.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you prevent a single-event transient on the ORing controller from momentarily connecting both feeds? What derating would you apply to the hot-swap FET given the inrush energy?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing is to separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves acknowledgment before any critique, because if they feel heard they'll engage with the technical substance instead of defending their work. I would ask them to walk through their margin calculation and the assumptions behind it, and I would listen for where the assumptions diverge from the environment requirements — often the disagreement isn't about the math but about what worst case was assumed. Then I would make the concern concrete and shared rather than positional: instead of "I think this is under-margined," I'd say "here's the specific environment condition I'm worried about, here's what I think the part sees under it, and here's why I think the margin closes." That turns it into a joint problem — can we agree on the worst case, and does the design survive it? If we still disagree, I'd propose a way to settle it with evidence: a calculation both of us trust, a datasheet or test report we can both read, or a small test. And I'd be genuinely open to being wrong — sometimes the junior engineer has found something I missed, and saying so publicly builds the trust that makes the next disagreement easier. The goal is the right decision, not winning the review, and the way to get there is to make the reasoning visible and the evidence the arbiter.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the evidence couldn't be produced before the design had to be frozen? How do you make sure a junior engineer feels safe raising a concern about your proposal in the same way?
