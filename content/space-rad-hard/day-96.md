# space-rad-hard — Day 96

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant power distribution scheme for a payload where several loads share a common upstream rail, and a single-event latch-up (SEL) in any one load must not collapse the rail for the others?

**Answer:** The core principle is that a latch-up is a local fault and must be contained locally — the distribution architecture should never let a single load's overcurrent event propagate upstream. I'd structure it as a tiered protection scheme. At the top, the primary 28V bus feed gets a main fuse or latching current limiter sized for the total payload current plus margin, so it only acts on a genuine bus-level fault. Below that, each load branch gets its own independent current-limiting protection — either a latching current limiter (LCL) or an eFuse with a defined trip threshold and a latch-off behavior rather than auto-retry, because a latched CMOS structure will keep drawing current until power is removed. The latch-off is important: an auto-retry limiter can sit in a thermal oscillation, repeatedly dumping current into a latched device and cooking it. Each branch limiter should be sized with margin above the load's worst-case inrush and steady-state current, so normal transients don't trip it, but below the level that would drag the shared rail down. I'd also add per-branch current telemetry so the system controller can identify which branch latched and command a controlled power cycle of just that branch rather than the whole payload. Decoupling and bulk capacitance placement matters too — enough local energy storage per branch that a brief current spike is absorbed locally rather than pulling the shared rail. Finally, I'd verify the scheme against the actual fault current a latch-up can draw, not just the nominal load current, and confirm the upstream rail stays within spec during the worst-case single-branch event.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a latching current limiter and a fuse for a given branch, given that fuses are one-shot? How would you test this containment behavior on the ground without actually inducing latch-up?

## Q2: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has proposed using a single commercial LDO with no radiation data to post-regulate a critical analog rail, arguing that the upstream DC-DC is already rad-tolerant so "the LDO doesn't matter." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a real flaw: radiation tolerance doesn't cascade down a power chain. The upstream converter being rad-tolerant says nothing about what happens inside the LDO — its internal bandgap reference, error amplifier, and pass element are all separately susceptible to total ionizing dose drift and single-event transients. A TID-induced shift in the LDO's reference directly translates to a DC offset on the analog rail, which for a precision measurement path is exactly the kind of slow, mission-lifetime error that's hardest to catch. And an SET on the LDO's error amplifier can produce a transient on the rail that the downstream ADC sees as a measurement glitch. So the right framing is: what is this rail feeding, and what error budget can it tolerate? If it's a precision analog reference or sensor supply, an uncharacterized LDO is a real risk regardless of what's upstream. I'd want to see either radiation data on the part, a documented rationale for why its failure modes don't matter to this particular load, or a mitigation — for example, a topology where the LDO's contribution to the error budget is bounded and monitored, or a different part with at least TID characterization. I'd also point out that "the upstream is rad-tolerant" is actually an argument for spending the qualification effort where it matters, not for skipping it on the downstream part. The constructive move is to ask the engineer to quantify the LDO's contribution to the rail's error budget under TID drift and SET, and let the numbers drive the decision.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the LDO has no radiation data at all, what low-cost steps could you take to bound the risk without a full qualification campaign? How would you distinguish a TID-induced rail drift from an ordinary thermal drift during operation?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is often the accuracy bottleneck in a precision measurement chain, so an uncharacterized reference is a serious concern — but the response isn't automatically "replace it," it's "bound and monitor its contribution." First I'd characterize what the reference actually does to the error budget: initial accuracy, tempco, and long-term drift are usually specified, and the missing piece is TID and ELDRS behavior. Without data, I'd treat the reference's drift as an unknown that must be either measured, bounded conservatively, or calibrated out. A calibration routine can remove a static offset, but it can't remove drift that happens between calibrations, so the question becomes how often calibration occurs and how much drift accumulates in that interval. I'd look at whether the system has a stable on-board or in-system calibration source — for example, a ratiometric measurement against a known quantity, or a second reference of a different technology that drifts differently, so a disagreement between them flags reference drift. I'd also consider whether the reference can be periodically re-validated against a trusted standard during the mission. On the topology side, I'd keep the reference's load current low and stable, since reference output impedance and load regulation interact with drift, and I'd add filtering to suppress SET-induced transients on the reference output. If the error budget can't be met with an uncharacterized part, the honest conclusion is that a characterized reference — or a documented, tested mitigation — is required, and I'd make that case with the numbers rather than as an opinion.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a calibration scheme that catches reference drift without a trusted external standard? What's the risk of using two references of the same technology for cross-checking?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part is that the failure mode is specifically designed to defeat the naive recovery mechanism — a heartbeat that keeps toggling looks healthy to a simple watchdog, so the test plan has to prove that the recovery logic catches a processor that is "alive but not working." I'd start by defining what "executing the control loop" actually means in observable terms: not just a GPIO toggle, but evidence that the loop is completing its real work — a sequence counter that advances only when the full loop runs, a checksum over the loop's outputs, or a challenge-response where the supervisor asks the processor to prove it's doing the right thing and checks the answer against expected behavior. Then the test plan injects the SEFI condition by fault injection rather than radiation: for example, halting the processor in a debugger while leaving the heartbeat ISR running, or corrupting the loop's state so it spins without producing output, or forcing the loop counter to stall. The test verifies that the recovery mechanism — whether that's a windowed watchdog that requires the loop-completion signal, a supervisor that checks the challenge-response, or a cross-checked redundant controller — detects the condition within the specified time and resets or re-initializes the processor. I'd test across the range of SEFI-like states: processor halted, processor spinning in a tight loop, processor running but with corrupted state, and processor running the wrong task. Each case should be verified to trigger recovery and to leave the system in a known-good state afterward. I'd also test the recovery mechanism's own failure modes — what happens if the watchdog itself is affected — and verify the system doesn't enter a reset loop. The plan should include a pass/fail criterion for recovery time and for post-recovery state integrity, not just "it came back."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you inject a "running but not working" fault without a radiation source? What's the risk of a recovery mechanism that's too aggressive and resets on benign transients?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal — the engineer has done real work, and the review should acknowledge that before critiquing the margin. I'd start by asking them to walk through their analysis: what environment they assumed, what the worst-case conditions are, and how they arrived at the margin. Often the disagreement is really about a missing worst-case case rather than a wrong conclusion, and letting them present their reasoning surfaces that. Then I'd frame the concern in terms of the specific failure mode and its consequence, not as "this is wrong" — for example, "here's the condition where this margin collapses, and here's what the system does when it does." I'd bring data: the relevant radiation environment spec, the part's behavior under the missing condition, or a calculation showing where the margin goes negative. If the engineer pushes back with a reasonable argument, I'd take it seriously — they may know something about the part or the application I don't, and the goal is the right answer, not my answer. The constructive path is to agree on what evidence would settle it: a calculation, a test, a datasheet review, or a derating check. If we can't resolve it in the review, I'd assign a specific action to close the gap rather than leaving it as an unresolved opinion, and set a date to revisit. Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative — the review is a place to find problems early, and an engineer who feels heard is more likely to raise the next concern themselves. The decision ultimately has to be defensible on the technical merits, and if the margin really is inadequate, I'd make that call clearly and explain the reasoning so it's a decision, not a verdict.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's analysis turns out to be correct and your concern was unfounded? How do you make sure a "let's get more data" outcome doesn't become a way to avoid a decision?
