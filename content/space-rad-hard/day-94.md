# space-rad-hard — Day 94

## Q1: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant, high-reliability power feed for a payload that has two independent 28V bus inputs, where the system must survive a shorted or latched load on one feed without losing the other, and must also handle hot-swap events without disturbing the bus?

**Answer:** I'd start by treating the two feeds as genuinely independent domains rather than paralleling them into a single node. Each feed gets its own input protection, its own current-limited hot-swap controller, and its own ORing element — ideal-diode controllers or a diode-OR with low-forward-drop devices — so that a fault on one feed cannot back-drive or collapse the other. The ORing stage is the critical boundary: it must block reverse current during a latch-up event on the opposite feed and must not let a hot-swap inrush on one side sag the shared downstream rail.

For hot-swap, I'd use a controller with programmable dV/dt ramp and a defined current-limit profile rather than a simple fuse-and-FET arrangement, so inrush into the downstream bulk capacitance is controlled and the bus transient stays within spec. I'd size the bulk capacitance deliberately — enough to ride through a feed switchover, not so much that inrush becomes the dominant stress. For latch-up survival, the key is that the current limit on each feed is set below the point where a latched load can drag the bus below the undervoltage threshold of the other feed's downstream converters. I'd also add per-load current monitoring so a latched branch can be identified and isolated rather than tripping the whole feed.

Fault containment needs to be thought through end to end: a latch-up on one load should trip that load's protection, not the upstream feed. That argues for a tiered protection scheme — fast per-load limiting, slower per-feed limiting, and a bus-level undervoltage lockout that only acts as a last resort. I'd verify the whole thing with a fault-injection test matrix: short each load, latch each load, hot-swap each feed at worst-case phase, and confirm the other feed and the downstream rails stay within tolerance throughout.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the current-limit threshold for each feed given that the downstream converters have their own inrush and the loads have varying criticality? What would you do if the two feeds are not truly independent — for example, if they share a common return path?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a radiation-tolerant analog-to-digital conversion subsystem where the ADC's voltage reference is a COTS part with no radiation data, and the measurement must hold accuracy over a multi-year mission?

**Answer:** The reference is often the weakest link in a precision measurement chain, and an uncharacterized COTS reference is a real risk because its failure modes are exactly the ones that hurt accuracy: TID-induced drift, low-dose-rate effects that only show up slowly, and single-event transients that produce momentary glitches. My first move would be to avoid making the reference a single point of failure for accuracy. That means either selecting a reference with at least some published radiation data, or architecting around the COTS part so its drift is observable and correctable.

Architecturally, I'd add a stable, low-drift secondary reference — even a simple ratiometric or bandgap arrangement — that the system can periodically compare against the primary reference. If the two diverge beyond a threshold, the system knows the primary has drifted and can either compensate or flag the measurement as degraded. This turns an unobservable failure into a detectable one, which is the most important property for a multi-year mission where you can't service the instrument.

For SETs on the reference, I'd filter the reference output with an RC network sized so the transient is attenuated below the ADC's resolution, and I'd add a plausibility check in firmware: a reading that jumps by more than the physically plausible rate of change for that sensor gets rejected or re-sampled. I'd also consider whether the measurement can be made ratiometric to the reference — if the sensor excitation and the ADC reference share the same source, reference drift largely cancels. Finally, I'd characterize the COTS part as far as budget allows — at minimum a TID step-stress test to the mission dose plus margin — and document the residual risk explicitly rather than assuming "precision" implies radiation tolerance.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish reference drift from genuine sensor drift if both are slow and monotonic? What calibration cadence would you choose, and how would you handle a mission where in-flight calibration opportunities are rare?

## Q3: You're reviewing a design where a junior engineer has used a single-ended, non-redundant analog signal chain — sensor, amplifier, ADC — for a measurement that feeds a control loop. The engineer argues that the digital side already has TMR, so the analog side is "covered." How would you evaluate this argument?

**Answer:** The argument has a category error in it: TMR on the digital side protects against upsets in the digital logic, but it does nothing for a fault that originates in the analog front-end. If the amplifier or ADC produces a wrong value, TMR will faithfully vote on three copies of the wrong value and output it. Redundancy downstream of a fault doesn't remove the fault — it just propagates it consistently. So the analog chain needs its own protection strategy, sized to the criticality of the measurement.

I'd evaluate it in layers. First, is the measurement genuinely control-critical, or is it monitored and rate-limited? If a wrong value can drive an actuator to a dangerous state, the analog path needs diversity or plausibility checking. Second, what failure modes are we actually worried about — SETs in the amplifier, reference drift, ADC gain/offset shift from TID, or a stuck sensor? Each has a different mitigation. For SETs, filtering plus a rate-of-change plausibility check in firmware catches most transient glitches cheaply. For slow drift, periodic calibration against a known reference catches it. For a hard failure like a stuck output, you need either a second independent sensor or a cross-check against a correlated measurement.

The cheapest robust answer is usually not full analog triplication but a plausibility layer: compare the measurement against a model or against a second, dissimilar sensor, and reject or flag values that are physically implausible. That gives you most of the fault coverage at a fraction of the cost and complexity of triplicating the analog chain. I'd frame the review comment not as "your design is wrong" but as "TMR protects the voter, not the input — let's talk about what protects the input."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement a plausibility check that doesn't itself become a single point of failure? If the measurement is truly safety-critical, what would a diverse redundant analog path look like, and what are the matching challenges between the two paths?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a test plan to verify that a system recovers correctly from a single-event functional interrupt (SEFI) that leaves the processor still drawing current and still toggling a heartbeat line, but no longer executing the control loop?

**Answer:** The hard part of this scenario is that the usual recovery mechanisms are defeated: a watchdog that only checks a heartbeat GPIO will see the toggle and conclude the processor is healthy, and a current monitor will see normal draw. So the test plan has to verify recovery from a failure mode that looks healthy from the outside. That starts with defining what "executing the control loop" actually means observably — a sequence counter that advances only when the loop completes, a checksum over loop outputs, or a challenge-response where the processor must compute something on demand. The heartbeat must be tied to loop completion, not just to a timer interrupt.

For the test itself, since you can't inject real radiation on the ground, I'd use fault injection at the firmware and hardware level. On the firmware side, I'd deliberately corrupt the program counter, corrupt a loop state variable, or force the processor into a tight loop that still services the heartbeat timer, and verify the recovery mechanism catches it. On the hardware side, I'd use a debugger or a JTAG-based injection to halt the core while leaving peripherals running, which reproduces the "alive but not executing" state closely. If a particle accelerator is available, a focused beam run on the processor is the gold standard, but the fault-injection plan should stand on its own for routine regression.

The recovery mechanism itself needs to be diverse: a watchdog that requires a specific sequence of writes, not just a toggle; a supervisory microcontroller that checks the sequence counter and can force a reset; and a reset path that is independent of the processor being tested. The test plan should verify each layer independently and then verify the layers together, including the case where the supervisory device itself is the one that's upset. I'd also include a "recovery time" criterion — how long until the control loop is valid again — because a system that recovers in ten seconds may still be unacceptable for a fast control loop.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the watchdog so it can't be satisfied by a processor stuck in a loop that happens to hit the feed sequence? What would you do if the supervisory device and the main processor share a power rail and a latch-up takes both down?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer has proposed a solution you believe is under-margined for the radiation environment. The engineer is confident and has done real work on it. How would you handle the disagreement so that the review stays constructive and the right technical decision gets made?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the person from the proposal. The engineer has done real analysis, and that deserves to be acknowledged before anything else — starting with "this is well thought out, let me push on one assumption" sets a very different tone than "this is wrong." The goal of the review is the right decision, not winning the argument, and a confident engineer who feels heard is far more likely to actually update their view than one who feels dismissed.

Then I'd get concrete about the disagreement. "Under-margined" is a judgment; I'd want to turn it into a number or a scenario we can both look at. What is the actual margin, against what worst-case condition, and what happens at the edge? Often the fastest path to resolution is to ask the engineer to walk through the failure scenario I'm worried about and see whether their analysis already covers it — sometimes it does and I've missed something, sometimes the gap becomes obvious to them once they articulate it. If we still disagree, I'd propose a cheap way to settle it: a bench measurement, a worst-case analysis, or a derating calculation we do together.

If the disagreement persists and the risk is real, I'd escalate the decision rather than the person — bring it to the review board or the systems lead with both positions documented, and let the decision be made on the record with the risk explicitly accepted or mitigated. What I would not do is quietly override the engineer or let the review end with the issue unresolved and unrecorded. The constructive outcome is that the engineer learns how margin is judged in this environment, the decision is documented, and the review process stays something people trust.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's proposal is actually correct and your concern was based on a stale assumption? How do you make sure a junior engineer who was overruled in review doesn't disengage from future reviews?
