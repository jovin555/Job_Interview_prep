## Q1: How would you approach setting up a Zephyr RTOS project using the west build system so that the same source tree produces both a production image and a debug image with extra logging and assertions, without maintaining two separate trees?

**Answer:** The cleanest way to do this is to treat the difference between the two images as configuration, not code. West already gives you the mechanism: a build is defined by a board target plus a set of Kconfig fragments and devicetree overlays, and you can layer those without touching the application source. I'd create a small set of named configuration files — something like a production fragment that disables debug logging, turns off assertions, and selects the release optimization level, and a debug fragment that enables verbose logging, keeps assertions on, and pulls in any debug-only subsystems. Then the two builds are just two west invocations that point at different fragments, both against the same source tree.

The important discipline is that the application code must not contain `#ifdef DEBUG_BUILD`-style branches that fork behavior. Everything should be driven through Kconfig symbols so the build system, not the source, decides what gets compiled in. That keeps the production image auditable — you can dump the resulting `.config` and show exactly what was enabled — and it means the debug image is exercising the same code paths, just with more instrumentation.

I'd also make sure the debug image is the one used in CI for the test suite, so the assertions actually catch regressions, while the production image is what gets built for release and archived with its config. A short README or build script that documents the exact west commands for each variant prevents someone from hand-editing a config and producing an image nobody can reproduce.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you prevent a debug-only Kconfig symbol from accidentally being enabled in a production build? And how would you handle a third variant, say a factory-test image, without the variant list growing unmanageably?

## Q2: How would you approach using a spectrum analyzer with a near-field probe to determine whether a radiated emission at a specific frequency is coming from a switching regulator's harmonic or from a digital clock harmonic, and how would you confirm your hypothesis?

**Answer:** The first step is arithmetic, not measurement. Before touching the analyzer, I'd list the candidate sources and their fundamental frequencies — the switching regulator's switching frequency and its harmonics, and every clock on the board and its harmonics — and see which ones land on the offending frequency. Often that alone narrows it to one or two suspects, and it tells you what to look for.

Then I'd use the near-field probe spatially. A switching regulator's emission tends to localize around the inductor, the switch node, and the input/output capacitors, and it will often show a broad family of harmonics at multiples of the switching frequency. A digital clock harmonic tends to localize around the clock source, the trace carrying it, and the connector or cable it feeds. Moving the probe slowly across the board and watching which region peaks at the frequency in question is the core diagnostic.

To confirm the hypothesis rather than just correlate, I'd perturb the system one variable at a time. If I suspect the regulator, I can change its switching frequency — many regulators allow a resistor change or a spread-spectrum setting — and see whether the emission moves to the new harmonic. If I suspect a clock, I can disable or gate that clock in firmware, or temporarily remove its source, and see whether the emission disappears. A source that moves when you change its fundamental, and vanishes when you disable it, is confirmed. I'd also check whether the emission is common-mode or differential-mode by clamping a ferrite or probing with a current probe on the cable, since that changes the mitigation strategy.

The trustworthiness question matters here: near-field probe measurements are relative, not calibrated absolute field strength, so I use them to localize and confirm, not to declare pass/fail against a limit. The formal number comes from the calibrated antenna measurement.

**Possible follow-ups:** If changing the switching frequency isn't an option because it's fixed by the regulator, what other perturbations could you use to confirm the source? And how would you distinguish a harmonic radiated directly from the switch node versus one coupled onto a cable and radiated from there?

## Q3: How would you approach setting up a differential pair routing constraint in Cadence Allegro for a CAN-FD bus running at 5 Mbps on a mixed-signal medical device PCB, and how would you verify the constraints are actually honored in the final layout?

**Answer:** I'd start by defining the electrical intent before touching the constraint manager: target differential impedance for the pair, an allowed intra-pair skew, and a maximum uncoupled length. For CAN-FD at 5 Mbps the bit rate is modest compared to, say, USB, so the constraints are less aggressive than a high-speed serial link, but the pair still needs to be treated as a pair — matched lengths, controlled impedance referenced to a continuous plane, and minimal stubs. I'd confirm the impedance target with the stackup and the fab house rather than assuming a number, since the target depends on the dielectric and trace geometry.

In the constraint manager I'd create a differential pair object for the CAN-FD nets, assign the impedance and skew rules to it, and set up the physical constraints — trace width and gap derived from the stackup — so the router has a defined target. I'd also set a max uncoupled length so the pair doesn't split apart around a connector or a via field without anyone noticing.

Verification is where a lot of teams get lazy. Defining a constraint doesn't mean it's honored. I'd run the DRC with the electrical and physical rules enabled and actually read the differential-pair violations, not just clear them. Then I'd do a visual and measurement pass: check the actual routed lengths and skew in the constraint manager's reporting, confirm the pair stays coupled through the connector breakout, and check that the reference plane under the pair is continuous — a split in the ground plane under a differential pair is a classic silent failure that DRC won't always flag. For a medical device I'd capture that verification in the design review record so there's traceability that the constraint was checked, not just defined.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the connector pinout forces the pair to separate, and what would you do about the resulting uncoupled length? And how would you decide whether 5 Mbps CAN-FD even needs controlled-impedance routing versus just matched lengths?

## Q4: How would you approach configuring a Keil uVision project for a medical device firmware build so that the optimization level, linker script, and startup code are locked down and auditable, rather than relying on whatever the IDE defaults to?

**Answer:** The goal is that a build is reproducible and that the settings that affect behavior are visible and version-controlled, not buried in a project file someone edited by hand. I'd start by treating the project file itself as a reviewed artifact — it goes in version control, and changes to optimization level, linker script path, or startup file are treated as design changes that get reviewed, not casual tweaks.

For the optimization level, I'd set it explicitly per target configuration and document why. For a medical device, the choice between `-O0`, `-O1`, and higher levels is not just a performance decision — it affects timing, it affects whether debug information maps cleanly to source, and it can expose or mask latent bugs. I'd pin the level that was used during verification and make sure the release build uses that same level, so what was tested is what ships. If the debug build uses a different level, that difference is documented and understood.

The linker script and startup code I'd bring into the project as explicit, versioned files rather than relying on a default the toolchain supplies. The linker script defines the memory map — flash, RAM, any reserved regions for calibration data or bootloader — and that memory map is a safety-relevant fact about the device. The startup code initializes the system before `main`, and if it's the vendor default, nobody on the team actually knows what it does. I'd review it, keep it in the tree, and make sure the vector table and stack setup are correct for the part.

Finally, I'd make the build auditable: a build script or documented procedure that produces the image plus a record of the exact compiler version, optimization flags, and linker script used, archived with the release. That way, months later, you can regenerate the same image and show a regulator exactly what went into it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you catch a situation where someone changed the optimization level locally and the bug only appears at that level? And what would you check in the startup code specifically for a part that has calibration data stored in a reserved flash region?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where a junior engineer has set up the lab's spectrum analyzer and near-field probe for an EMI pre-compliance scan, and you discover the resolution bandwidth and detector mode are set for a quick "get a feel for it" scan rather than the settings the test standard requires. The engineer is confident the settings are fine because "the peaks show up either way." The formal scan is scheduled for tomorrow, and the lab time is booked. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** My first move is to separate the technical issue from the interpersonal one, because both need addressing and conflating them makes both worse. Technically, the engineer isn't wrong that the peaks show up — a fast scan with a wide resolution bandwidth and a peak detector will reveal that energy exists at a frequency. What it won't do is give a number you can compare against a limit, because resolution bandwidth affects the measured amplitude and the detector mode determines how the analyzer reports it. So the scan is useful as a survey and useless as evidence. I'd explain that distinction concretely rather than just asserting the settings are wrong, because "the peaks show up either way" is a reasonable observation from someone who hasn't yet internalized why the standard specifies those parameters.

Then I'd deal with the schedule. The formal scan needs the correct settings, and the lab time is booked, so the practical question is whether we can reconfigure and still use the booked slot. Reconfiguring resolution bandwidth and detector mode is usually a matter of minutes, not hours, so the honest answer is often that we fix the settings and proceed — the risk was catching it, not fixing it. If the booked time genuinely can't accommodate a correct scan, I'd rather reschedule or escalate for more lab time than submit a scan I know isn't to standard, because a pre-compliance scan that doesn't match the standard's settings gives false confidence and can waste far more time later when the formal test disagrees with it.

I'd close the loop with the engineer by making the "why" stick — walking through how resolution bandwidth and detector mode change the reported level, and setting up a short checklist for pre-compliance scans so the next person doesn't have to rediscover it. The tone matters: the goal is a engineer who now owns the standard's requirements, not one who feels blamed for a setup mistake.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the engineer pushed back and argued the wide-RB scan is good enough for a pre-check, how would you respond? And how would you build that pre-scan checklist so it's actually used rather than ignored?
