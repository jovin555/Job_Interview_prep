## Q1: How would you approach setting up a hierarchical schematic in Altium Designer so that a shared power supply block is reused across several board variants, and how would you keep the variants from drifting apart over time?

**Answer:** The core idea is to make the power supply a single authoritative sheet, not a copy-pasted block. I'd create a dedicated schematic document for the power supply, define its ports (inputs, outputs, enables, feedback nodes) as the contract with the rest of the design, and instantiate it as a sheet symbol in each top-level variant. The ports are the interface; anything inside the sheet is private to it, which means I can revise the internal topology without touching the parent sheets.

For variant management, I'd lean on Altium's variant system for the BOM-level differences (fitted/not-fitted, alternate part numbers) and keep the schematic topology identical across variants wherever possible. Where a variant genuinely needs a different topology — say a different input voltage range — I'd rather parameterize the sheet with a parameter set at the instance level than fork the sheet. If a fork is unavoidable, I'd document why in the sheet's parameters and treat the forked sheet as a new revisioned block, not an edit in place.

To stop drift, the discipline is: one library of reusable sheets, a clear naming and revision convention, and a review step that diffs the sheet instances against the master. I'd also keep the variant definitions in a controlled document so that "which variant uses which power block revision" is answerable months later — that traceability matters as much in a regulated program as the schematic itself.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where two variants need the same block but with different connector pinouts? What would you put in the sheet's parameter list to make the contract self-documenting?

## Q2: How would you approach setting up a constraint manager in Cadence Allegro for a mixed-signal PCB where high-speed digital nets and sensitive analog nets share the same design, and how would you verify the constraints are actually being honored rather than just defined?

**Answer:** I'd start by classifying nets before writing a single constraint: physical classes for the high-speed digital groups (by interface and by speed), separate classes for the analog signal paths, and a class for power. The classification is the hard part — once nets are correctly grouped, the constraint rules become mostly mechanical. For the digital side I'd set impedance targets, propagation delay and length-matching tolerances, and differential pair rules where applicable. For the analog side the constraints are different in kind: keep-out and spacing rules from aggressor nets, reference-plane continuity, and often a maximum via count or a preferred layer.

The verification question is the important one. Defining constraints in the manager proves nothing; I'd confirm they're honored by running the DRC with the constraint-driven checks enabled and then spot-checking the actual routed geometry against the rule — measuring the real trace length, the real pair skew, the real spacing — rather than trusting a green checkmark. I'd also check that no nets escaped classification, because an unclassified net silently gets default rules. A short written sign-off that lists which constraints were verified and how gives the review a paper trail.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you catch a net that was added late in the design and never assigned to a class? What's your approach when a length-matching rule conflicts with a keep-out requirement?

## Q3: How would you approach using a spectrum analyzer with a near-field probe to determine whether a radiated emission at a specific frequency is coming from a switching regulator's harmonic or from a digital clock harmonic, and how would you confirm your hypothesis?

**Answer:** The first step is arithmetic, not measurement: list the candidate sources and their fundamental frequencies, then check which ones have a harmonic landing on the offending frequency. A switching regulator at a known switching frequency and a digital clock at a known rate will usually produce different harmonic families, and the emission frequency often only fits one of them cleanly. That narrows the hypothesis before I touch the probe.

Then I'd use the near-field probe spatially. A switching regulator's emission tends to localize around the inductor, the switch node, and the input/output capacitors; a clock harmonic tends to localize around the clock source, its trace, and the receiving end. Moving the probe across the board and watching the amplitude at the specific frequency tells me which region dominates. I'd also check whether the emission tracks the regulator's load or switching behavior — changing load, or disabling the regulator and running from a linear supply, should shift or kill a regulator harmonic but leave a clock harmonic largely intact. Conversely, stopping or slowing the clock should affect the clock harmonic.

Confirmation means changing one variable at a time and seeing the predicted response. If the emission follows the regulator's operating point and disappears when the regulator is bypassed, that's strong evidence. I'd treat a single measurement as a hint, not a conclusion, and I'd want at least two independent observations agreeing before acting.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if both a regulator harmonic and a clock harmonic land on the same frequency? How would you distinguish a genuine radiated emission from something picked up on the probe cable itself?

## Q4: How would you approach configuring a Segger J-Link for a Zephyr RTOS target where an automated test harness needs to flash the device, run a test sequence, and capture a crash dump without manual intervention — and what failure modes would you design around?

**Answer:** I'd drive the J-Link through its scripting/command interface rather than a GUI, so the harness can invoke it headlessly. The sequence is: connect and verify target voltage and ID, erase and program the image, reset and run, then wait for a completion signal from the test — either a pass/fail flag the firmware sets or a timeout. On a crash, the harness needs to halt the core and read out the fault registers and the relevant memory regions before anything else resets the target, because a crash dump is only useful if it's captured before the next reset.

The failure modes I'd design around: the target not being detected (wrong interface, wrong speed, target unpowered), a flash operation that partially completes, the firmware hanging so the harness waits forever, and the debug connection itself perturbing timing-sensitive tests. For each I'd want a defined timeout and a defined recovery — retry the connect, re-flash from a known-good image, power-cycle the target through a controlled relay, and log the failure state rather than silently continuing. I'd also make the harness idempotent so a re-run starts from a clean, known state.

The crash-dump capture deserves its own path: configure the debugger to halt on fault, read the stacked registers and the fault status registers, and write them to a file with a timestamp and the firmware revision. That way a failure in CI is reproducible and traceable rather than a one-off.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you make sure the debug connection doesn't change the timing of the test you're running? What would you log to distinguish a firmware crash from a harness failure?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where a junior engineer has set up the lab's spectrum analyzer and near-field probe for an EMI pre-compliance scan, and you discover the resolution bandwidth and detector mode are set for a quick "get a feel for it" scan rather than the settings the test standard requires. The engineer is confident the settings are fine because "the peaks show up either way." The formal scan is scheduled for tomorrow, and the lab time is booked. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a teaching moment first and a scheduling problem second, because the engineer's confidence is the real issue — "the peaks show up either way" is true and also irrelevant, since the standard specifies settings for a reason and a scan with the wrong ones isn't evidence of compliance. I'd sit down with them, walk through why resolution bandwidth and detector choice change what the measurement means, and show the difference on the actual instrument rather than arguing abstractly. Getting them to see the gap themselves is more durable than me overriding them.

On the schedule: I'd check whether the correct settings can be applied and the scan re-run within the booked window — often the setup change is quick and the scan itself is the long part, so it may be salvageable. If not, I'd be honest with the lab and the project about the risk and look for the earliest realistic slot, rather than letting a non-compliant scan stand in for a compliant one. The one thing I wouldn't do is let the formal scan proceed with settings I know are wrong just because the time is booked; that trades a schedule problem for a credibility problem.

Afterward I'd want the correct settings documented as a checklist or a saved instrument setup so the next person doesn't have to rediscover them, and I'd make sure the engineer owns that artifact — it turns the mistake into something the team benefits from.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer pushed back and said the standard's settings were overly conservative? What would you do if the lab said the only slot was weeks away?
