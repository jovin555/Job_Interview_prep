# embedded-linux-bsp — Day 128

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a situation where the kernel boots and the root filesystem mounts, but a userspace service that depends on a kernel driver starts before the driver has finished initializing, causing intermittent failures on first access?

**Answer:** This is fundamentally a boot-ordering problem, and I'd attack it on two fronts: making the driver's readiness observable, and making the consumer wait on that signal rather than on a fixed delay.

First I'd confirm the diagnosis. I'd check whether the driver's `probe()` is actually completing late — looking at `dmesg` timestamps relative to the service's start, and whether the failure correlates with a slow probe (e.g., a sensor that needs a settling time, or a bus that's slow to come up). If the driver is a loadable module, the module may simply not be loaded when the service starts; if it's built-in, `probe()` may be deferred or slow. Either way, the fix is not to sprinkle `sleep` calls into the service — that's fragile and just narrows the race window.

The robust approach is to expose readiness through a mechanism the init system understands. For a character device, the cleanest option is often to have the driver create the device node only after initialization completes, and have the service depend on that device via a systemd `.device` unit or a udev rule that triggers the service. For a platform driver, I'd consider having the driver register a `sysfs` attribute or a completion that userspace can poll, and have the service block on it. If the service is a daemon under systemd, `After=` plus a proper `Wants=`/`Requires=` on the device unit, combined with `Restart=on-failure` as a safety net, gives deterministic ordering without timing assumptions.

I'd also look at whether the driver itself can be made to finish initialization earlier — for example, moving non-essential work out of `probe()` into a deferred workqueue so the device node appears promptly, while the slow part (calibration, firmware load) happens asynchronously and is gated by a readiness flag the service checks. The key principle is that "device exists" and "device is ready" are different states, and the interface should make that distinction explicit rather than relying on the consumer to guess.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a driver that must be ready before the root filesystem is even mounted — what changes about the ordering problem? What are the risks of using `Restart=on-failure` as the primary mitigation rather than fixing the ordering?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a Yocto BSP for a product that must produce both a production image and a factory-test image, where the factory image needs additional diagnostic tools, a different kernel command line, and a writable root filesystem?

**Answer:** The goal is to share everything that's common — the BSP layer, kernel, device tree, bootloader — and diverge only at the image and configuration layer, so the two images can't drift apart in ways that make factory testing unrepresentative of production.

I'd organize it with a common base: a `distro` or `machine` configuration that defines the SoC, kernel, and device tree, plus a shared `packagegroup` for the runtime essentials both images need. Then I'd create two image recipes — say `image-production` and `image-factory` — where the factory image `require`s the production image recipe and adds its extras. That way the factory image is a strict superset: any change to the production image automatically flows into the factory image, which is exactly what you want for test fidelity.

For the divergent pieces: the extra diagnostic tools go into a `packagegroup-factory-tools` that only the factory image installs. The kernel command line is trickier — I'd handle it through a `KERNEL_CMDLINE` override in the factory image's configuration, or via a separate `u-boot` environment fragment appended for the factory build, rather than editing the shared bootloader recipe. The writable root filesystem is a matter of image features: the production image would use `read-only-rootfs` (and likely an overlay or A/B scheme), while the factory image drops that feature and may add `debug-tweaks` for convenience — though I'd be careful that `debug-tweaks` never leaks into production, which is a classic mistake.

I'd also make the distinction visible in the build: separate `MACHINE` or `DISTRO` variants, or at minimum clearly named image targets, so CI can build both and a reviewer can see at a glance which is which. And I'd document the factory image as a test artifact that is never shipped, so nobody accidentally flashes it onto a customer unit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you prevent the factory image's writable rootfs and debug features from being accidentally included in a production build? What's your approach to keeping the two images' kernel configuration identical when only userspace differs?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging a situation where a custom board running Linux boots successfully, but a GPIO-controlled peripheral (e.g., a sensor power switch) doesn't respond when toggled from userspace, even though the GPIO chip is detected and the pin is exported correctly?

**Answer:** The fact that the GPIO chip is detected and the pin exports cleanly tells me the controller driver is working and the pin is claimed — so the problem is downstream of the GPIO subsystem: either the pin isn't actually routed to the peripheral, the electrical state isn't what I think it is, or something else is also driving that line.

I'd start by verifying the pin's actual state at the hardware level. Reading back the GPIO value via sysfs or `gpiod` tells me what the controller thinks the line is doing, but not what the pin is physically doing — so I'd put a scope or meter on the pin and confirm it actually toggles. If it doesn't, the issue is likely pinmux: the pin may be configured for an alternate function in the device tree, or the pinctrl state may not have been applied because the node is missing a `pinctrl-0`/`pinctrl-names` property, or the pinctrl driver's group definition doesn't match the SoC's actual pin numbering. I'd cross-check the device tree against the SoC's pinmux documentation and the board schematic.

If the pin does toggle but the peripheral still doesn't respond, I'd suspect the peripheral's own requirements: does the power switch need a specific polarity, a minimum hold time, or a sequencing relationship with another rail? A common failure is that the GPIO is toggled but the peripheral needs a delay after power-up before it will talk on its bus, or the switch is active-low when the driver assumes active-high. I'd also check for contention — another driver or a bootloader-left state holding the line, which would show up as the pin reading back the wrong value or as a `gpio` request conflict in the logs.

Finally, I'd check whether the GPIO is being driven by a consumer that isn't the one I think — e.g., a regulator-fixed node in the device tree that's already claiming the pin as its `gpio` property, so my userspace export succeeds but the line is actually owned by the regulator framework. `gpioinfo` and the kernel's GPIO debugfs would reveal who holds the line.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a pinmux problem from an electrical problem without a scope? What would you check in the device tree if the pin toggles correctly but the peripheral still doesn't power up?

## Q4: How would you approach implementing a kernel driver for a device that needs to handle multiple simultaneous I2C sensor reads while maintaining deterministic timing in a medical monitoring application?

**Answer:** The core challenge is that I2C is a shared, relatively slow bus, and "deterministic timing" on it means bounding worst-case latency, not just average throughput. I'd design around three things: serializing bus access cleanly, keeping the critical path short, and making the timing budget explicit.

First, the bus itself is a shared resource, so all accesses must go through the I2C core's locking — I would not try to bypass it. If multiple sensors need to be read "simultaneously," I'd clarify what that actually means: usually it means each sensor has a fixed sampling deadline, not that the reads happen in parallel. I'd schedule reads so that the highest-priority sensor's read is issued first and its completion is guaranteed within its deadline, with lower-priority reads filling the gaps. The I2C core's per-adapter mutex means a long transfer on one sensor can block another, so I'd keep individual transfers short (read only the registers needed, avoid large burst reads on the critical sensor) and consider whether the critical sensor can be moved to its own bus or to a different peripheral entirely if the bus can't meet the deadline.

Second, I'd avoid doing work in interrupt context that could be deferred. For sensors with data-ready interrupt lines, the ISR should do the minimum — timestamp, flag, maybe a single short read if the sensor's FIFO requires it — and hand off processing to a threaded handler or workqueue. For polled sensors, I'd use a high-resolution timer or the kernel's `iio` trigger framework rather than a userspace polling loop, so the timing is kernel-controlled and not subject to scheduler jitter.

Third, I'd make the timing observable. I'd instrument the driver to record per-read latency (issue-to-completion) and expose it via debugfs or tracepoints, so I can verify the worst case against the deadline rather than assuming it. If the deadline is genuinely hard, I'd also consider whether the sensor should be on a bus with better determinism — SPI with DMA, or a dedicated I2C controller — and whether the kernel's real-time scheduling (PREEMPT_RT) is needed to bound the latency of the driver's own execution.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a sensor whose data-ready interrupt fires faster than the bus can service it — what's your backpressure strategy? What role does the `iio` subsystem play here versus a custom character driver, and when would you choose one over the other?

## Q5: Behavioral — You're the BSP lead for a medical device project, and during a design review the hardware team proposes moving the main processor's debug UART to a different pinmux group to free up pins for a new peripheral. The software team says the current pinmux is baked into the device tree and bootloader, and changing it risks breaking early boot console output that the manufacturing line relies on. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a cross-team risk-tradeoff decision rather than a purely technical one, and my job as BSP lead is to make the tradeoff visible and drive it to a decision with the right people in the room.

First, I'd get the facts straight on both sides. From hardware: which pins exactly, what the new peripheral needs, and whether there's an alternative pin assignment that avoids touching the debug UART. From software: what specifically depends on the current UART — is it just the bootloader console, or does the manufacturing line's test fixture parse boot output, or does it rely on the UART for flashing/recovery? The severity of the risk depends entirely on that answer, and "the manufacturing line relies on it" could mean anything from "nice to have" to "the line stops."

Then I'd frame the options concretely. Option one: keep the debug UART where it is and find another pin for the new peripheral — often the cheapest path if the hardware team has flexibility. Option two: move the UART, but stage the change so the bootloader and device tree are updated together and the manufacturing fixture is updated in lockstep, with a transition period where both old and new console outputs are validated. Option three: move the UART but keep a secondary debug path (e.g., a different UART or a USB-based console) so manufacturing isn't dependent on the moved pins.

I'd bring the manufacturing/test owner into the conversation early rather than letting software and hardware negotiate in isolation — they're the ones who'll absorb the risk. I'd also insist on a validation plan: if we move the UART, we prove the new console works on a prototype before committing, and we don't remove the old path until the new one is qualified. The decision itself is the project lead's or architect's to make, but my job is to make sure it's made with the manufacturing impact on the table, not discovered later.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the hardware team says the pin change is non-negotiable and the manufacturing team says the fixture can't be updated in time? How would you document this decision so it's traceable later, given the regulatory context?

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the validation so that a pinmux change is caught before it reaches the manufacturing line? What's your approach to keeping the bootloader and kernel device tree pinmux definitions from drifting out of sync?
