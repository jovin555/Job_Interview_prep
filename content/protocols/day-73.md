## Q1: How would you approach debugging a UART link where the receiver reports framing errors only when a high-current load (e.g., a motor or heater) switches on the same board?

**Answer:** Framing errors that correlate with a switching load point strongly toward a physical-layer or reference-clock problem rather than a protocol bug, so I'd start by separating "the bits arriving wrong" from "the receiver misinterpreting correct bits." First, I'd confirm the correlation is real and repeatable — capture the error with a scope or logic analyzer triggered on the load's switching edge, and look at the actual waveform on RX: is the start bit edge clean, is the bit period stable, is there ringing or a ground bounce at the transition? A high-current load switching creates a fast di/dt, which couples into nearby traces and, more importantly, can shift the local ground reference between the transmitter and receiver. If the UART ground return shares an impedance path with the load current, the receiver's threshold can effectively move mid-bit, and the stop-bit sample lands in the wrong place — which the UART reports as a framing error.

From there I'd work through the usual suspects in order of likelihood: (1) ground/reference integrity — check whether the UART ground and the load ground share a return, and whether a star ground or a separate analog/digital return would help; (2) coupling — is the UART trace routed near the load's switching node or its gate drive, and would a ground guard or increased spacing reduce it; (3) the receiver's clock source — if the MCU uses an internal RC oscillator, its tolerance over temperature and supply ripple can eat into the UART's sampling margin, and a switching load can cause a brief supply dip that shifts the RC frequency; (4) supply decoupling on both ends of the link. I'd also check whether the errors are truly framing errors or whether the UART is misclassifying noise as a break/framing condition, since some peripherals conflate the two.

The fix is usually a combination: improve the ground return, add or resize decoupling, move the UART away from the aggressor, and if the margin is still thin, reduce the baud rate or switch to a differential physical layer (RS-422/RS-485) so the link is no longer referenced to a shared, noisy ground. I'd verify with a soak test that toggles the load repeatedly and confirms zero errors over a long run, not just a single capture.

## Q2: How would you approach calculating the maximum bus capacitance for an I2C bus operating at 400 kHz with multiple devices, and what happens if you exceed that limit?

**Answer:** The I2C specification sets a maximum bus capacitance of 400 pF for both standard-mode (100 kHz) and fast-mode (400 kHz) — the limit doesn't relax at the higher speed, which is a common misconception. The reason is that the bus is open-drain: the rise time is set by the pull-up resistor charging the total bus capacitance, and the spec defines a maximum rise time (roughly 1000 ns for standard-mode, 300 ns for fast-mode) that the RC time constant must satisfy. If capacitance goes up, either the rise time exceeds spec and the receiver samples before the line has settled, or you have to shrink the pull-up to compensate — which increases the low-level current the driver must sink and can push a device past its IOL limit.

To calculate it, I'd sum the contributions: each device's pin capacitance (typically 5–10 pF, from the datasheet), the PCB trace capacitance (roughly 1–3 pF per cm depending on stackup and trace geometry), connector capacitance, and any cable capacitance if the bus leaves the board. For a 30 cm board with several devices, it's easy to approach or exceed 400 pF once you add trace and pin capacitance, especially with long traces or a flex cable. I'd also account for the fact that capacitance is distributed, not lumped, so the effective rise time at the far end of a long trace can be worse than a simple lumped calculation suggests.

If the limit is exceeded, the options are: reduce the pull-up value (within the driver's sink-current limit and the device's VOL spec), split the bus into segments with a buffer or repeater (which also isolates capacitance), reduce trace length or move devices closer, or drop to a lower bus speed where the rise-time budget is larger. The key point is that the 400 pF figure is a system-level budget, not a per-device one, and it has to be checked against the actual layout, not just the schematic.

## Q3: In a system where an SPI master talks to several slaves at different clock speeds and with different CPOL/CPHA modes, how would you approach structuring the firmware so that mode and speed changes are handled safely?

**Answer:** The core risk is that SPI mode and clock speed are properties of the transaction, not the bus, so any code path that changes them must guarantee no other transaction is in flight and that the change is applied before the next chip select asserts. I'd structure this as a per-slave descriptor that bundles the device's CPOL, CPHA, max clock, word size, and chip-select line, and have the SPI driver reconfigure the peripheral from that descriptor at the start of every transaction rather than assuming a global mode. That way the mode is always explicit and can't drift.

The safety concerns are ordering and atomicity. On most MCUs, changing CPOL/CPHA while the peripheral is enabled can glitch the clock line, so the sequence should be: ensure the bus is idle, disable the SPI peripheral (or at least the clock output), apply the new mode and prescaler, re-enable, then assert chip select and transfer. If the chip-select line is a GPIO rather than a hardware-controlled signal, I'd make sure it's deasserted before any reconfiguration and only asserted after the peripheral is stable. I'd also guard against re-entrancy — if the SPI driver is shared across tasks or interrupt contexts, a mutex or a "bus busy" flag prevents one task from reconfiguring mid-transaction of another.

For testability, I'd keep the mode/speed selection in a small table or descriptor array so it's data-driven and easy to review, and I'd add a debug hook that logs the actual mode and prescaler in effect at each transaction so a mismatch shows up immediately in a trace. Finally, I'd verify on a scope that the clock idles at the correct level for each slave and that the first clock edge after chip select matches the expected CPHA, since that's where mode errors usually show up.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a slave that requires a mode change mid-transfer, such as a device that uses one mode for commands and another for data?
- What would you check first if one slave works at its rated speed but another produces corrupted data only at the higher clock?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a protocol abstraction layer so that application code can talk to sensors over I2C, SPI, UART, or CAN-FD without knowing which transport is underneath?

**Answer:** The goal is to separate "what the application wants" from "how the bytes get there," so I'd define a transport-agnostic interface at the level of a sensor transaction — something like read register, write register, or read a block — and implement that interface once per physical transport. The application calls the sensor-level API; a thin dispatch layer maps it to the right transport driver based on a compile-time or runtime binding. The key design decision is where to draw the abstraction line: too low (just "send bytes") and the application still has to know about framing and addressing; too high (a full sensor model) and you lose the ability to handle transport-specific quirks.

I'd keep the interface small and explicit: a handle that identifies the device, a transaction descriptor (register address, direction, length, buffer), and a return code that distinguishes transport errors from device errors. Each transport driver implements the same signature but handles its own framing — I2C start/stop and addressing, SPI chip-select and mode, UART framing and flow control, CAN-FD message ID and DLC. Error semantics need to be normalized too: a NACK on I2C, a CRC failure on CAN-FD, and a framing error on UART should all surface as a transport-level failure with enough detail to log, but the application shouldn't have to branch on which one occurred.

For testability, I'd make the transport layer injectable so unit tests can substitute a mock that returns canned responses or simulates faults, and I'd keep the sensor-level logic free of any transport-specific headers. The trade-off is a small amount of indirection and a risk of over-generalizing — if only one transport is ever used, the abstraction is overhead. But in a medical device where the same sensor might move from I2C on a prototype to CAN-FD in the final architecture, the abstraction pays for itself in reduced rework and clearer traceability.

## Q5: Imagine you're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes using a single shared interrupt line for three different peripherals — a UART, an SPI sensor, and a GPIO alarm input — arguing that it saves pins and the firmware can just poll the peripherals to find out which one fired. How would you guide the team to evaluate this approach?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging the legitimate motivation — pin count matters, especially on a small package — and then reframe the question as "what does the system need to guarantee about response time and determinism?" The shared-interrupt-plus-polling approach is workable only if the worst-case latency of polling all three peripherals is acceptable for the most time-critical one. So the first thing I'd ask the team to do is quantify: what's the required response time for the alarm input, what's the UART's data rate and FIFO depth, and how long does it take to poll and clear each peripheral's status? If the alarm input must be serviced within, say, a few hundred microseconds, and polling the SPI sensor's status register takes longer than that, the approach fails on its own terms.

Then I'd walk through the failure modes. A shared interrupt line means the ISR can't tell which peripheral fired without reading status registers, and those reads can have side effects — reading a UART status register may clear a flag, reading an SPI status may require a bus transaction that itself takes time. If two peripherals fire close together, the ISR may service one and miss the other's edge, or the second interrupt may be lost entirely if the line is level-triggered and the first handler hasn't cleared it. There's also the question of interrupt priority and nesting: if the alarm input shares a line with a chatty UART, the alarm's effective priority is dragged down to the UART's.

I'd guide the team toward a decision by listing the options with their costs: (1) separate interrupt lines — simplest and most deterministic, at the cost of pins; (2) shared line with a fast "which peripheral?" decode using a dedicated status register or a small CPLD — more complex but keeps pin count down; (3) shared line with polling, acceptable only if the timing analysis shows margin. I'd ask the junior engineer to bring a timing budget to the next review, and I'd make sure the decision is documented with the rationale, because in a medical device this is exactly the kind of choice that shows up in a risk analysis.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you structure the ISR if the team decides to keep the shared line, to minimize the chance of missing an alarm edge?
- What would you put in the risk file to justify the shared-interrupt decision if it's kept?
