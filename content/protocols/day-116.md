## Q1: How would you approach debugging an I2C bus where the SCL line is held low by a slave after a power glitch, and the master cannot generate any further clock edges?
**Answer:** A slave holding SCL low is a distinct failure mode from the more common SDA-stuck-low case, and the recovery strategy differs. The first step is to confirm which line is actually stuck — scope both SDA and SCL at the slave's pins, not just at the master, because a level shifter or series resistor can mask the true state. If SCL is genuinely held low by the slave, the master cannot clock out a recovery sequence, so the usual "toggle SCL nine times" trick does not apply. The practical recovery is to force the bus free by cycling power to the offending slave (or the whole bus segment) under firmware control, then re-initialize. For root cause, I would look at why the slave latched: a brownout on the slave's supply that leaves its I2C state machine mid-transaction, a missing or too-slow power-on reset, or a slave that requires a minimum SCL high time after reset that the master violates during early boot. Mitigations include a dedicated load switch or reset line per slave so the master can recover a single device without disturbing the rest of the bus, sequencing slave power-up after the master is ready, and adding a bus monitor that flags a stuck line before the application blocks on it. In a medical device, the recovery must be bounded and observable — the firmware should time out, attempt recovery, log the event, and if recovery fails, transition to a safe state rather than hanging indefinitely.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a slave holding SCL low from a master that has stopped generating clocks due to a firmware fault? What would you put in the device tree or board file to make per-slave reset recovery possible?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between independent chip selects and a daisy-chain SPI configuration when several sensors on the same board have different CPOL/CPHA requirements and PCB space is tight?
**Answer:** The two configurations solve different problems, and the mode requirement usually decides it. In a daisy-chain, all devices share one chip select and one clock, and data shifts through them in series — which means every device in the chain must agree on the same clock mode, because there is only one clock waveform. If the sensors have different CPOL/CPHA requirements, a true daisy-chain is not viable without external glue logic, so independent chip selects are the correct answer even at a pin-cost. The trade-off to weigh is pins versus routing: independent chip selects cost one GPIO per slave plus a star-ish routing of MISO, while daisy-chain saves pins but serializes the data path (a read from the last device requires shifting through all preceding devices) and complicates fault isolation, since one bad device breaks the whole chain. On a space-constrained board I would still favor independent chip selects, but reduce the pin cost by using a GPIO expander or a small decoder for the chip-select lines, keeping the high-speed SCK/MOSI/MISO nets short and shared. I would also check whether the sensors can be strapped or configured to a common mode at the cost of some throughput, since that sometimes makes daisy-chain viable and saves both pins and board area.
**Possible follow-ups:** What are the signal-integrity concerns with a daisy-chain SPI at high clock rates, and how would you mitigate them? How would you handle a mixed-mode SPI bus where one device must run at a much lower clock than the others?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the electrical layer of an RS-485 link that must remain safe if one node's transceiver fails and holds the bus in a permanent dominant state?
**Answer:** A transceiver stuck dominant is one of the nastier RS-485 failure modes because it silently blocks every other node from transmitting, and on a half-duplex bus it can look like a dead network rather than an obvious fault. The design approach has several layers. First, choose transceivers with a defined fail-safe behavior and, where possible, a thermal-shutdown or current-limit feature that prevents a failed part from driving the bus indefinitely. Second, add series resistors or a small series termination at each node's driver output so a shorted or stuck driver cannot hard-clamp the bus to a rail — this limits the fault current and lets the rest of the bus still develop a differential. Third, provide a per-node isolation or disconnect mechanism (an analog switch, a load switch on the transceiver supply, or an isolated transceiver with a controllable enable) so the controller can electrically remove a suspect node from the bus. Fourth, at the protocol layer, implement a bus-idle timeout and a health-check that detects a permanently dominant bus and triggers the disconnect-and-report sequence. In a medical device, the safe state matters: if the bus is stuck, the system should fail toward a defined safe condition and alarm, not silently continue with stale data. I would also verify the common-mode range and fail-safe biasing so that a stuck node does not push the bus outside the receivers' common-mode window and corrupt the remaining nodes' reception.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you detect a permanently dominant bus in firmware without false-triggering on a legitimate long frame? What role does isolation play in a medical device RS-485 link, and when would you choose it over a simple disconnect switch?

## Q4: How would you approach implementing end-to-end integrity protection for a safety-critical CAN-FD payload, beyond the CRC the controller already provides?
**Answer:** The controller CRC protects the frame on the wire, but it does not protect against faults that occur above the CAN layer — a corrupted RAM buffer, a software bug that assembles the wrong bytes, a gateway that mangles a payload, or a stale message that is replayed. End-to-end protection closes that gap. The standard approach is an application-layer wrapper: a sequence counter, a data-ID or message-type field, and a CRC computed over the payload plus the counter and ID, all carried inside the CAN-FD data field. The receiver validates the CRC, checks that the sequence counter advanced as expected (rejecting duplicates and detecting dropped messages), and checks the data-ID against the expected message for that slot. The CRC polynomial and width should be chosen for the payload size and the target residual error rate — a 16-bit CRC is common for short payloads, but the analysis should be documented rather than assumed. The counter must be wide enough that wrap-around does not create ambiguity within the worst-case message age, and the receiver needs a defined behavior for a counter gap: reject, alarm, or request retransmission depending on the safety function. I would also define a freshness timeout so a message that passes CRC but is older than the safety function's deadline is treated as invalid. The whole scheme should be traceable to the risk analysis — the integrity level of the function determines the CRC width, counter width, and timeout.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you choose between a 16-bit and a 32-bit CRC for a given payload and safety integrity level? How would you handle the interaction between the E2E counter and CAN-FD's own retransmission behavior?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes a custom application-layer protocol over UART for a medical device, and you notice the framing has no length-field validation and no sequence numbers. How would you guide the team to evaluate and improve the design?
**Answer:** I would treat this as a teaching and risk-framing conversation rather than a correction, because the goal is for the team to see the failure modes themselves. I would start by asking the engineer to walk through what happens when a single bit flips in the length field — in most naive framing schemes, the receiver then consumes the next several valid frames as payload, silently corrupting data with no error indication. That usually makes the risk concrete without me having to assert it. From there I would structure the review around three questions: how does the receiver know where a frame starts and ends (delimiting and length validation), how does it know the frame is intact (a checksum or CRC over the payload), and how does it know the frame is fresh and not a duplicate or a replay (a sequence number and a timeout). I would also raise the medical-device angle: a silently corrupted frame that the application cannot detect is a hazard, and the risk analysis needs to show that the protocol detects the credible fault modes or that a higher layer does. Then I would let the team compare options — adding a length checksum, a payload CRC, and a sequence counter to the existing framing versus adopting a well-understood framing standard — and document the decision and its rationale so it is traceable. The outcome I want is not "use my design" but a protocol whose fault detection is deliberate, tested, and defensible in the design history file.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you test a framing protocol to demonstrate that it detects the fault modes you identified? How would you decide whether to fix the custom protocol or replace it with an existing standard, given schedule pressure?
