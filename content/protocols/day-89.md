## Q1: How would you approach designing a multi-drop sensor network where one node needs deterministic, low-latency response for closed-loop control while another node generates bursty high-volume telemetry, and both must share the same physical medium?

**Answer:** The first step is to recognize that these two traffic classes have fundamentally different requirements, and a single shared medium can only serve both if the architecture explicitly separates them in time or in priority. I'd start by quantifying the control loop's actual deadline — not just "low latency" but the worst-case acceptable response time and jitter — and the telemetry's peak burst size and tolerable latency. That gives me the numbers to decide whether one physical medium can carry both.

If the control node's deadline is tight relative to the medium's raw bit rate, I'd lean toward a protocol with native priority arbitration, such as CAN-FD, where the control message's identifier gives it deterministic priority over telemetry frames. The key is that arbitration is non-destructive and priority-based, so a high-priority control frame wins the bus without corrupting lower-priority traffic — the telemetry frame simply retries. I'd also reserve bandwidth by capping telemetry's bus utilization (e.g., keeping it well below the theoretical maximum) so the control frame's worst-case arbitration delay stays bounded.

If the medium is something like RS-485 with a polled master, determinism comes from the schedule, not the protocol. In that case I'd time-slot the bus: a fixed, short control poll window at a guaranteed interval, with telemetry filling the remaining slots. The master's scheduler becomes the determinism mechanism, and telemetry must be chunked so it never monopolizes a slot long enough to push the control poll past its deadline.

Either way, I'd validate the design with a worst-case timing analysis before building — compute the control frame's maximum arbitration or scheduling delay under peak telemetry load, and confirm it meets the deadline with margin. Then I'd stress-test it: saturate telemetry and measure control-loop jitter empirically. The failure mode to watch for is a design that works on average but violates the deadline in the tail, which is exactly what a medical control loop can't tolerate.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the case where the telemetry node's burst is so large that even a priority scheme can't guarantee the control deadline — what architectural change would you consider? How would you verify the worst-case timing claim during validation rather than just trusting the analysis?

## Q2: You're debugging an I2C bus where communication works reliably at 100 kHz but produces intermittent NACKs at 400 kHz, with the same devices and the same PCB. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The fact that it works at 100 kHz but not 400 kHz with identical hardware points strongly at an electrical margin problem that only manifests when timing and edge rates tighten — not a logic or protocol bug. I'd approach it as a signal integrity investigation first, protocol second.

First I'd look at the bus physically with a scope: rise and fall times on SDA and SCL, and whether the edges are clean or rounded. At 400 kHz the bus has less time per bit, so a slow rise time from excessive bus capacitance or undersized pull-ups eats into the setup/hold margin and can push a device past its input threshold at the wrong moment. I'd measure the actual bus capacitance against the 400 pF fast-mode limit, and check the pull-up values against the RC time constant for the faster edge requirement. A common cause is pull-ups sized for 100 kHz that are simply too weak for 400 kHz.

Second, I'd check for glitches and crosstalk — at higher edge rates, a fast transition on a neighboring trace can couple into SDA or SCL and create a spurious edge that a slave interprets as a start/stop or a clock, producing a NACK or a stuck bus. I'd look at whether the NACKs correlate with activity on adjacent signals, and whether the layout has long parallel runs or insufficient ground return.

Third, I'd consider whether a specific slave is the culprit — some devices have a maximum supported clock frequency below 400 kHz, or their input filters are marginal at the faster rate. I'd isolate by removing devices one at a time or by testing each slave individually at 400 kHz.

The fix usually falls into a few buckets: reduce pull-up resistance (within the sink-current limits of the devices), shorten or reroute traces to cut capacitance and crosstalk, add series termination if reflections are involved, or drop back to a lower speed for the marginal device. I'd confirm the fix by re-measuring the eye at 400 kHz and running the bus under load for an extended period, since intermittent NACKs often only show up statistically.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the NACK is coming from a specific slave versus a bus-level timing violation? What's the trade-off between lowering the pull-up resistance and the sink-current capability of the devices on the bus?

## Q3: How would you approach calculating the maximum bus capacitance for an I2C bus operating at 400 kHz with multiple devices, and what happens if you exceed that limit?

**Answer:** The I2C specification sets a maximum bus capacitance of 400 pF for fast mode (400 kHz), and that number is the budget for the entire bus — every device pin capacitance, every trace, every connector, and the pull-up resistors' own contribution. I'd approach it as a budget: sum the input capacitance of each device from its datasheet, add the estimated PCB trace capacitance (roughly a picofarad per centimeter depending on stackup and trace geometry), add connector and cable capacitance if the bus leaves the board, and compare the total against 400 pF with margin.

The reason the limit exists is the RC time constant formed by the bus capacitance and the pull-up resistance. The bus must rise from a logic low to a valid logic high within the rise-time window allowed at that speed — for fast mode, roughly 300 ns. If capacitance is too high, the rise time exceeds the window, and a device sampling the line may read it as still low when it should be high, causing missed bits, spurious starts/stops, or NACKs. It's a timing failure, not a hard electrical failure, which is why it often shows up as intermittent rather than total.

If the total exceeds 400 pF, I have a few options. I can reduce the pull-up resistance to speed the rise — but only within the sink-current limits of the devices, since a too-strong pull-up means a device pulling low must sink more current than it's rated for, which can violate its V_OL spec. I can reduce capacitance by shortening traces, removing connectors, or splitting the bus. Or I can use an I2C buffer/repeater, which isolates capacitance into segments so each segment stays under the limit — this is the standard answer for a bus that genuinely needs many devices or long runs. A bus extender or a different physical layer (e.g., a differential I2C variant) is the option when the capacitance is inherent to the topology.

The practical discipline is to do this budget early in the design, before layout, and to leave margin — targeting something like 70–80% of the limit rather than designing right at 400 pF, because real capacitance is higher than the estimate and temperature/voltage shift the thresholds.

**Possible follow-ups:** How does the sink-current limit of a device constrain how low you can push the pull-up resistance, and how would you check it? When would you choose a bus buffer over simply splitting into two separate I2C buses?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a CAN-FD network where a node occasionally goes bus-off and stays off until the controller is reset, even though the bus itself appears healthy afterward?

**Answer:** Bus-off means the node's transmit error counter exceeded the threshold — the controller has concluded it can't reliably communicate and has removed itself from the bus. The fact that it stays off until reset tells me the recovery isn't automatic in this implementation, and the fact that the bus looks healthy afterward tells me the fault was transient or node-local, not a permanent bus wiring problem. So I'd focus on what drove the error counter up in the first place.

First I'd pull the node's error counters and error state history — most CAN controllers expose transmit/receive error counts and the last error code. That tells me whether the errors were transmit-side (the node's own frames were being corrupted or it was losing arbitration badly) or receive-side (it was seeing errors from other nodes). A node that goes bus-off from transmit errors is often the one with a physical-layer problem — a bad transceiver, a marginal termination, a stub, or a ground offset — because its own transmissions are the ones failing.

Second, I'd look at the physical layer specifically at that node: termination resistance measured at the node's connector, common-mode voltage, and whether the node sits at the end of a long stub. A node with a long stub or missing termination can transmit a distorted signal that other nodes flag as an error, driving its own error counter up. I'd also check for ground potential differences between nodes, which can push the common-mode voltage outside the transceiver's range and corrupt edges.

Third, I'd consider whether the errors correlate with bus load or with a specific other node's transmissions — a node that transmits a malformed frame or has a bit-timing mismatch (wrong sample point, wrong bit rate in the data phase) can cause error frames that penalize everyone, but the node with the weakest margin goes bus-off first.

On recovery: I'd want the firmware to implement an explicit, controlled bus-off recovery rather than relying on a manual reset — typically a monitored recovery that waits for the bus to be idle (11 recessive bits) before re-integrating, and logs the event so the root cause can be found. Simply auto-resetting in a tight loop would mask the problem and could thrash the bus. The real fix is finding why the error counter climbed; the recovery mechanism is just the safety net.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a node-local physical-layer fault from a network-wide bit-timing mismatch using only the error counters and a scope? What's the risk of implementing automatic bus-off recovery in a medical device, and how would you bound it?

## Q5: How would you approach handling a situation where a junior engineer on your team has implemented a communication protocol incorrectly, and the error is only discovered during regulatory compliance testing, causing a significant schedule delay?

**Answer:** The first priority is to stabilize the situation before assigning blame. I'd want to understand the actual scope of the defect — is it a protocol conformance issue that affects the device's ability to meet a specific requirement, or a robustness issue that only appears under fault conditions? That determines whether we're looking at a targeted fix or a broader rework, and it drives the conversation with the compliance team and project management about schedule impact. I'd get the facts on the table quickly so the delay estimate is grounded rather than speculative.

Then I'd separate the technical recovery from the people and process questions, because they need different handling. Technically, I'd bring the right people together — the junior engineer, whoever owns the compliance test, and a second reviewer — to do a focused root-cause analysis: what was the intended behavior, what did the implementation do, and at what point in the process did the gap go undetected? That last question matters as much as the defect itself, because a defect that reaches compliance testing usually means a review or test-coverage gap upstream, not just an individual mistake.

On the people side, I'd be deliberate about how I engage the junior engineer. The instinct to shield or to blame are both wrong. I'd frame it as a shared problem to solve, make clear that the goal is fixing the system that let the defect through rather than assigning fault, and involve them in the fix so they build the competence and ownership. Publicly, I'd protect them from being scapegoated; privately, I'd be direct about what went wrong and what I expect going forward. A junior engineer who is afraid to surface problems is a much bigger risk than one who made a mistake.

Process-wise, I'd look at why the defect escaped: was there a protocol conformance test that should have caught it earlier, a code review that missed it, or a requirements ambiguity that the engineer reasonably misread? I'd add the missing check — a conformance test, a review checklist item, a traceability link from requirement to test — so the same class of defect is caught before compliance next time. And I'd communicate the schedule impact honestly and early to stakeholders, with a recovery plan, rather than letting it surface late.

The measure of how this is handled isn't whether the delay was avoided — it's whether the team comes out with a fixed defect, a stronger process, and an engineer who is more capable and more willing to raise issues.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the root cause was an individual performance issue versus a process gap, and how would that change your response? What would you put in place to catch protocol conformance defects earlier in the lifecycle on the next project?
