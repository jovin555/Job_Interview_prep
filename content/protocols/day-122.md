# protocols — Day 122

## Q1: You're debugging a USB 2.0 device that works on most hosts but intermittently fails enumeration on one particular host, where the failure appears as a control-transfer timeout during the GET_DESCRIPTOR phase. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a host-tolerance problem first, not a device-firmware bug, because the device clearly works elsewhere. The GET_DESCRIPTOR timeout is a control-transfer stall, so the first thing I'd do is capture the actual bus traffic with a hardware USB analyzer on the failing host and compare it side-by-side with a passing host. That tells me whether the device is NAKing, stalling, or simply not responding in time — three very different root causes.

From there I'd work through the likely suspects in order of probability. First, timing: some host controllers are stricter about the control-transfer response window, so if the device firmware does anything slow between receiving SETUP and returning the descriptor — blocking on a flash read, waiting on a mutex, servicing a long interrupt — it can miss the window. I'd check whether the descriptor is served from RAM or generated on the fly, and whether the endpoint 0 handler has any blocking calls. Second, descriptor content: a malformed or unusually large descriptor, a wrong wLength, or a configuration descriptor whose total length doesn't match the sum of its interfaces can cause some host stacks to retry or time out. I'd validate the descriptor tree against the spec and against a known-good reference. Third, electrical/signal integrity: a marginal D+ pull-up, a slow VBUS ramp, or a crystal that's slightly off on that host's port can push the device outside the host's tolerance even though another host's PHY is more forgiving.

I'd also test the same device through a known-good hub on the failing host — if it enumerates through the hub but not the root port, that points at the host's root-port timing or power rather than the device. And I'd check whether the failure correlates with the host's OS/driver version, since some host controller drivers have different retry behavior. The fix usually ends up being either making the endpoint-0 handler strictly non-blocking and fast, tightening the descriptor to spec, or adding a small amount of robustness (e.g., ensuring the device is ready to respond before it attaches). The key discipline is to isolate device vs. host vs. cable/port before changing firmware.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a device that's NAKing too long versus one that's stalling? If the analyzer shows the device responding correctly but the host still times out, where would you look next?

## Q2: How would you approach calculating the maximum bus capacitance for an I2C bus operating at 400 kHz with multiple devices, and what happens if you exceed that limit?

**Answer:** I'd start from the spec limits rather than from a rule of thumb. For standard-mode and fast-mode I2C, the bus capacitance limit is 400 pF total, and that budget is shared across every device pin, every trace, every connector, and the pull-up resistors themselves. So the calculation is a sum: each device's input capacitance (from its datasheet, typically a few pF to ~10 pF), plus the PCB trace capacitance (roughly 1–2 pF per cm depending on stackup and trace geometry), plus connector and cable capacitance if the bus leaves the board, plus a small allowance for the pull-ups and any ESD/protection devices. I'd build that as a table early in the design so it's visible and reviewable, not something discovered at bring-up.

If the total exceeds 400 pF, the practical consequence is that the rise time on SDA/SCL gets too slow for the 400 kHz timing budget. I2C is open-drain, so the rise is set by the RC time constant of the pull-up and the bus capacitance; too much capacitance means the line doesn't reach the high threshold within the specified rise time, and you get marginal or failed ACKs, especially at the faster rate. The fix isn't just "use a smaller pull-up" — a smaller pull-up improves rise time but increases low-level sink current, which can exceed a device's IOL rating and eat power budget. So the real levers are: reduce capacitance (shorter traces, fewer devices per bus, buffer/repeater segments), reduce bus speed, or use an active pull-up/bus buffer that isolates capacitance between segments. On a medical device I'd also weigh that a marginal bus is a reliability risk, not just a performance one, so I'd rather split the bus with a buffer than run it at the edge of the limit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would an I2C bus buffer or repeater change your capacitance budget? What's the trade-off between lowering the pull-up value and staying within a device's sink-current spec?

## Q3: You're debugging a CAN-FD network where the bus works fine at the nominal (arbitration) bit rate but nodes intermittently report CRC or form errors only during the data phase at the higher bit rate. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The fact that arbitration works but the data phase fails is a strong clue that the problem is in the high-speed portion of the frame, so I'd focus there rather than on the controller configuration as a whole. CAN-FD lets the data phase run at a much higher bit rate than arbitration, and that higher rate has a much tighter timing budget — so anything that was marginal at the nominal rate becomes a real error at the data rate.

I'd work through it in layers. First, physical layer: check termination at both ends, stub lengths, and whether the topology is a clean daisy chain or has branches. At the data-phase bit rate, reflections and stub ringing that were invisible at the arbitration rate can corrupt the sample point. I'd scope the differential signal at a couple of nodes during the data phase and look at eye quality, ringing, and whether the sample point (set by the transceiver's and controller's propagation delay symmetry) lands in a clean part of the bit. Second, bit timing: verify the data-phase prescaler, propagation segment, and sample point are correct for the actual bus length and transceiver delays — a sample point that's fine at 500 kbit/s can be wrong at 5 Mbit/s. Third, transceiver and controller compatibility: not all transceivers support the same data-phase rates, and some have asymmetric delays that need to be accounted for in the timing. Fourth, the "form error" specifically suggests a node is seeing a bit that violates the frame format — often a symptom of one node's transmitter being marginal or a node sampling at the wrong point, so I'd try to identify whether the errors cluster around one particular node or one particular cable segment.

The disciplined approach is to isolate by substitution: shorten the bus, reduce the data-phase rate, swap a suspect transceiver, and see which change makes the errors disappear. That narrows it to topology, timing, or a specific component rather than guessing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to lower the data-phase bit rate versus fix the physical layer? What role does the transceiver's loop delay play in setting a safe data-phase rate?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a protocol abstraction layer so that application code can talk to sensors over I2C, SPI, UART, or CAN-FD without knowing which transport is underneath?

**Answer:** The goal is to keep the application's view of a sensor stable — "read this register," "stream these samples," "configure this mode" — while the transport underneath is a replaceable detail. I'd structure it in layers. At the bottom, a transport driver interface with a small, uniform set of primitives: open/close, a read/write of a byte buffer with a timeout, and an event/callback for asynchronous completion. Each physical transport (I2C, SPI, UART, CAN-FD) implements that interface, hiding its own quirks — addressing, framing, CRC, flow control, bus arbitration — behind it. Above that, a device/sensor layer that knows the sensor's register map and command semantics but only calls the transport interface. The application sits on top of the device layer and never sees a transport type.

The important design decisions are: (1) define the interface around the *worst-case* transport, not the easiest one — if CAN-FD is asynchronous and message-oriented while I2C is synchronous and register-oriented, the interface needs to accommodate both without leaking one transport's assumptions into the others; (2) keep error semantics uniform — a timeout, a NACK, a CRC failure, and a bus-off should all surface as a small common set of error codes so the application can handle them consistently; (3) make timing and buffering explicit, since a UART may need flow control and a CAN-FD frame has a fixed payload size, so the interface should express "this may block up to X" or "this is fire-and-forget" rather than assuming; and (4) keep the transport selection at configuration/build time, not scattered through application code, so swapping a sensor from I2C to SPI is a wiring/config change, not a rewrite.

I'd also make it testable: a mock transport that can inject timeouts, NACKs, and corrupted frames lets you exercise the device layer and application without hardware, which is exactly what you want for a medical device where you need to demonstrate robustness, not just happy-path operation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a transport that's message-oriented (CAN-FD) versus one that's register-oriented (I2C) without forcing one model onto the other? Where would you put retry logic — in the transport layer or the device layer?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes using a single shared interrupt line for three different peripherals — a UART, an SPI sensor, and a GPIO alarm input — arguing that it saves pins and the firmware can just poll the peripherals to find out which one fired. How would you guide the team to evaluate this approach?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging the real constraint — pin count and board space are legitimate concerns, and the engineer isn't wrong that sharing an interrupt can work. Then I'd reframe the review around the question that actually matters: what does the system lose in latency, determinism, and diagnosability, and is that acceptable for this device?

The core issue is that a shared interrupt turns a hardware event into a software search. When the line asserts, the ISR has to poll each peripheral to find the source, which adds latency and, more importantly, makes the worst-case response time depend on how many peripherals are queried and in what order. For a UART that's fine — it has a buffer and can tolerate a few microseconds. For an SPI sensor it depends on the data rate and whether the sensor has a FIFO. But for a GPIO alarm input, especially in a medical device, the alarm may be the most time-critical event in the system, and putting it behind a poll of two other peripherals is a determinism risk I'd want quantified, not assumed away. I'd also raise the failure modes: if two peripherals assert simultaneously, the ISR must handle both; if a peripheral's interrupt is level-sensitive and the condition isn't cleared promptly, the shared line can stay asserted and starve the others; and if one peripheral's interrupt is misconfigured, it can mask the alarm entirely. Diagnosability matters too — with separate lines, a scope tells you exactly what fired; with a shared line, you're inferring.

So I'd guide the team to a decision by asking for: the measured or estimated worst-case latency for the alarm path through the shared ISR, a clear priority and clearing strategy for each source, and a plan for how the design would be verified (e.g., fault injection where two sources assert together). If the alarm can tolerate the latency and the clearing logic is robust, sharing may be acceptable; if not, I'd push for at least the alarm on its own line, or an interrupt controller/expander that gives per-source status. The point of the review isn't to win the argument — it's to make the trade-off explicit and documented, which is also what a regulatory reviewer will want to see.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the shared ISR so that a stuck level-sensitive source can't starve the others? What would you want to see in the design documentation to show the shared-interrupt latency was actually analyzed?
