## Q1: How would you approach designing a fail-safe mechanism for an RS-485 network where a sensor node must never hold the bus in a dominant state if its firmware crashes or the node loses power mid-transmission?
**Answer:** The first principle is that a single node must never be able to take down the entire bus, especially in a medical context where other nodes may still be transmitting safety-relevant data. I'd approach this at multiple layers. At the hardware level, I'd look at the driver enable pin — it should be controlled by a circuit that defaults to receive mode on power-up or reset, not transmit. Many RS-485 transceivers have a receiver-enable and driver-enable pin; I'd ensure the driver-enable is pulled to the disabled state through a resistor so that even if the microcontroller's GPIO is floating during boot, the node can't drive the bus. I'd also consider a watchdog circuit that can physically disable the driver if firmware stops toggling a heartbeat line — this could be an external watchdog timer IC whose output gates the driver-enable signal. At the firmware level, I'd implement a transmit timeout: before enabling the driver, the software arms a timer; if the transmission isn't complete within the expected window, the firmware forces the driver disabled and enters an error state. This handles the case where the firmware is alive but stuck in a loop. For the power-loss scenario, the transceiver's driver-enable pin should be pulled such that when VCC collapses, the driver output goes to a high-impedance state — most transceivers do this inherently, but I'd verify it in the datasheet and test it. Finally, I'd add bus-level protection: a bias network or a bus guardian that detects a sustained dominant state beyond a valid frame length and disconnects the offending node. In practice, I'd also test the failure modes explicitly — pulling the driver-enable high while the node is unpowered, crashing the firmware mid-byte, and disconnecting power mid-frame — to confirm the bus recovers and other nodes can continue communicating.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the watchdog timeout so it doesn't interfere with normal long messages? What happens if the bus guardian itself fails — how do you make that failure safe?

## Q2: You're debugging a system where an SPI bus communicates with three sensors on separate chip selects. The bus works correctly at 1 MHz but produces corrupted data at 10 MHz. How would you approach this?
**Answer:** I'd start by recognizing that this is almost certainly a signal-integrity or timing-margin problem rather than a logic error, since the bus works at the lower frequency. The first thing I'd do is look at the physical layer: check the actual PCB trace lengths from the master to each slave, look for vias, and measure the signal quality at the slave's pins using an oscilloscope. At 10 MHz, the bit period is 100 ns, so even modest trace lengths, stray capacitance, or ringing can eat into setup/hold margins. I'd probe the clock line and the data line simultaneously at the slave device to check for overshoot, undershoot, and slow edges. I'd also verify the clock polarity and phase settings match what each sensor actually expects — a mismatch can be marginal at low speed but fail at high speed. Another common issue is that the master's clock is derived from a PLL or divider that introduces jitter, or the SPI peripheral's clock configuration isn't actually producing the intended frequency. I'd check the actual clock frequency and duty cycle on the scope rather than trusting the configuration. I'd also look at the slave's datasheet for the maximum clock rise/fall time specification — if the master's output drive strength is too low or too high, the edges may be too slow or ring excessively. If the scope shows clean signals, I'd then suspect the slave's internal timing: some sensors have a maximum clock frequency that's lower than the bus speed, or they need a minimum idle time between transactions that isn't being met. I'd also check whether the chip-select lines are properly deasserted between transactions and whether there's any contention on MISO — if one slave's MISO isn't tri-stating properly when its chip select is high, it could corrupt reads from other slaves at higher speeds. Finally, I'd try reducing the clock speed in steps (8, 6, 4 MHz) to find where the failure starts — that boundary often points to whether it's a setup/hold issue (gradual) or a resonance/reflection issue (sharp threshold).

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific oscilloscope measurements would you take to distinguish between a signal-integrity problem and a timing-margin problem? How would you decide whether to fix this in firmware (lower clock, adjust phase) versus hardware (termination, drive strength)?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting between USB 2.0 interrupt transfers and isochronous transfers for a medical device that streams real-time sensor data to a host computer, with a requirement that no data be lost?
**Answer:** The key tension here is that isochronous transfers provide guaranteed bandwidth and low latency but have no retry mechanism — if a packet is corrupted, it's simply dropped. Interrupt transfers, despite the name, are essentially low-rate bulk transfers with a guaranteed maximum latency; they do get retried on error, but the host polls them at a limited rate. For a medical device with a hard "no data lost" requirement, I'd first question whether the data truly needs to be continuous or whether the application can tolerate gaps with appropriate error handling. If no data can be lost, isochronous alone is not appropriate because the USB protocol itself doesn't guarantee delivery. I'd consider a hybrid approach: use an isochronous endpoint for the real-time stream where latency matters, but also buffer the data locally on the device and provide a mechanism for the host to request retransmission of any missing frames — perhaps through a bulk endpoint or a control transfer. This gives the low-latency stream for real-time display while ensuring a complete record can be reconstructed. Alternatively, if the data rate is modest — say a few kilobytes per second — interrupt transfers with a short polling interval might be sufficient, since the USB 2.0 spec allows interrupt endpoints to be polled as frequently as every 1 ms (or even 125 µs with micro-frames). The trade-off is that interrupt transfers share bandwidth with other traffic and can experience jitter. I'd also look at the actual data rate: if the sensor produces, say, 100 samples per second of 16-bit data, that's only 200 bytes per second — well within interrupt transfer capability. The real question is what "no data lost" means in context: does the host need every sample in real time, or can it tolerate a short delay while missing data is retransmitted? I'd also consider the host application's buffering — even with a reliable transport, the host software needs to handle scheduling jitter and buffer overruns. For a medical device, I'd document the chosen approach in the risk management file, identifying what happens if data is lost despite the transport mechanism, and what the clinical consequence would be.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you implement the retransmission mechanism for missing isochronous data? What if the host application can't tolerate the latency of a retransmission request — how would you redesign?

## Q4: Imagine you're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes using a single CAN-FD bus for a medical device that carries both a safety-critical control message requiring delivery within 2 ms and high-volume telemetry from multiple sensors. The engineer argues that CAN-FD's higher data rate in the data phase eliminates any bandwidth concerns. How would you guide the team to evaluate this approach?
**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that CAN-FD does offer a significant bandwidth improvement over classic CAN, but I'd steer the discussion toward the real question: is the bus utilization and worst-case latency actually acceptable, not just the average throughput? The higher data-phase bit rate reduces the time each frame occupies the bus, but it doesn't change the fundamental arbitration mechanism — a lower-priority frame can still be delayed by higher-priority traffic, and a burst of telemetry can delay the safety-critical message if priorities aren't configured correctly. I'd guide the team to do a proper worst-case analysis: calculate the maximum time the safety-critical message could wait, considering the longest possible non-preemptable transmission (the longest CAN-FD frame at the arbitration bit rate) plus any queued higher-priority traffic. I'd also ask about the telemetry generation pattern — if sensors burst data at the same time, the bus could saturate momentarily even if average utilization is low. I'd suggest the team model the bus with a spreadsheet or simulation tool, varying the telemetry rate and message sizes to find the worst-case response time for the safety-critical message. I'd also raise the question of whether the telemetry and control traffic should share a bus at all — in some medical devices, separating safety-critical control onto a dedicated bus (or at least a dedicated CAN-ID range with strict priority) simplifies the analysis and reduces the risk of a telemetry fault affecting control. If they do share the bus, I'd recommend: assigning the safety-critical message the highest priority, limiting the size of telemetry frames so they don't monopolize the bus, implementing a transmission rate limiter on telemetry nodes, and adding a bus-off recovery strategy that prioritizes the control node. I'd also ask the engineer to consider what happens during fault conditions — if a telemetry node malfunctions and transmits continuously, does the control message still get through? CAN's error handling should eventually cause the faulty node to go bus-off, but the time to recovery needs to be within the 2 ms budget. Finally, I'd suggest they document the worst-case latency analysis and the assumptions behind it, since this will be needed for the regulatory submission.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the worst-case latency analysis empirically? What if the analysis shows the 2 ms requirement can't be met — what architectural changes would you consider?

## Q5: How would you approach handling a situation where a junior engineer on your team has implemented a communication protocol incorrectly, and the error is only discovered during regulatory compliance testing, causing a significant schedule delay?
**Answer:** I'd approach this in two phases: first, address the immediate technical and schedule problem; second, understand how the error happened and what process changes prevent recurrence. For the immediate issue, I'd call a focused meeting with the junior engineer, the test engineer, and relevant stakeholders to understand exactly what failed and what the compliance impact is. I'd want to know: Is this a protocol implementation error that can be fixed in firmware, or does it require a hardware change? Does the non-compliance affect patient safety, or is it a documentation/process issue? I'd work with the team to scope the fix and estimate the schedule impact realistically — it's better to be honest about the delay than to rush a fix and risk another failure. I'd also check whether the error indicates a gap in our verification process: did we have a protocol conformance test plan, and if so, why didn't it catch this? For the junior engineer, I'd avoid blame and instead focus on coaching. I'd review the implementation together, identify where the misunderstanding originated — perhaps an ambiguous specification, a datasheet misinterpretation, or a lack of familiarity with the protocol's edge cases — and use it as a teaching opportunity. I'd also make sure they're involved in the fix and the re-testing, so they own the solution rather than feeling like it was taken away from them. For the process side, I'd lead a root-cause analysis using a structured method like 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram, looking at contributing factors: Was the requirement clear? Was there a peer review of the protocol implementation? Did the test plan cover this specific aspect? Were there assumptions made about how the compliance tester would interpret the standard? I'd then propose concrete improvements — for example, adding a protocol conformance checklist, involving the compliance engineer earlier in development, or creating a test harness that exercises the protocol against a reference implementation. Finally, I'd communicate transparently with management about the delay, the fix plan, and the preventive measures, framing it as an investment in quality rather than just a setback.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you balance supporting the junior engineer's development with the pressure to deliver on schedule? What would you do if the junior engineer becomes defensive or disengaged after the incident?
