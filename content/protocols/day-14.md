## Q1: How would you approach designing a mixed-signal PCB layout for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the analog sensor front-end and the digital communication interface (e.g., SPI or I2C) share the same board, and you're concerned about noise coupling into the sensitive analog measurements?

**Answer:** I'd start by establishing a clear physical and electrical separation strategy between the analog and digital domains. The first step is a careful component placement review: the analog front-end, including the sensor, precision reference, and any gain stages, should be placed in a dedicated zone away from the microcontroller, communication transceivers, and any switching regulators. I'd avoid routing digital communication traces—especially clock lines—under or parallel to analog signal paths. A solid, unbroken ground plane is essential; I'd partition it into analog and digital sections joined at a single point, typically under the ADC or at the star ground location, to prevent digital return currents from flowing through the analog reference path. For the communication interface itself, I'd pay attention to series termination on clock and data lines to reduce ringing and high-frequency harmonics, and I'd use local decoupling capacitors close to every digital IC. If the sensor signal is very low-level, I might also consider adding a small guard ring or shielding trace around the analog input. Finally, I'd verify the layout with a pre-compliance EMC scan and, more importantly, measure the actual noise floor at the ADC input with the digital interface actively communicating—this tells you whether the separation strategy is actually working in practice.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether to use a split ground plane versus a single solid ground plane with careful component placement?
- What specific measurements would you take to quantify the noise coupling before committing to a board revision?

## Q2: You're debugging a system where a sensor on an SPI bus communicates correctly at 1 MHz but produces corrupted data at 8 MHz, even though the sensor datasheet specifies operation up to 10 MHz. The PCB traces are approximately 5 centimeters long. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** At 8 MHz with 5 cm traces, this is likely a signal integrity issue rather than a fundamental speed limitation. I'd start by looking at the SPI timing parameters in the datasheet—specifically setup and hold times relative to the clock edge. At higher clock speeds, the timing margin shrinks, and even small propagation delays or clock skew can violate the sensor's requirements. I'd check whether the master's clock polarity and phase settings match what the sensor expects; a mismatch that's tolerable at low speed can become fatal at higher speeds. I'd also examine the signal quality with an oscilloscope: look for ringing, overshoot, and slow rise/fall times caused by trace capacitance combined with the driver's output impedance. If the edges are degraded, I'd add series termination resistors (typically 22–33 ohms) at the master's output to match the trace impedance and reduce reflections. I'd also verify that the MISO line isn't being contended by another slave—a slave that doesn't tri-state its output properly when its chip select is deasserted can corrupt reads at higher speeds. Finally, I'd check the power supply decoupling at the sensor; if the sensor's internal logic is browning out during fast switching, that can cause intermittent data corruption that only appears at higher clock rates.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine whether the issue is timing margin versus signal integrity without access to a high-bandwidth oscilloscope?
- What would you look for in the sensor's datasheet to identify the most likely timing parameter being violated?

## Q3: How would you approach implementing a fault-tolerant communication strategy for a medical device where a safety-critical sensor reading must be delivered reliably, but the physical link (e.g., RS-485 or CAN-FD) is subject to intermittent interference from external equipment?

**Answer:** I'd approach this from multiple layers rather than relying on any single mechanism. At the physical layer, I'd ensure proper termination, fail-safe biasing, and shielding to minimize the susceptibility to interference in the first place. At the protocol layer, I'd implement a message structure with a strong CRC—for CAN-FD, that might mean using the 17-bit or 21-bit CRC in the data phase, plus an end-to-end CRC at the application layer if the message passes through intermediate nodes. I'd also include a sequence counter and timestamp in every message so the receiver can detect missing or out-of-order data. For the retransmission strategy, I'd design a scheme where the safety-critical message is sent with a bounded number of retries, but crucially, I'd also implement a "stale data" detection mechanism—if a message hasn't been refreshed within a defined interval, the receiving system must enter a safe state rather than continue using outdated readings. I'd also consider redundant transmission paths if the system architecture allows: for example, sending the critical data over two independent physical channels or using a secondary communication method as a backup. Finally, I'd characterize the failure modes during system testing by injecting controlled interference and measuring the error detection and recovery behavior, ensuring that the system degrades gracefully and predictably.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide between retransmission versus accepting a missed message and moving to a safe state?
- What metrics would you use to validate that the fault-tolerant strategy meets the safety requirements?

## Q4: Imagine you're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes using a single CAN-FD bus for a medical device system that carries both a safety-critical control message requiring delivery within 2 ms and high-volume telemetry data from multiple sensors. The engineer argues that CAN-FD's higher data rate in the data phase will solve any bandwidth concerns. How would you guide the team to evaluate this approach?

**Answer:** I'd guide the team to separate the two concerns: bandwidth and determinism. CAN-FD's higher data phase bit rate does increase throughput, but it doesn't inherently guarantee bounded latency for the critical message—that depends on arbitration priority and the worst-case blocking time from lower-priority frames. I'd ask the engineer to walk through a worst-case timing analysis: what's the longest time the critical message could wait if every lower-priority node starts transmitting simultaneously? With CAN-FD, you also need to consider that a lower-priority frame, once it wins arbitration, cannot be preempted—so a long telemetry frame in the data phase could delay the critical message beyond the 2 ms budget. I'd suggest calculating the worst-case queuing delay using the standard CAN schedulability analysis, accounting for the actual frame lengths at the proposed bit rates. If the analysis shows the 2 ms bound can't be guaranteed, I'd explore options: increasing the priority of the critical message (which may already be at the highest priority), reducing the maximum length of telemetry frames, or splitting the traffic onto two separate buses. I'd also ask the engineer to consider what happens during error conditions—CAN's error recovery mechanisms can add significant delay, and that worst-case must be included in the analysis. The key message is that throughput and latency are different problems, and the design must be proven against the latency requirement, not just the aggregate bandwidth.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you perform the worst-case latency calculation for a CAN-FD network with mixed message types?
- What would you consider a safe margin between the calculated worst-case latency and the 2 ms requirement?

## Q5: How would you approach developing a test strategy for verifying that a medical device's communication interfaces (e.g., I2C, SPI, UART, USB) remain reliable under extended operation, given that intermittent failures often only appear after hours or days of continuous use?

**Answer:** I'd structure the test strategy around three pillars: stress testing, targeted fault injection, and long-duration soak testing. For stress testing, I'd push the interfaces to their specified limits—maximum clock speeds, worst-case bus loading, and maximum cable lengths—because marginal designs often fail first at the extremes. I'd also vary environmental conditions like temperature and supply voltage, since timing margins shrink as components heat up or as the battery voltage sags. For fault injection, I'd deliberately introduce the kinds of disturbances that cause intermittent failures in the field: glitches on the bus lines, momentary power supply dips, clock stretching on I2C, bus contention on SPI, and electromagnetic interference from nearby equipment. The goal is to verify that the error detection and recovery mechanisms actually work—for example, that an I2C bus hang is detected and cleared, or that a UART framing error triggers a retransmission. For soak testing, I'd run representative communication patterns continuously for extended periods, logging every error event with timestamps and system state. The key is to automate the monitoring so that even a single bit error over a week of operation is captured. I'd also implement a "health check" mechanism in the firmware itself—periodic loopback tests or CRC-verified transactions—that can detect degradation before it becomes a complete failure. Finally, I'd analyze any failures found during testing using root-cause techniques like 8D, because an intermittent communication failure is often a symptom of a deeper issue—a marginal timing parameter, a weak driver, or an inadequate decoupling capacitor—that needs to be fixed at the design level, not just patched in firmware.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide how long a soak test needs to run to give you confidence in reliability?
- What specific fault-injection techniques would you prioritize for a device that uses multiple communication protocols?
