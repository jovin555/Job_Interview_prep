# protocols — Day 118

## Q1: How would you approach debugging an I2C bus where a device intermittently NACKs its address, but only at 400 kHz and only after the board has been running warm for a while?

**Answer:** This pattern — speed-dependent and temperature-dependent — points at marginal timing rather than a logic bug, so I'd treat it as a signal-integrity and timing-margin problem first. At 400 kHz the bus has roughly a quarter of the bit period of 100 kHz, so rise times that were comfortably absorbed at the slower rate can now eat into setup/hold margins. I'd start by scoping SCL and SDA at the failing device with a fast probe and a short ground lead, looking at rise time, overshoot, and whether the edges are clean or rounded. A slow rise time from excessive bus capacitance or undersized pull-ups is the classic culprit: the bus still works when cool, but as the board warms, leakage and driver characteristics shift and the effective margin shrinks until the device samples an invalid level and NACKs.

I'd measure the actual bus capacitance and compare against the 400 pF fast-mode budget, then recompute the pull-up from the rise-time requirement (t_r ≈ 0.847 × R × C for the 30–70% window, checked against the 300 ns fast-mode limit) rather than just picking a "standard" value. I'd also check whether the pull-up is a single resistor or distributed, and whether any series resistors or long stubs are adding delay. On the thermal side, I'd use freeze spray or a heat gun to confirm the correlation and localize which component is temperature-sensitive — sometimes it's the slave's own input thresholds drifting, sometimes it's a regulator or level shifter in the pull-up path. If the margin is genuinely thin, options are lowering the bus speed for that segment, reducing capacitance (shorter traces, fewer stubs), or using an active pull-up/bus buffer. I'd also verify the slave's timing spec against the master's actual clock, since some parts have minimum bus-free and setup times that are easy to violate at 400 kHz.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a pull-up/capacitance problem from a slave input-threshold problem with just a scope and a datasheet? If you can't change the hardware, what firmware-side mitigations would you consider?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring firmware so that an SPI master can talk to several slaves requiring different clock speeds and different CPOL/CPHA modes, without mode changes corrupting transactions?

**Answer:** The core risk is that changing CPOL/CPHA or clock speed mid-stream can produce a spurious clock edge or an undefined idle level that a slave interprets as a partial transaction. So the first principle is: never change mode or speed while a chip select is asserted, and never leave a slave selected across a reconfiguration. I'd structure the driver around a per-device descriptor that carries its mode, max clock, word size, and CS timing, and have a single "begin transaction" path that (1) deasserts all CS lines, (2) reconfigures the SPI peripheral to the target device's mode and speed, (3) waits for the peripheral to be idle and for the bus to settle at the new idle level, then (4) asserts only that device's CS. The "end transaction" path deasserts CS and returns the bus to a safe idle state.

The subtle part is the idle-level transition. If you switch from a mode where SCLK idles low to one where it idles high, the line has to move while no slave is selected, otherwise a selected slave sees an extra edge. So the ordering — deselect, reconfigure, settle, select — matters more than the reconfiguration itself. I'd also make the CS lines GPIO-controlled where possible so the driver owns the exact timing rather than relying on the peripheral's automatic CS, which may not respect the settle delay. On the RTOS side, I'd wrap each device in its own bus-access mutex or use a bus manager so two tasks can't interleave a reconfiguration with another device's transaction. For testability, I'd expose the descriptor table and add a loopback or scope-based test that exercises every mode/speed combination in sequence to catch any edge artifacts.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device that requires a minimum delay between CS deassert and the next CS assert on the same bus? What would you do if the SPI peripheral can't change CPOL/CPHA without a full disable/enable cycle?

## Q3: You're debugging a CAN-FD network where a node occasionally goes bus-off and stays off until the controller is reset, even though the bus itself appears healthy afterward. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Bus-off means the transmit error counter passed 255, so the node accumulated enough errors to be removed from the bus — and the fact that it stays off until reset tells me the recovery isn't automatic in this design, which is itself worth questioning. I'd first separate "why did it go bus-off" from "why didn't it recover." For the first, I'd look at the error counters and error frames leading up to the event: a node that goes bus-off is usually the one seeing errors first, which often means it's the victim of a physical-layer problem (bad termination, a stub, a marginal transceiver, common-mode shift) rather than the cause. I'd scope the bus during the event, check for reflections and ringing at the data-phase bit rate, and verify termination and stub lengths against the CAN-FD data-rate/length trade-off — CAN-FD's faster data phase tightens the physical-layer budget considerably compared to classic CAN.

I'd also check whether the node is transmitting at a data-phase bit rate or sample point that doesn't match the rest of the network, since a mismatch produces errors concentrated on that node's frames. On the recovery side, I'd look at the controller's bus-off recovery configuration — many controllers support automatic bus-off recovery after 128 occurrences of 11 recessive bits, and if that's disabled or the driver doesn't re-enable the controller, the node stays off. I'd want the firmware to detect bus-off, log the error counters and a timestamp, and either rely on automatic recovery or perform a controlled re-init with a backoff so a persistently faulty node doesn't thrash the bus. Finally, I'd add diagnostics: error-counter telemetry and a bus-off event log so the failure is observable in the field rather than only reproducible on a bench.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between automatic bus-off recovery and a controlled re-init with backoff for a medical device? What does a node going bus-off first tell you about whether it's the aggressor or the victim?

## Q4: How would you approach designing fail-safe biasing for an RS-485 bus, and why does it matter more in a medical device than in a general industrial network?

**Answer:** Fail-safe biasing guarantees that when no driver is active — all transceivers in receive, or the bus floating during power-up, a node reset, or a cable disconnect — the differential voltage stays above the receiver's threshold so every receiver reads a defined idle (mark) state instead of oscillating or randomly toggling. Without it, a floating bus can produce spurious start bits and garbage frames. The standard approach is a pull-up on one line and a pull-down on the other, sized so the differential bias exceeds the receiver's fail-safe threshold (often a few hundred millivolts) while the bias network still presents an acceptable load and doesn't violate the driver's differential output when active. The resistor values are a trade-off: larger values load the bus less but provide weaker bias and are more susceptible to noise; smaller values bias harder but load the active driver and waste power. You also have to account for the termination resistors in parallel with the bias network, and for how many nodes and how much cable capacitance the bias has to charge.

Why it matters more in a medical device: an undefined bus state isn't just a data-integrity nuisance — it can be interpreted as a spurious command or a false alarm, and in a patient-connected system that's a safety concern, not a robustness nicety. Regulatory thinking pushes you toward a defined safe state on any single fault, so a floating or disconnected bus should read as "no valid data / idle," never as random traffic. I'd also consider what happens if one node's transceiver fails and holds the bus dominant — fail-safe biasing doesn't fix that, so it pairs with the fault-tolerant electrical design (series protection, isolated transceivers, watchdog-driven driver disable). I'd verify the bias by measuring the differential idle voltage across the full bus with all drivers off, at temperature extremes, and with the cable at its maximum length.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you calculate the bias resistor values given the termination and the number of nodes? What's the interaction between fail-safe biasing and the receiver's built-in failsafe feature on modern transceivers?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes using a single UART with a software-based protocol multiplexer to talk to four different sensors, each with a different baud rate and protocol. The hardware engineer argues this is unreliable and wants four separate UART peripherals. How would you guide the team to a decision?

**Answer:** I'd reframe it from "which engineer is right" to "what does the requirement actually demand," and make the trade-offs explicit so the decision is defensible rather than a preference contest. First I'd pin down the real constraints: aggregate throughput and latency per sensor, whether any sensor needs deterministic response, how often baud rates must change, whether the sensors can tolerate gaps, and what the pin and peripheral budget actually is. A software multiplexer on one UART means reconfiguring the baud rate and protocol state machine between sensors, which introduces turnaround latency, risks losing bytes during reconfiguration, and makes timing analysis much harder — that's the hardware engineer's legitimate concern. But four UARTs cost pins, board space, and possibly a larger MCU, and if the traffic is low-rate and non-deterministic, the multiplexer may be entirely adequate.

I'd ask the junior engineer to show the timing budget: worst-case time to service all four sensors, the reconfiguration overhead, and what happens if a sensor starts transmitting while the UART is configured for another. I'd ask the hardware engineer to quantify the cost of four UARTs — pins, cost, layout. Then I'd look for a middle path: many MCUs have more UARTs than the team assumes, or a UART plus a couple of SPI/I2C peripherals could cover the sensors, or an external UART expander could bridge the gap. If the multiplexer survives the timing analysis with margin, it's a legitimate choice; if it doesn't, the four-UART approach is justified by the numbers, not by seniority. The key is that the decision gets documented with the reasoning, because in a medical device that reasoning becomes part of the design history.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the timing analysis shows the multiplexer is marginal — just barely adequate? How would you structure the review so the junior engineer feels their proposal was evaluated fairly rather than overruled?
