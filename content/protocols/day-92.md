## Q1: How would you approach debugging a CAN-FD network where a node occasionally goes bus-off and stays off until the controller is reset, even though the bus itself appears healthy afterward?

**Answer:** Bus-off is the CAN controller's terminal error state — the transmit error counter (TEC) has exceeded 255, so the controller has removed itself from the bus by design. The fact that it stays off until reset tells me the firmware has no automatic recovery path, which is the first thing to fix: a recovery policy that waits for the bus-idle condition (128 occurrences of 11 recessive bits) and then re-initializes the controller, with a bounded retry count and an escalation to a safe state if recovery keeps failing. But recovery is the symptom fix; the real question is why TEC climbed in the first place. I'd instrument the node to log TEC/REC over time along with the error counters and the last few error frames, because the pattern distinguishes the causes. If TEC climbs steadily with occasional ACK errors, I'd suspect a wiring/termination problem or a missing/late ACK from other nodes — check that the node isn't the only transmitter during a period when others are silent, and verify the sample point and bit timing against the actual bus length and transceiver loop delay. If it's a burst of bit errors or stuff errors, I'd look at the data-phase bit rate versus bus length and stub lengths — CAN-FD's faster data phase shrinks the allowable bus length dramatically, and a network that was fine at classic CAN rates can fail only in the data phase. I'd also check whether the node's own transceiver is marginal (common-mode range, supply droop during transmit) and whether a specific message ID or payload length correlates with the failures. Finally, I'd confirm the error counters aren't being reset or masked by the driver in a way that hides the true error type, and compare against a bus analyzer capture taken at the same time so I can see the error frames from the bus's point of view rather than the node's.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the automatic recovery so it doesn't mask a persistent fault and keep flapping the node on and off the bus? What's the difference between error-passive and bus-off in terms of what the node can still do on the network?

## Q2: How would you approach calculating pull-up resistor values for an I2C bus that must support both standard-mode (100 kHz) and fast-mode (400 kHz) operation with multiple devices on the bus?

**Answer:** The pull-up is bounded from both sides, and the tighter constraint usually wins. The upper bound comes from the rise-time requirement: the bus must reach the high threshold within the mode's specified rise time (roughly 1000 ns for standard mode, 300 ns for fast mode), and since the bus is an RC network dominated by total bus capacitance, Rp ≤ tr / (0.8473 × Cb) using the standard approximation. Because fast mode has the tighter rise time, I'd size for 400 kHz and then verify the 100 kHz case still meets its (looser) timing. The lower bound comes from the sink-current limit of the weakest device: at VOL(max), each device must be able to pull the line low through Rp without exceeding its IOL rating, so Rp ≥ (VDD − VOL) / IOL. I'd take the worst-case IOL across all devices on the bus. Then I check the resulting value against the standard's recommended range and against power: a small Rp burns static current whenever the bus is low, which matters in a battery-powered device, so I'd lean toward the largest resistor that still meets rise time rather than the smallest. I'd also account for the fact that bus capacitance isn't just the sum of pin capacitances — trace capacitance, connector capacitance, and any cable all add up, and I'd measure or estimate Cb conservatively rather than trusting datasheet pin values alone. If the two bounds don't overlap — which happens with long traces or many devices — that's the signal to reconsider the topology (segment the bus with a buffer/repeater, or move to a different interface) rather than force a resistor value.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the chosen value on the bench, and what would you look for on a scope? What changes if the bus has to run at fast-mode-plus (1 MHz)?

## Q3: You're debugging a UART link where the receiver reports framing errors only when a high-current load (e.g., a motor or heater) switches on the same board. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The correlation with load switching points at a disturbance coupled through power or ground rather than a baud-rate mismatch, since the link presumably works when the load is quiet. I'd start by confirming the mechanism with a scope: probe the UART lines at the receiver pin, the receiver's supply rail, and the ground reference between the two ends, triggered on the load-switch event. Framing errors mean the stop bit wasn't where the receiver expected it, which can come from a genuinely corrupted bit, a glitch on the line, or a ground-reference shift that momentarily moves the receiver's thresholds. If I see supply droop or ground bounce coincident with the switch, the fix is in the power/ground domain — bulk and local decoupling at the receiver, separating the load's return path from the logic ground, checking that the load's inrush isn't pulling the rail below the receiver's minimum. If the UART lines themselves show coupling, I'd look at routing (are they running near the motor drive or its return?), add series termination or a small RC filter at the receiver, and consider whether the lines should be differential rather than single-ended if the run is long or noisy. I'd also check the receiver's oversampling and whether its baud clock is derived from a source that's disturbed by the switching event. Throughout, I'd want a repeatable trigger — a GPIO toggled at the switch event — so I can capture the failure deterministically instead of waiting for it.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the scope shows the UART line itself is clean but framing errors still occur, where would you look next? How would you decide whether to harden the existing single-ended link versus move to RS-485?

## Q4: How would you approach choosing between a star topology and a daisy-chain topology for a multi-drop sensor network in a medical device where the sensors are physically distributed?

**Answer:** I'd frame it around the electrical and operational consequences rather than a default preference. A star (home-run) topology gives each node its own point-to-point segment back to a hub, which keeps stub lengths short, limits the capacitance and reflections each node sees, and makes fault isolation easy — a bad node or cable affects only its own branch. The cost is cabling volume, connector count, and a hub with many ports, which matters in a device where cable routing and weight are constrained. A daisy-chain keeps cabling minimal and is natural for sensors strung along a patient-support structure, but every node sits on one shared medium, so total bus length, stub lengths, and termination become system-level constraints, and a single open or short can take down everything downstream. For a differential bus like RS-485, I'd check the daisy-chain against the stub-length and total-length limits at the intended bit rate, and confirm termination can be placed correctly at the two physical ends. I'd also weigh determinism: a shared bus means arbitration or polling across all nodes, whereas a star hub can give each branch independent timing. Then the operational side — serviceability (can a field engineer replace one sensor without disturbing others?), fault containment, and whether the physical layout actually allows clean daisy-chaining without long spurs. My usual conclusion is that star wins when fault isolation and per-node timing matter and cabling is manageable, daisy-chain wins when cabling is the dominant constraint and the bus can be engineered within its limits — and I'd document the trade-off explicitly rather than let it default.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle termination in a daisy-chain if the physical ends aren't accessible? What would make you consider a hybrid (segmented star with buffered branches)?

## Q5: How would you approach handling a situation where a junior engineer on your team has implemented a communication protocol incorrectly, and the error is only discovered during regulatory compliance testing, causing a significant schedule delay?

**Answer:** First I'd separate the two problems: containing the immediate schedule impact, and understanding how the defect escaped to compliance testing. On containment, I'd get the facts quickly — what exactly is non-conforming, is it a protocol-behavior bug or a documentation/traceability gap, and what's the minimum correct fix versus the minimum to pass this test phase — then bring the affected stakeholders (test lab, project lead, quality) into one conversation about options and trade-offs rather than deciding unilaterally. I'd resist the temptation to patch to the test and move on; in a regulated context the fix has to be correct and traceable, because a workaround that passes now but isn't properly justified creates a bigger problem later. On the human side, I'd talk to the engineer privately and without blame — the goal is to understand their reasoning and whether they had the information and review they needed, because "how did this get through" is usually a process question, not an individual failing. Then I'd look at the escape path: was the protocol specified clearly enough, was there a review of the implementation against the spec, was there any pre-compliance verification of that behavior? The durable fix is usually earlier — a clearer interface spec, a review checkpoint, or a test that exercises the protocol against its requirements before formal testing. I'd also make sure the lesson gets shared with the team so it isn't repeated, and follow up to confirm the corrective action actually took hold.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to fix forward in the current test phase versus pause and re-plan? What would you change in the review process so a protocol defect is caught before compliance testing next time?
