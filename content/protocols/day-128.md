# protocols — Day 128

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a UART link where the receiver reports framing errors only when a high-current load (e.g., a motor or heater) switches on the same board?

**Answer:** Framing errors that correlate with a load switching point to a physical-layer disturbance rather than a protocol bug, so I'd start by separating the two. First, I'd confirm the correlation is real and repeatable: capture the error rate with the load idle, then with the load switching at a known rate, and see whether errors cluster at the switching edges. If they do, the likely mechanisms are ground bounce and supply droop — a high-current load pulls a transient through the shared ground return and the supply rail, which momentarily shifts the receiver's reference and corrupts the stop-bit sampling. I'd probe the UART lines and the receiver's ground reference with a scope, triggered on the load's enable signal, and look for a common-mode transient or a supply dip at the moment of switching. On the firmware side, I'd check whether the errors are true framing errors (stop bit not seen) versus noise-induced bit flips, since that tells me whether the receiver is losing sync or just sampling a corrupted bit. Mitigations I'd evaluate: improving the ground return between the load and the MCU (star ground, separate return path), adding local bulk and high-frequency decoupling at the load and at the transceiver, slowing the UART edge rate or adding series termination if the line is long, and — if the disturbance is unavoidable — reducing baud rate or adding a checksum/retry layer so a corrupted frame is detected and re-sent rather than silently accepted. The key discipline is to fix the electrical cause first and treat protocol-level retries as a backstop, not the primary fix, especially in a medical device where silent corruption is a safety concern.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a ground-bounce problem from a supply-droop problem with a scope? If you can't change the board layout, what firmware-level mitigations would you consider, and what are their limits?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting between RS-422 and RS-485 for a medical device that needs to connect a central controller to several distributed sensor modules?

**Answer:** The decision hinges on topology and duplex requirements rather than raw electrical performance, since both are differential and share similar noise immunity and cable-length characteristics. RS-422 is a point-to-point or one-driver-to-many-receivers arrangement — one transmitter, up to a handful of receivers, full-duplex if you use two pairs. RS-485 is a true multi-point bus: multiple drivers share one pair, half-duplex, with the transceivers tri-stated when not transmitting and arbitration handled at the protocol layer. So the first question is: do the sensor modules ever need to talk back to the controller, and do they need to talk to each other? If the controller is the only talker and the modules only listen, RS-422 is simpler and avoids the turnaround and bus-contention problems entirely. If the modules must respond — which is typical for a sensor network — RS-485 is the natural fit, and I'd then design for the half-duplex realities: driver-enable timing, fail-safe biasing so the bus doesn't float when idle, termination at both ends, and a protocol that handles the turnaround budget. I'd also weigh practical concerns: RS-485 transceivers are more widely available and cheaper in multi-point configurations, and the ecosystem of reference designs is larger. For a medical device I'd add that the choice must be justified in the design history file — the rationale for topology, termination, and fault behavior should be documented, because a reviewer will ask why a shared bus was chosen over point-to-point for a safety-relevant link.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the modules only need to send occasional status and the controller polls them, does that change your answer? How would you handle a module that fails and holds the bus in a dominant state on an RS-485 network?

## Q3: How would you approach calculating termination resistor values for an RS-485 network, and how do stub length and node count change your answer?

**Answer:** The starting point is the characteristic impedance of the cable — typically around 100 to 120 ohms for twisted pair — and the standard practice is to terminate both physical ends of the bus with a resistor equal to that impedance, so the far end absorbs the wave and the near end matches the driver. That gives you a single 120-ohm resistor at each end, and the parallel combination presents about 60 ohms to the driver, which is what the transceiver's drive capability is specified against. The termination must be at the two extreme ends of the cable, not at every node — mid-bus termination just loads the line and wastes drive current. Stub length is the branch from the main bus to each node's transceiver; the rule of thumb is to keep stubs short relative to the signal's rise time, because a long stub acts as an unmatched transmission line and causes reflections that show up as ringing and, at high baud rates, as bit errors. As node count rises, the cumulative capacitance of transceivers and stubs loads the bus and slows edges, which effectively reduces the maximum usable length-times-bit-rate product; that's why a bus that works at 9600 baud over 150 meters with 12 nodes may fail at 115200 with the same topology. So my answer changes with node count and stub length in the direction of: shorter stubs, fewer/lower-capacitance transceivers, and possibly a lower baud rate or a repeater/segment split. I'd also account for fail-safe biasing resistors, which sit alongside the termination and must be sized so the idle differential stays above the receiver's threshold without swamping the termination.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure whether reflections are actually the problem versus capacitance loading? What's the trade-off between adding a repeater and reducing the baud rate on a long bus?

## Q4: How would you approach designing a protocol abstraction layer so that application code can talk to sensors over I2C, SPI, UART, or CAN-FD without knowing which transport is underneath?

**Answer:** The goal is to define a transport-agnostic interface at the boundary the application sees, and push all transport-specific detail below it. I'd start by characterizing what the application actually needs from a sensor: a way to read a register or a block of data, a way to write configuration, a way to receive asynchronous events or alarms, and a notion of transaction completion and error. That becomes a small set of operations — something like `read`, `write`, `subscribe`, and an error/status model — expressed in terms of the sensor's logical address space, not in terms of bus addresses or chip selects. Below that interface, each transport implements the same operations: the I2C backend maps a logical read to a register-address write followed by a read with the right addressing; the SPI backend handles chip-select assertion, mode and clock setup, and the fact that SPI has no acknowledgement so errors must be inferred; the UART backend frames the request and handles the request/response timing; the CAN-FD backend maps to message IDs and handles the larger payload and the segmentation if needed. The application layer never sees any of that. Two things make this work in practice: a consistent error and timeout model across all backends, so the application handles failure uniformly, and a way to express transport capabilities — maximum payload size, whether the transport is reliable, whether it supports asynchronous notification — so the application can adapt without hard-coding assumptions. I'd also keep the abstraction thin enough that it doesn't hide timing behavior the application needs to reason about, because in a medical device the latency characteristics of the transport matter and shouldn't be papered over.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a sensor that exists only on one transport and has a feature the abstraction doesn't expose? Where would you put retry logic — in the abstraction layer or in each backend?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes using a single UART with a software-based protocol multiplexer to communicate with four different sensors, each with different baud rates and protocols. The hardware engineer argues this is unreliable and wants four separate UART peripherals. How would you guide the team to evaluate this approach?

**Answer:** I'd frame this as a requirements-and-risk trade rather than a binary argument, and get both engineers to state their concerns in testable terms. First, I'd ask the junior engineer to walk through the timing: with four sensors at different baud rates sharing one UART, the multiplexer has to reconfigure the baud rate between transactions, and that reconfiguration plus the turnaround time has to fit inside each sensor's required polling interval. If any sensor has a tight latency requirement, the shared UART may not meet it, and that's a quantitative question, not an opinion. Second, I'd ask about the failure modes: what happens if a sensor sends an unexpected byte or a partial frame — does the multiplexer get stuck, and how does it recover? A software multiplexer that loses framing on one sensor can block all four, which is a single point of failure the four-UART design doesn't have. Third, I'd ask the hardware engineer to quantify the cost of four UARTs — pin count, board space, and whether the chosen MCU even has four available — because "unreliable" needs to be weighed against real constraints. Then I'd steer the team toward a decision framework: if the sensors' timing is loose and the protocols are simple, the shared UART with a robust framing/recovery layer may be acceptable and saves pins; if any sensor is latency-critical or the protocols are stateful, the dedicated peripherals are the safer choice. I'd also suggest a middle path — a shared UART for the low-rate sensors and a dedicated peripheral for the critical one — and ask the team to prototype the multiplexer's recovery behavior before committing. The point of the review is to make the trade explicit and documented, not to let seniority decide it.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific test would you ask for to prove the multiplexer recovers from a framing error? If the MCU has only two UARTs, how would that constrain the decision?
