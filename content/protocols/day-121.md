# protocols — Day 121

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a CAN-FD network where the bus works fine at the nominal (arbitration) bit rate but nodes intermittently report CRC or form errors only during the data phase at the higher bit rate?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a signal-integrity-versus-timing problem specific to the data phase, because the arbitration phase and data phase use different bit rates and the failure only appears when the faster rate is active. The first step is to confirm the pattern: does the error rate scale with bus length, with the number of nodes, or with specific frames that carry long payloads? That tells me whether I'm looking at a physical-layer margin issue or a configuration mismatch.

On the physical side, the data phase at a higher bit rate shrinks the bit time, so the same cable and stub network that had plenty of margin at the nominal rate can now be marginal. I'd check termination at both ends, measure the actual differential waveform with a scope at the worst-case node (looking for ringing, overshoot, and slow edges), and verify that the sample point in the data phase is set correctly — the transceiver's propagation delay symmetry and the controller's sample-point configuration both matter more at high data rates. I'd also check whether the achievable data-phase bit rate is compatible with the total bus length and topology; there's a real trade-off between bit rate and physical extent, and a network that's fine at one rate can be out of spec at another.

On the configuration side, I'd verify that every node agrees on the same nominal rate, data rate, and sample points, and that the transceivers are actually rated for the data-phase rate. A common trap is mixing transceivers with different loop-delay characteristics, or leaving one node at a default sample point that's fine at the nominal rate but too late at the data rate. I'd also look at whether long frames are the ones failing — if so, that points toward accumulated timing error over the payload rather than a single-edge problem.

The systematic approach: reproduce with a known traffic pattern, capture with a CAN analyzer that can decode both phases, correlate errors with specific frame types and node positions, then bisect by shortening the bus, reducing node count, or lowering the data rate to confirm the margin hypothesis before changing hardware.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between lowering the data-phase bit rate and reworking the physical layer? What role does the transceiver's loop delay play in setting the maximum data-phase rate for a given bus length?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a UART link between two boards on the same PCB where one side runs from an internal RC oscillator and the other from a crystal, and you need reliable communication at a moderately high baud rate?

**Answer:** The core issue is baud-rate tolerance: UART is asynchronous, so both ends must agree on the bit time closely enough that sampling stays within the valid window across a full frame. An internal RC oscillator typically has several percent of frequency error over temperature and voltage, while a crystal is far tighter. If the RC-based side is off by, say, a few percent and the crystal side is near-nominal, the combined error can eat most of the sampling margin, and the last data bits of a frame are where it shows up first.

My approach: first quantify the budget. Sum the worst-case oscillator error on both ends, add the receiver's own sampling tolerance, and compare against the roughly half-bit of margin a UART frame allows. If the budget is tight, the options are to lower the baud rate so the absolute timing error per bit shrinks, to trim/calibrate the RC oscillator against a known reference at production or runtime, or to move to a synchronous scheme or a protocol with a clock line. I'd also make sure the RC-based side uses a divider that produces the closest achievable baud rate, and check the actual measured frequency rather than trusting the datasheet typical.

Beyond the oscillator, I'd verify the physical layer is clean — on a short on-board link, signal integrity is rarely the problem, but a shared ground reference and proper idle levels still matter. And I'd add framing/parity or a CRC at the application layer so that when margin does degrade, the error is detected rather than silently corrupting data. For a medical device, detection matters as much as prevention.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you calibrate an RC oscillator at runtime without an external reference? What's the trade-off between lowering baud rate and adding a synchronous clock line?

## Q3: How would you approach selecting between a multi-drop shared bus and a point-to-point star topology for a medical device where several sensor modules must report to a central controller, and some modules are physically far from the controller?

**Answer:** I'd frame this as a trade-off across four axes: determinism, wiring complexity, fault containment, and physical reach. A shared multi-drop bus (RS-485 or CAN-FD, for example) uses less cabling and scales to many nodes, but every node shares one medium, so a single faulty transceiver can affect everyone, arbitration or polling adds latency, and the achievable bit rate falls as bus length and node count grow. A star topology gives each module a dedicated link, which makes latency per link predictable and contains faults to one branch, but it multiplies cabling and connector count and pushes the controller toward many transceivers or ports.

For the "far from the controller" modules, physical distance is the deciding factor: differential multi-drop buses handle long runs well if terminated and stubbed correctly, whereas a star with long individual runs can become a cable-routing and cost problem. So I'd ask whether the far modules can share a bus among themselves and connect to the controller over one trunk, which is often the best of both — a segmented topology rather than a pure star or pure bus.

I'd also weigh determinism requirements: if some modules need bounded-latency response, a shared bus needs a priority scheme (CAN arbitration, or a polled schedule with guaranteed slots), while a star can give each link its own timing budget. And I'd consider fault tolerance and serviceability — in a medical device, being able to isolate a failed module without taking down the network is valuable, which favors segmentation.

The decision usually lands on a hybrid: a differential bus trunk for the distributed modules, with careful termination and stub-length discipline, and dedicated links only where a module genuinely needs isolation or guaranteed bandwidth.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would stub length and node count constrain the maximum bit rate on the shared-bus option? How would you contain a single failed transceiver on a shared bus so it can't take down the whole network?

## Q4: How would you approach implementing flow control on a UART link where the receiver occasionally cannot keep up with the transmitter, but the protocol has no built-in backpressure mechanism?

**Answer:** Since the protocol has no native backpressure, I'd add flow control at a layer I control, and choose between hardware and software depending on what the hardware supports. If RTS/CTS lines are available, hardware flow control is the most robust: the receiver deasserts RTS before its buffer fills, and the transmitter's UART stops mid-stream without any software involvement, so there's no window where bytes are lost while firmware reacts. The key is setting the assert/deassert thresholds with enough hysteresis and enough headroom that the transmitter can finish the bytes already in flight before the receiver overflows.

If there are no spare lines, software flow control (XON/XOFF) is the fallback, but it has real weaknesses: the flow-control characters travel in the same stream, so they're subject to the same latency as data, and they can collide with binary payloads unless the framing escapes them. I'd only use it if the payload is text or properly escaped.

A third option is to design the application protocol so the receiver never has to absorb an unbounded burst — fixed-size frames with an ACK/window scheme, or a request/response pattern where the transmitter only sends what the receiver has asked for. That's often cleaner than retrofitting flow control onto a streaming protocol.

Regardless of mechanism, I'd size the receive buffer against the worst-case latency the receiver can experience (interrupt latency, higher-priority tasks, flash writes) times the link rate, and add an overrun detection path so that if flow control does fail, the receiver flags it rather than silently dropping data — important in a medical context.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you size the RTS/CTS thresholds given a known worst-case receiver service latency? What are the failure modes of XON/XOFF when the payload is binary?

## Q5: You're leading a design review where a junior engineer proposes a custom framing protocol over UART for a medical device, and you notice the framing has no length-field validation and no sequence numbers. How would you guide the team to evaluate and improve the design?

**Answer:** I'd start by making the failure mode concrete rather than just asserting the design is wrong — walk through a specific scenario: a single bit error in the length field causes the receiver to consume the next several valid frames as payload, so corruption propagates silently and the receiver resynchronizes on the wrong boundary. That usually lands better than an abstract critique, and it frames the review around risk rather than seniority.

From there I'd structure the discussion around what the protocol needs to guarantee: frame boundary detection that can't be fooled by a corrupted length, detection of lost or duplicated frames, and detection of corrupted payload. That maps to concrete additions — a start delimiter plus a length field that's range-checked against a maximum, a checksum or CRC over the frame, and a sequence number so gaps and duplicates are detectable. I'd also raise resynchronization: how does the receiver recover after a bad frame without losing the stream, which usually means a defined idle-timeout or delimiter-based resync.

I'd keep the tone collaborative — ask the junior engineer to walk through their reasoning and the constraints they were optimizing for (maybe they were keeping overhead low), then have the team weigh those constraints against the risk. In a medical device the regulatory angle matters too: the design needs to be defensible in a hazard analysis, and "silent data corruption with no detection" is hard to defend. I'd capture the decision and rationale in the design record so it's traceable.

Finally, I'd make sure the fix is verified — a fault-injection test that corrupts the length field and confirms the receiver rejects and resyncs rather than misparsing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you choose between a checksum and a CRC for this frame? How would you structure the fault-injection test to prove the receiver resynchronizes correctly after a corrupted frame?
