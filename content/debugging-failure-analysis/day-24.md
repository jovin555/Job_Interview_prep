# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 24

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's firmware and hardware teams disagree on whether an intermittent communication error is caused by a marginal timing violation or a firmware race condition — the device occasionally fails to complete a sensor read within the required time window, and the error rate increases as the device warms up?
**Answer:** This is a classic firmware-hardware boundary problem, and the temperature dependence is a strong clue that narrows the investigation. I'd structure this in three phases. First, I'd establish a common ground truth by instrumenting the system to capture both domains simultaneously — a logic analyzer on the SPI or I2C bus, synchronized with firmware trace logs that timestamp the exact point where the read times out. This gives us a single, agreed-upon record of what actually happens during a failure event, rather than each team working from their own assumptions. Second, I'd characterize the timing margins directly. Since the error rate increases with temperature, I'd run the device in a thermal chamber and measure the actual clock frequencies, rise times, and setup/hold margins at the sensor interface across the temperature range. If the timing margins are shrinking as the device warms up, that points to a marginal hardware design; if the timing is rock-solid but the firmware's timeout handling has a race window that only manifests under certain conditions, that points to software. Third, I'd look at the failure mechanism from the firmware side — specifically, whether the timeout handling code has any re-entrancy issues or shared-state problems that could cause a race condition. The key is to avoid the "blame game" and instead focus on what the data shows. I'd also check whether the sensor's datasheet timing specifications are being met at the worst-case temperature, and whether the firmware's timeout value has adequate margin above the worst-case sensor response time. The temperature correlation is particularly useful because it suggests a systematic effect — something like a crystal oscillator drifting with temperature, or a pull-up resistor value that's marginal at higher temperatures — rather than a random software glitch.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the timing margin is actually adequate if the datasheet specifications are ambiguous? What specific instrumentation would you use to capture both the bus activity and the firmware state simultaneously?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a medical device where the failure occurs only when the device is operated at a specific altitude or in a low-pressure environment, such as during air transport of a patient?
**Answer:** Low-pressure environments introduce several distinct failure mechanisms that don't appear at sea level, so I'd approach this systematically. First, I'd consider the physics of what changes with altitude: reduced air density affects cooling efficiency (convection heat transfer drops), which can cause components to run hotter; lower pressure reduces the dielectric strength of air, which can lead to arcing or corona discharge at high-voltage nodes; and sealed enclosures can experience pressure differentials that stress seals or cause flexing of the PCB. I'd start by reproducing the failure in a controlled environment — an altitude chamber or vacuum chamber that can simulate the pressure profile — while instrumenting the device with thermocouples on critical components, current probes on power rails, and monitoring for any electrical discharge activity. I'd also check whether the device has any pressure-sensitive components, such as a barometric pressure sensor, that might be affected. One common issue is that the reduced cooling at altitude causes a component to exceed its thermal derating, even though the ambient temperature is within specification — so I'd compare junction temperatures at sea level versus altitude. Another possibility is that the lower pressure allows arcing across a PCB trace or connector pin that has marginal spacing, especially in a high-voltage section like a motor driver or power supply. I'd use a corona camera or listen for audible discharge, and inspect the PCB for any areas with tight clearances. I'd also review the design's creepage and clearance distances against the applicable standards for the operating altitude — many standards require increased spacing for higher altitudes. Finally, I'd check whether the firmware has any altitude-dependent behavior, such as a pressure sensor reading that's used in an algorithm and could be causing a fault condition at low pressure.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific derating calculations would you perform for a component at altitude? How would you distinguish between a thermal failure and an electrical discharge failure at low pressure?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's power supply exhibits audible noise (whining or buzzing) that correlates with the device's processing load, and the noise is only noticeable in a quiet clinical environment?
**Answer:** Audible noise from a power supply is almost always related to mechanical vibration of a magnetic component — typically an inductor or transformer — driven by electrical excitation at an audible frequency. The correlation with processing load is a strong clue because it suggests the noise is related to the current draw pattern or the switching behavior of the regulator. I'd start by identifying the source of the noise — using a stethoscope or an acoustic probe to localize which component is vibrating, and a spectrum analyzer on the audio signal to identify the frequency content. Then I'd correlate that with the electrical activity: I'd probe the switching node of the power supply with an oscilloscope and look at the switching frequency and duty cycle under different processing loads. A common cause is that the regulator is operating in pulse-skipping or burst mode at light loads, which produces a low-frequency envelope on the switching waveform — and that envelope frequency can fall into the audible range. Another possibility is that the load current is modulating at an audible rate due to the processor's activity pattern — for example, if the processor wakes up periodically to perform a task, the resulting current steps can excite a mechanical resonance in the inductor. I'd also check whether the inductor's core material is appropriate — some ferrite materials exhibit magnetostriction, which causes physical deformation and audible noise. If the noise is from the inductor, the fix might be to change the inductor to a different core material or construction, add a damping mechanism, or adjust the regulator's switching frequency or control loop to avoid the audible range. I'd also consider whether the noise is actually coming from a ceramic capacitor — these can exhibit piezoelectric effects and produce audible noise when subjected to AC voltage ripple. In a medical device, this is more than a cosmetic issue — it could be distracting or alarming to patients and clinicians, so it needs to be addressed even though the device is functioning electrically.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the noise is from the inductor or a ceramic capacitor? What design changes would you consider to eliminate the noise without compromising the power supply's performance?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's analog measurement is accurate when tested with a known-good reference signal, but shows a consistent offset error when connected to the actual patient sensor — and the offset varies between different sensor units?
**Answer:** This is a classic interface-mismatch problem, and the fact that the offset varies between sensor units is the key clue. When the device works with a reference signal but not with real sensors, the issue is almost certainly in the interaction between the device's analog front-end and the sensor's electrical characteristics — not in the front-end itself. I'd start by characterizing the sensor's output impedance, drive capability, and any DC bias requirements. A common cause is that the sensor has a higher output impedance than the reference source, and the front-end's input bias current or input impedance is loading the sensor — creating a voltage divider that shifts the reading. The variation between sensor units would then be explained by unit-to-unit differences in output impedance or internal resistance. I'd measure the actual sensor output impedance and compare it to the front-end's input impedance, and I'd check the front-end's input bias current specification. Another possibility is that the sensor requires a specific bias voltage or excitation current that the front-end isn't providing — for example, a bridge-type sensor needs a stable excitation voltage, and if the front-end's excitation source has a tolerance or drift, that would show up as an offset. I'd also check the cable and connector — different sensor units might have different cable lengths or connector resistances, which could contribute to the offset. I'd look at the grounding and shielding arrangement — if the sensor's ground reference isn't the same as the front-end's ground, that would create a common-mode offset. The systematic approach is to measure the sensor's electrical characteristics, compare them to the front-end's specifications, and then calculate what offset would result from the mismatch. I'd also test with a variable resistor or a simulated sensor that can replicate the impedance characteristics of the real sensors, to confirm the mechanism. Once the root cause is identified, the fix might be to add a buffer amplifier, adjust the front-end's input impedance, or calibrate the system to account for the sensor's characteristics.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the offset is caused by input bias current or by a ground reference mismatch? What measurements would you take on the sensor itself to characterize its output impedance?

## Q5: How would you handle a situation where you're leading a failure investigation, and a senior engineer on your team is convinced they know the root cause based on their experience with a similar device, but the evidence doesn't fully support their hypothesis — and they're pushing to implement a fix before the investigation is complete?
**Answer:** This is a common and delicate situation in failure analysis, and the key is to respect the senior engineer's experience while maintaining the integrity of the investigation process. I'd start by acknowledging their hypothesis and the value of their experience — they may well be right, and their pattern recognition is a useful tool. But I'd frame the discussion around evidence and risk: in a medical device context, implementing a fix based on an unconfirmed hypothesis carries significant risk, both in terms of patient safety and in terms of regulatory compliance. I'd propose a structured approach where we test their hypothesis explicitly — design an experiment or gather the specific evidence that would confirm or refute it. This turns the disagreement into a collaborative investigation rather than a conflict. I'd also review the evidence together and identify what's missing — what data would make us confident in the root cause? If their hypothesis is correct, the evidence should support it, and we should be able to find that evidence. If the evidence doesn't support it, we need to understand why — maybe the hypothesis is incomplete, or maybe there are multiple contributing factors. I'd also consider the schedule pressure: if the fix is low-risk and reversible, we might implement it as a temporary mitigation while the investigation continues, but we'd document it clearly as a hypothesis-driven change, not a confirmed root-cause fix. In a medical device context, this is important because any change needs to go through the design change process, and if the root cause isn't confirmed, the fix might not be effective or might introduce new issues. I'd also involve the quality or regulatory team to ensure we're following the proper process. The goal is to maintain the senior engineer's engagement and respect their contribution while ensuring the investigation reaches a defensible conclusion.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation if the senior engineer becomes defensive or resistant to the structured approach? What specific evidence would you require before implementing a fix in a medical device context?
