# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 107

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is treat this as a variable-isolation problem and resist the temptation to re-verify the electronics, because the electronics have already been shown to be correct under one set of conditions. The delta between the two states is mechanical and environmental: enclosure, mounting, cable routing, and the electrical environment of the installed position. I'd reproduce the failure deliberately and then peel back one variable at a time — run the unit open on the bench, then in the enclosure but not mounted, then mounted but not connected to its final cabling, then fully installed. That sequence usually tells you which transition introduces the offset.

Once I know which transition matters, I'd think about the mechanisms that produce a *consistent* offset rather than noise: a ground reference shifting (the analog front-end's local ground now sits at a different potential relative to the ADC reference because of where the enclosure or mounting hardware bonds), a changed return-path impedance, a parasitic load or leakage path introduced by the enclosure or a mounting screw contacting a trace or shield, or a mechanical stress effect on a sensor or its solder joints. A consistent offset points more toward a DC-level mechanism — reference, bias, leakage, or stress — than toward coupling, which tends to show up as noise or drift.

I'd measure rather than speculate: probe the analog front-end's reference and ground at the point of use with the unit in each configuration, and compare the actual differential voltage the ADC sees against what the bench harness delivered. If the offset tracks a ground potential difference, that's a layout/grounding finding. If it tracks mechanical assembly, I'd look at board flex, connector seating, and whether the enclosure is pinching or preloading anything. The corrective action then follows the mechanism — a grounding/bonding change, a layout revision, a mechanical tolerance fix, or a strain-relief change — and I'd verify it by repeating the full configuration matrix, not just the failing one.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a ground-reference shift from a genuine sensor offset in that setup? What would you do if the offset only appeared in the customer's installed position and you couldn't bring that fixture into the lab?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle?

**Answer:** I'd start by confirming the measurement itself is trustworthy, because ripple numbers are easy to get wrong. I'd probe at the point of load — right at the decoupling capacitors and the IC's supply pins — with a short ground lead or a proper tip-and-barrel probe, not a long ground clip that picks up loop inductance. I'd also check whether what I'm calling ripple is actually switching noise, a load-transient response, or ground bounce on my probe reference. Getting the measurement right first prevents chasing a phantom.

Assuming the ripple is real and load-dependent, the mechanism is almost always one of three things: the regulator's loop can't respond fast enough to the transient (insufficient output capacitance, high ESR, or a control loop with inadequate phase margin for that load step), the current path between the regulator and the load has too much impedance (trace inductance, via count, connector resistance) so the transient develops a voltage across it, or the high-current subsystem is injecting noise back onto the rail through a shared return or a common impedance. I'd separate these by measuring at the regulator output versus at the load — if the regulator output is clean and the load sees ripple, it's a distribution/impedance problem; if the regulator output itself is moving, it's a loop or bulk-capacitance problem.

From there it's a divide-and-conquer on the fix: add bulk or high-frequency decoupling locally at the load to see if the transient response improves, check the return path for the high-current subsystem to make sure it isn't sharing impedance with the sensitive rail, and look at the load-step waveform to understand the di/dt and whether the regulator is hitting a current limit or slew-rate limit. I'd also verify the subsystem's own switching behavior — sometimes the "ripple" is the subsystem's PWM edge coupling through a shared ground, in which case the fix is layout and return-path separation rather than more capacitance.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure loop stability or transient response without a dedicated network analyzer? If adding capacitance at the load fixes it, how do you decide whether that's an acceptable design change or a band-aid over a layout problem?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline?

**Answer:** This is one of the harder classes because every guardrail the system has is passing — the value is in range, the self-tests are green, and there's no fault to trigger on. The first move is to make the failure observable in real time rather than only in offline review. I'd add instrumentation that captures the raw path: the ADC output, the timestamp, the conversion sequence, and the state of any multiplexer or channel-select logic at the moment of the reading, so I can compare what the firmware *received* against what it *reported*. If the raw value is correct and the reported value is wrong, the bug is in the processing, scaling, or storage path. If the raw value is already wrong, it's upstream in acquisition.

A plausible-but-wrong value often comes from a stale or misattributed sample: a conversion that completed late and got paired with the wrong timestamp, a channel that wasn't fully settled when it was read, a buffer that got overwritten before it was consumed, or a scaling constant applied to the wrong channel. I'd look hard at the sequencing and buffering logic and at any concurrency between the acquisition and the consumer — a race where the consumer reads a half-updated value is a classic source of "plausible but wrong." I'd also check whether the error correlates with specific events: a particular operating mode, a high-activity period, a temperature, or a time since boot.

To pin it down I'd build a targeted stress test that runs the acquisition path hard and logs both raw and processed values continuously, then look for the first divergence. Once I can reproduce it deterministically, I can bisect the code path or the timing. The corrective action is usually a sequencing fix, a double-buffer or atomic-swap pattern, a settle-time correction, or a validation check that catches the misattribution before it propagates — and I'd add a regression test that exercises the exact timing condition that produced it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the logging so it doesn't itself perturb the timing you're trying to catch? If the error only shows up in the field and never on the bench, what would you change about your test setup?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** When the fault lands on a memcpy but the addresses and bounds check out, I stop looking at the copy itself and start looking at what the copy depends on. A memory copy is a very sensitive canary: it touches a lot of memory quickly, so it tends to be where latent corruption, alignment problems, or a corrupted stack finally manifests. The fault location is the symptom, not necessarily the cause.

I'd work through the likely mechanisms systematically. First, alignment and access width: some architectures fault on unaligned access, and a copy routine that assumes word alignment can fault if a pointer is only byte-aligned — the addresses can be "valid" and still be misaligned. Second, stack integrity: if the fault is in a copy that uses the stack, a prior overflow or a corrupted link register could send execution somewhere unexpected, and the reported trace may be misleading. Third, memory that *looks* valid but isn't: a pointer that was freed, a buffer that overlaps another, or a region that's valid at the moment of the check but has been reallocated or clobbered by the time the copy runs — a classic use-after-free or double-use pattern. Fourth, concurrency: if another context (an ISR, a DMA engine, or another task) is writing to the same region, the copy can fault on a torn or transiently invalid state even though each individual access looks fine.

To narrow it, I'd enable memory protection or a canary/guard pattern around the buffers, turn on any hardware fault-status registers to see the precise fault type (bus fault vs. alignment vs. memory-management), and add a lightweight check that logs the pointer values and the calling context at entry to the copy. If the fault is intermittent and stack-related, I'd look at stack high-water marks and interrupt nesting depth. The fix is whatever the mechanism turns out to be — an alignment-safe copy, a bounds/ownership fix, a concurrency guard, or a stack-size correction — and I'd verify by running the same workload under the fault-injection or stress conditions that reproduced it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you use the fault-status registers to distinguish a bus fault from an alignment fault? If the corruption is coming from a DMA engine writing outside its intended buffer, how would you detect that?

## Q5: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The deadlock usually isn't a technical disagreement so much as each team having tested its own domain in isolation and found it clean — which is exactly what you'd expect if the failure lives at the boundary. My job as lead is to reframe the question from "whose fault is it" to "what single experiment would produce evidence that discriminates between the hypotheses," and then get both teams to agree on that experiment before anyone runs it.

I'd start by writing down both hypotheses explicitly and, for each, what observable evidence would confirm or refute it. That forces specificity: "hardware timing is marginal" becomes "the sensor's clock or data-line timing is out of spec under condition X," and "firmware has a race" becomes "the read completes but the consumer sees stale data under condition Y." Then I'd design a test that captures the boundary directly — instrument the bus or the interface so we can see, at the moment of failure, what the hardware actually presented and what the firmware actually did with it. A logic analyzer or bus capture that both teams trust, with a shared trigger, turns a he-said-she-said into a shared dataset.

I'd also set ground rules that keep it productive: no conclusions until the shared capture exists, both teams contribute to the test plan, and we timebox the experiment so it doesn't drag. If the capture is ambiguous, I'd escalate to a controlled fault-injection or marginal-condition test — vary the supply, temperature, or timing deliberately and see which side's margin runs out first. The point is to make the investigation evidence-driven and jointly owned rather than a negotiation between two positions, and to keep the tone collaborative so neither team feels they're being set up to take the blame.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the shared capture shows the failure is genuinely at the boundary and both sides contributed? How do you keep a cross-functional investigation from stalling when the two teams have very different levels of available test equipment?
