# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 109

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a device that fails only when it is powered from its internal battery, but passes every test when powered from a bench supply at the same nominal voltage?

**Answer:** The first principle is that "same nominal voltage" is not the same electrical source, so I'd stop treating the bench supply as a valid control and start characterizing the differences between the two sources. A bench supply is typically a low-impedance, well-regulated, earth-referenced source with a long cable; a battery is a finite-impedance source whose voltage sags under transient load, whose return path is floating relative to earth, and which may have protection circuitry (PTC, protection IC, fuel gauge) in series. Any of those differences can turn a marginal design into a failing one.

My approach would be to instrument both scenarios identically and compare: battery voltage at the terminals and at the board input during the failure, inrush and load-transient current profiles, and the AC content on the rails. I'd specifically look for transient dips that a bench supply's bulk capacitance would mask, and for ground-reference differences — with a battery, the board's "ground" floats, so any earth-referenced probe or cable can create an unintended return path that changes behavior. I'd also verify the battery path's series elements: protection FETs, sense resistors, and connectors all add impedance that the bench lead does not.

Then I'd try to reproduce the failure on the bench by emulating the battery realistically — adding series resistance and a realistic cable, and removing the earth reference where safe to do so — rather than assuming the bench setup is representative. If the failure follows the source impedance rather than the source voltage, that points at a transient or return-path issue; if it follows the presence of the protection circuitry, that points at a specific component or its configuration. Throughout, I'd change one variable at a time so I can attribute the behavior to a cause rather than to coincidence.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you safely emulate a battery's source impedance and floating reference on the bench without compromising the test setup? If the failure turns out to be a transient dip, how would you decide between adding bulk capacitance, changing the battery path impedance, or fixing the load's transient behavior?

## Q2: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's power rail collapses only during a specific operating mode, but the regulator and its passives all measure within specification when the board is powered in isolation?

**Answer:** The key observation is that "the regulator measures fine in isolation" only tells you the regulator is not defective — it says nothing about whether the regulator is being asked to do something it can't do in that operating mode. So I'd reframe the question from "is the regulator good?" to "what is different about the load, the input, or the sequencing in that mode?"

I'd start by capturing the rail's behavior in the failing mode with a scope, triggering on the collapse, and simultaneously capturing the regulator's input voltage, the enable pin, and the load current — ideally with a current probe or a low-value sense resistor, since a collapse under a transient load is often a current-limit or thermal event rather than a regulation failure. I'd check whether the collapse is preceded by a current spike, a drop on the input rail, or a glitch on enable or feedback. Each of those implicates a different mechanism: input droop points upstream, a current spike points at the load or at inrush into a subsystem, an enable glitch points at sequencing or a supervisory signal.

Then I'd look at what the operating mode actually changes: which subsystems power up, in what order, and whether any of them present a transient load the regulator wasn't characterized for. I'd also verify the passives in circuit rather than on the bench — a capacitor that measures correctly out of circuit can still be ineffective if its effective capacitance is reduced by DC bias, temperature, or a poor solder joint, and a feedback divider can be fine at DC but wrong dynamically. If the collapse is repeatable, I'd try to reproduce it on the bench by emulating the mode's load profile with an electronic load, which lets me sweep the transient and find the margin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a regulator entering current limit from a genuine loop-stability problem during the transient? What would you check first if the collapse only happens on some units and not others?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** A reversible, temperature-correlated offset is a strong clue that the mechanism is thermal rather than a permanent degradation, so I'd treat this as a thermal characterization problem before treating it as a component-failure problem. The first step is to make the thermal variable measurable: instrument the unit with thermocouples or use a thermal camera to map where the heat actually goes, and correlate the offset curve against the temperature of specific components — the sensor, the instrumentation amplifier, the reference, and the ADC — rather than against elapsed time. Time is a proxy; temperature is the variable.

Then I'd separate the possible contributors. An offset that grows with temperature can come from the sensor bridge itself (TC offset of the bridge), from the amplifier's input offset voltage drift, from the voltage reference's temperature coefficient, or from mechanical stress — a package or PCB that expands differentially and strains the sensor or a solder joint. I'd test these by heating the board uniformly in a chamber versus heating individual components with a localized heat source, and by checking whether the offset tracks the reference or the amplifier when each is held at a stable temperature. If the offset disappears when the sensor is thermally isolated from the rest of the board, that points at thermal gradients or mechanical coupling rather than at the electronics.

I'd also verify the obvious: that the drift is within the combined datasheet tempco budget of the signal chain, or whether it exceeds it. If it exceeds the budget, something is behaving worse than specified — a wrong-grade part, a missing compensation network, or a layout issue like a thermocouple junction at a connector. The corrective action depends on which of these it is, so I wouldn't propose a fix until the dominant contributor is identified and quantified.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a sensor's intrinsic offset drift from drift introduced by the amplifier or reference? If the drift is within the datasheet budget but still trips the system's fault threshold, how would you approach that — as a design margin issue or a firmware threshold issue?

## Q4: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The deadlock usually comes from each team having tested its own domain in isolation and found it clean, which is exactly the condition under which an interaction bug hides. So I'd reframe the goal from "who owns it" to "what single experiment would discriminate between the two hypotheses," and I'd make that experiment the team's shared objective rather than a debate.

Concretely, I'd start by writing down both hypotheses as falsifiable statements and identifying what evidence would confirm or refute each. Then I'd design instrumentation that captures both domains on the same time base — a logic analyzer or scope with protocol decode on the bus, plus firmware trace timestamps, so that a bus event and a firmware event can be placed on one timeline. The most common resolution is that the timeline shows the failure is a marginal timing violation that neither side sees alone: the hardware is within spec but slow at temperature, and the firmware's timeout is within spec but tight, and the combination fails. That's not a hardware bug or a firmware bug — it's a margin problem at the interface.

I'd also set up a joint reproduction: same unit, same test, both engineers present, with each side's instruments connected simultaneously. Shared observation tends to dissolve ownership arguments faster than shared meetings. And I'd explicitly separate "root cause" from "corrective action" — sometimes the right fix is to add margin on both sides rather than to prove one side guilty, and I'd say that out loud so neither team feels the investigation is a blame exercise.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the discriminating experiment is inconclusive and both hypotheses remain plausible? What would you do to prevent this kind of interface-margin dispute from recurring on future projects?

## Q5: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** When the fault location is consistent but the addresses are valid, I'd be suspicious of the premise that the memcpy is the cause rather than the victim. A fault taken inside a memory copy with valid pointers often means something corrupted state before the copy ran — a stack overflow that overwrote the return address or a local variable, a buffer overrun elsewhere that damaged a length or pointer, or a race where another context modified the source or destination between the bounds check and the copy. The copy is where the damage becomes visible, not necessarily where it originated.

So I'd approach it as a corruption investigation. First, I'd check the fault status registers to see the precise fault type — a bus fault versus a usage fault versus an imprecise fault tells you very different things, and an imprecise bus fault in particular can point to a write that happened earlier. Then I'd look at stack depth and high-water marks, enable stack canaries or MPU-based stack guards if the platform supports them, and check whether the fault correlates with particular call paths, interrupt load, or timing. If the addresses are valid at the fault but the copy still fails, I'd also verify alignment and whether the region is actually accessible in the current MPU configuration — a valid address can still be an access violation if the MPU region permissions changed.

I'd also look for concurrency: is the buffer shared with an ISR or a DMA engine? A DMA transfer writing into the same region, or an ISR modifying a length field, would produce exactly this signature — valid addresses, consistent fault site, intermittent occurrence. Instrumenting with a lightweight trace or a watchdog-style checkpoint around the copy, and comparing the buffer contents before and after, would help localize whether the corruption is inbound or outbound. The fix follows from the mechanism, so I wouldn't change the memcpy itself until I know whether it's the culprit or the messenger.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you use the MPU or stack guards to catch the corruption closer to its origin rather than at the fault site? If the corruption turns out to be a DMA coherency issue, how would you confirm that and what would the corrective action look like?
