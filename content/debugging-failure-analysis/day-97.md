# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 97

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The key insight is that the offset appears only when two variables change together — enclosure assembly and final mounting — so the first job is to separate them. I'd build a test matrix: open bench, bench with enclosure but not mounted, and fully installed. If the offset tracks the enclosure alone, I'm looking at something mechanical or thermal — a connector seating differently, a cable routed against a warm component, a board flexing and shifting a solder joint or a sensor's mechanical reference. If it tracks the mounting position, I'm looking at grounding and coupling — the chassis becoming part of the signal return path, a mounting screw creating a ground loop, or the unit sitting near a noise source it wasn't exposed to on the bench.

From there I'd measure rather than speculate. I'd probe the analog front-end at the ADC input in each configuration and compare DC level and noise, then work backward through the signal chain — amplifier output, reference voltage, sensor excitation — to find where the offset first appears. A consistent DC offset usually points to a reference or bias issue: a ground reference that shifts when the chassis is bonded, a divider whose lower leg now includes chassis impedance, or a thermocouple effect at a connector that only fully mates when the enclosure is closed. I'd also check whether the offset is truly constant or drifts with warm-up, because that changes the hypothesis from grounding to thermal.

The discipline is to change one variable at a time and keep a written record of which configuration produces which measurement. Once I can reproduce the offset deterministically in one configuration, I isolate the mechanism with targeted instrumentation — a differential probe across the suspected ground path, a current probe on the chassis bond, or a temporary insulated mount to break the ground path and see if the offset disappears.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a grounding-induced offset from a thermal one if both are plausible? What would you do if the offset only appears in the customer's installation and you can't reproduce the final mounting position in the lab?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and power-distribution problem, so I'd start by characterizing it properly rather than just confirming it exists. First, measure the ripple correctly: a short ground lead on the probe, probing directly across the decoupling capacitor at the load rather than at the regulator output, because the ripple that matters is what the load actually sees. I'd capture the switching event with enough bandwidth to see both the low-frequency droop and the high-frequency spikes, and correlate the ripple in time with the subsystem's switching edge.

Then I'd separate the two mechanisms that produce this symptom. The first is insufficient decoupling or excessive impedance between the regulator and the load — the transient current has to come from somewhere, and if the local capacitance is too small or the trace inductance too high, the rail sags until the regulator loop responds. The second is a control-loop or stability issue — the regulator's transient response is slow or ringing, or the switching subsystem is injecting noise back through a shared ground or supply path. I'd distinguish them by adding bulk and high-frequency capacitance locally at the load and re-measuring: if the ripple collapses, it's a decoupling and impedance problem; if it persists, I'd look at the regulator's loop response and the return path.

I'd also check the return path carefully. A high-current subsystem switching can cause ground bounce if its return current shares impedance with the analog or logic ground, and that shows up as ripple that isn't really on the supply at all but between two "grounds." A differential probe between the regulator's ground reference and the load's ground reference will reveal that quickly. Finally, I'd verify with a current probe on the subsystem's supply to see the actual transient magnitude and di/dt, so the fix — more local capacitance, a ferrite or LC filter, layout change to shorten the high-di/dt loop, or a regulator compensation change — is sized to the real event rather than guessed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between adding bulk capacitance versus a ferrite bead versus changing the layout? What measurement would tell you the regulator's loop is the culprit rather than the decoupling?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** The hard part here is that nothing in the system flags the error, so the investigation has to start with making the failure observable. I'd first characterize the wrong values from the offline data: are they single samples or runs, do they cluster in time, do they correlate with a device state (warm-up, a particular operating mode, a communication event), and is the error a fixed offset, a scaling error, or a bit-level corruption? That pattern analysis usually narrows the mechanism before I touch hardware.

Then I'd instrument the device to capture the full chain at the moment of the event — raw ADC counts, the value after any filtering or calibration, and the value as transmitted — so I can see exactly where the plausible-but-wrong value is introduced. If the raw ADC count is already wrong, it's an analog or timing problem: a sample taken during a switching transient, a multiplexer settling issue, or a reference disturbance. If the raw count is correct but the processed value is wrong, it's a firmware path — a calibration coefficient applied incorrectly, a buffer overwritten, a race between the sampling interrupt and the processing task, or a stale value read from a shared variable without proper synchronization.

Because the error is plausible, I'd also suspect data-handling bugs rather than gross hardware faults: endianness or sign-extension mistakes, an off-by-one in a filter buffer, or a variable shared between an ISR and main context without atomic access. I'd add consistency checks in the firmware — range plausibility, rate-of-change limits, cross-checks against a redundant reading — not just to catch the bug but to turn a silent error into a logged, timestamped event I can correlate. The goal is to convert an intermittent, invisible failure into a reproducible, observable one, then bisect from there.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the logging so it doesn't itself perturb the timing you're trying to observe? If the raw ADC value is correct but the processed value is wrong, what are the first firmware constructs you'd inspect?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's power rail collapses only during a specific operating mode, but the regulator and its passives all measure within specification when the board is powered in isolation?

**Answer:** The fact that everything measures fine in isolation tells me the fault is an interaction, not a component defect, so I'd stop testing parts and start testing the system in the failing mode. First I'd reproduce the collapse reliably and capture it — a scope on the rail triggered on the collapse, with the mode-change signal and the regulator's enable and feedback pins also captured, so I can see the sequence of events rather than just the aftermath.

Then I'd ask what the specific operating mode changes. It could be a load step the regulator can't meet — a subsystem drawing a burst of current beyond what the loop can supply — or it could be a control-side event: the mode change toggling an enable pin, disturbing the feedback divider through a shared node, or a firmware sequence that briefly configures a GPIO that shares a pin or net with the regulator. A rail that collapses rather than sags suggests either a protection trip (overcurrent, thermal, undervoltage lockout) or the regulator being actively disabled. Checking the regulator's status and fault pins during the event distinguishes those.

I'd also look at inrush and sequencing. If the operating mode powers up a capacitive load or a motor or a radio, the inrush can trip current limit or pull the input rail down below the regulator's dropout, and the regulator itself is blameless in isolation because it never sees that transient. Measuring the regulator's input rail and its current during the mode transition, not just its output, is essential. Finally, I'd check for a shared net or pin conflict introduced by the mode — a GPIO reconfigured in firmware that happens to drive the regulator's enable or a nearby net — because that kind of interaction is invisible to any bench test of the regulator alone.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a protection trip and the regulator being disabled? What would you check first if the collapse correlates exactly with a firmware mode transition rather than with a load change?

## Q5: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is to argue about which is more likely. I'd reframe the discussion around discriminating evidence: for each hypothesis, what observation would be true if it were correct and false if it weren't? Then I'd design the cheapest, fastest test that separates them — ideally one test that gives a different, unambiguous result under each hypothesis. That converts a debate into an experiment, and it also gives the team a shared, objective basis for the decision instead of people defending positions.

Practically, I'd make the two hypotheses explicit and written down, with the evidence for and against each, so everyone is working from the same picture and gaps become visible. I'd assign owners to each line of inquiry so both get pursued in parallel rather than the team splitting into camps, and I'd set a decision point: a specific test or date after which we commit based on the evidence. If the discriminating test is expensive or slow, I'd look for a lower-cost proxy first.

I'd also manage the human side deliberately. People who have invested in a hypothesis can feel their judgment is being challenged, so I'd frame the process as the team testing both ideas fairly, credit the reasoning behind each, and make clear that being wrong about a hypothesis is normal and not a failure. If the evidence still doesn't fully separate them after the discriminating test, I'd be honest about the residual uncertainty, choose the path that is safest and most reversible, and document the open question and what would resolve it — rather than forcing false certainty. The deliverable is a defensible, evidence-based conclusion with the reasoning traceable, not just a winner.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is too expensive to run within the schedule? How do you keep the team that "lost" the argument engaged and productive afterward?
