# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 90

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's power rail collapses only during a specific operating mode, but the regulator and its passives all measure within specification when the board is powered in isolation?

**Answer:** The key insight is that "the regulator measures fine in isolation" tells you almost nothing about its behavior in the failing mode — a regulator that is stable at no load or light load can be marginal under a fast transient, and a passives network that looks correct on a bench meter can still be wrong for the loop. I'd start by reproducing the collapse with the board in its actual failing configuration and capturing the rail with a scope on a short ground lead at the load point, not at the regulator output — the difference between those two nodes is often the whole story. I'd trigger on the falling edge and look at what happens in the tens of microseconds before the collapse: is there a load step, a mode-transition current surge, a control-loop ringing event, or a supply droop upstream of the regulator? Then I'd divide the problem: does the collapse follow the load (swap in an electronic load that mimics the mode's current profile) or follow the board (does the same rail on a known-good unit survive)? If it follows the load, I'd profile the transient — rise time, magnitude, duration — and compare it against the regulator's load-transient spec and the output capacitance's actual impedance at that frequency. A very common finding is that the bulk and decoupling capacitance is adequate in total value but wrong in distribution, so the high-frequency transient is being served by a long loop with too much inductance. I'd also check the enable and feedback pins for glitches during the mode transition, and verify the mode change isn't momentarily reconfiguring the regulator or its sequencing. Throughout, I'd change one variable at a time and keep a written log of each experiment and result, because power-collapse investigations generate a lot of plausible hypotheses and you need the record to avoid re-testing the same idea.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a control-loop instability from a genuine load-induced droop using only scope captures? What would you do if the collapse only reproduced on the customer's unit and not on any of your lab boards?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a source-path-susceptibility problem and work all three legs of it. First, characterize the ripple properly: probe at the decoupling capacitors with a very short ground connection — a long ground clip will pick up radiated switching noise and lie to you — and capture both the time-domain waveform and an FFT so I know whether the ripple is at the switching fundamental, at a harmonic, or at a lower-frequency envelope like a beat or a load-repetition rate. That distinction drives everything: fundamental-frequency ripple points at output capacitance and loop response, while high-frequency content points at layout, ESL, and return-path issues. Second, look at the aggressor: how fast are the switching edges, what's the di/dt, and is the current returning where I think it is? A high-current subsystem with a poorly controlled return path will inject noise into the rail through shared impedance, and no amount of extra capacitance at the victim will fix a shared-return problem. Third, check the victim's susceptibility: what is the actual impedance of the decoupling network at the frequencies I measured, and is the sensitive circuit referenced to a quiet ground? I'd also verify the ripple isn't an artifact of where I'm probing — measuring across a ferrite bead or across a long trace will show ripple that the IC never sees. Once I have a hypothesis, I'd test it cheaply and reversibly: add a temporary bulk cap right at the load, then a high-frequency cap, then improve the return path with a copper braid or an added ground stitch, and see which change actually moves the measurement. Only after the mechanism is confirmed would I propose a layout or BOM change, and I'd re-verify with the same probe setup so the before/after comparison is honest.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell whether the ripple is conducted through the supply or coupled radiatively from the switching subsystem? What layout changes would you prioritize if the ripple is dominated by the return path?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** This is one of the harder classes of bug because every layer of defense has already passed — the value is in range, the self-test is happy, and there's no fault flag — so the investigation has to start by making the failure observable rather than by guessing at a cause. My first move is to instrument the data path end to end: capture the raw ADC or bus transaction at the hardware boundary, the value as stored in memory, and the value as transmitted, all time-stamped, so I can localize where the corruption is introduced. If the raw reading is already wrong, the problem is upstream in the analog front-end, the sensor interface, or the sampling timing. If the raw reading is right but the stored or transmitted value is wrong, I'm looking at a software path issue — a buffer aliasing problem, a race between the sampling ISR and the consumer, a scaling or calibration step applied twice or with stale coefficients, or a type/sign-extension error. The "plausible but wrong" characteristic is itself a clue: single-bit flips, byte-order swaps, and stale-buffer reuse all tend to produce values that look physically reasonable. I'd also look hard at the conditions under which bad values occur — time since boot, temperature, which other subsystems were active, whether the device was in a particular mode — because a correlation with a specific state often points straight at the mechanism. Fault injection is valuable here: deliberately corrupt or delay the data at each stage and confirm the downstream checks catch it, which both tests my hypotheses and reveals gaps in the validation that let this escape in the first place. Finally, I'd make sure the corrective action includes a detection improvement, not just a fix — if a plausible-but-wrong value can reach the output undetected, that's a separate finding worth its own corrective action.

**Possible follow-ups:** What kinds of runtime plausibility checks would you add without risking false positives on legitimate physiological variation? How would you distinguish a stale-buffer bug from a genuine sensor transient?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The fact that the offset appears only after assembly and installation tells me the mechanism is almost certainly mechanical, thermal, or electromagnetic rather than a component tolerance — the electronics didn't change, the environment did. I'd work through those three systematically. Mechanically, assembly can flex the PCB, change connector mating force, or alter the grounding path through chassis contact; a small offset that appears with enclosure torque often points at a ground reference or shield connection that's made or broken by the mechanical stack-up. Thermally, a sealed enclosure changes the internal temperature profile, and a consistent offset that appears only when assembled is a classic signature of a temperature-dependent term — bridge offset drift, amplifier input offset drift, or a reference that's being heated by a nearby component. I'd measure the actual internal temperature in both states and compare, and if there's a delta, I'd separate "temperature" from "assembly" by running the open unit in a chamber at the assembled unit's internal temperature. Electromagnetically, the enclosure and final mounting position change the coupling environment — a metal chassis or a nearby ground plane can shift stray fields into a high-impedance front-end. I'd probe the front-end with a scope in both states, look at the input pins and the reference, and try to reproduce the offset by bringing a grounded plate or the enclosure near the open unit. The discipline throughout is to reproduce the offset on the bench by adding one environmental factor at a time, because once I can reproduce it in a controlled state I can measure the mechanism directly instead of inferring it.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset turns out to be thermal, how would you decide between compensating in firmware and changing the hardware? How would you verify the fix across the full production population rather than just the units you have?

## Q5: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is reframe the situation for the team: two live hypotheses with partial evidence is not a stalemate, it's a normal mid-investigation state, and the job now is to design the discriminating test rather than to argue the merits of each theory. I'd ask each camp to state, concretely, what evidence would falsify their hypothesis and what observable difference the two mechanisms would produce — because if two hypotheses predict identical observations, they're not actually different explanations and the team is arguing about labels. Usually there is a discriminating experiment: a condition under which one mechanism predicts the failure and the other doesn't, or a measurement that should differ between them. I'd prioritize tests by information gained per unit of time and cost, and I'd run them in parallel where possible rather than serially, since schedule pressure is usually the real constraint. I'd also make sure the disagreement is about evidence and not about ownership — if the split maps onto team boundaries, I'd explicitly separate "whose area is it" from "what does the data say," and assign the discriminating test to someone neutral or to a pair drawn from both sides. I'd set a decision point in advance: if the discriminating test is inconclusive by a certain date, we escalate to a broader fault-injection or teardown campaign rather than continuing to debate. And I'd document the reasoning and the decision criteria as we go, because whatever we conclude has to survive review, and the record of why we ruled out the other hypothesis is as important as the conclusion itself.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive and the schedule won't allow it? How do you keep the losing side of the debate engaged and productive after the decision is made?
