# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 110

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's power-on reset circuit appears to work correctly on every unit you test, yet a small fraction of field returns show the device failing to start until the battery is disconnected and reconnected?

**Answer:** I'd start by recognizing that "works on every unit I test" is itself a data point — it means the failure is either rare, condition-dependent, or being masked by how I'm testing. The first move is to stop testing fresh units and get the actual returned units onto the bench, because a return that currently passes is still more valuable than a golden sample. I'd try to reproduce the no-start condition by replicating the field power-up sequence rather than a clean bench power-up: slow battery insertion, contact bounce, a partially discharged cell, and a power-up while the device is cold. A POR circuit that depends on an RC time constant or a threshold comparator is sensitive to ramp rate and to the state of the supply at the moment of connection, so I'd scope the reset line and the supply rail simultaneously at the instant of battery contact, looking for a reset pulse that's too narrow, a rail that rises too slowly relative to the reset threshold, or a brownout detector that releases before the supply is actually stable. I'd also check whether the "recovery" by battery disconnect points to a latch-up or a state that only clears on full power removal — that would shift the investigation from the POR network toward the load side. Throughout, I'd keep a written hypothesis list and eliminate one variable at a time rather than shotgunning component swaps.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether this is a design margin issue versus a component tolerance issue? What would you do if you could never reproduce it on the bench?

## Q2: You're debugging a device where a subsystem intermittently draws a large current spike at power-up, occasionally tripping the main supply's overcurrent protection. The spike doesn't appear on every unit and doesn't correlate with any obvious firmware event. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Inrush and transient load behavior is one of those areas where a DMM tells you almost nothing, so my first step is instrumentation: a current probe or a shunt with a wide-bandwidth scope, triggered on the supply rail, capturing the first few hundred milliseconds after enable. I want to see the shape of the spike — its amplitude, duration, and whether it's a single event or a burst — because that shape usually points at the mechanism. A single fast spike suggests a capacitive load charging through a low-impedance path or a hot-swap controller that isn't limiting properly; a burst suggests something oscillating or a regulator going unstable during startup. I'd then divide the load: disable subsystems one at a time, or use a current-limited bench supply set just above nominal so a trip becomes a repeatable, observable event rather than a rare one. If the spike is unit-dependent, I'd compare the passives on the suspect path across units — a capacitor with the wrong dielectric or a marginal soft-start component can shift the inrush enough to matter. The key discipline is to make the failure reproducible under controlled conditions before theorizing about root cause, because "occasionally trips" is not yet an investigation, it's a symptom.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine inrush problem from a supply that's simply undersized for the load? What role would derating and margin analysis play here?

## Q3: How would you structure a failure investigation when a returned medical device shows a symptom that could plausibly be caused by either a latent component defect or a firmware state that only occurs under a specific sequence of user actions — and you have limited returned units to destructively test?

**Answer:** With limited samples, the constraint itself shapes the plan: I can't afford to burn a unit on a guess, so I'd front-load all the non-destructive evidence before touching anything. That means pulling the device logs and any stored fault codes first, reconstructing the usage timeline, and checking whether the symptom correlates with a particular sequence of operations — because if it does, I can try to reproduce it on a lab unit rather than consuming a return. In parallel I'd do external inspection and non-destructive testing: thermal imaging during a controlled power-up, supply rail measurements at test points, and where accessible, in-circuit checks that don't require desoldering. I'd explicitly separate the two hypotheses and ask what evidence would discriminate between them — a latent component defect usually shows up as a parametric drift or an intermittent open/short under stress, while a firmware state bug usually reproduces when the triggering sequence is replayed. If the logs suggest a sequence, I'd invest lab units in reproducing it; if they suggest a stress-related component issue, I'd reserve one return for controlled stress and eventual decap or cross-section. The 8D discipline helps here: contain first, then define the problem precisely before committing scarce samples to destructive analysis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when you have enough evidence to close the investigation versus needing more samples? How do you document the traceability from root cause to corrective action?

## Q4: A device passes all functional tests at room temperature and at the specified cold and hot operating limits, but a small number of field units fail only in what appears to be a narrow temperature band partway through warm-up. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** A failure that appears only in a band during transition — not at the endpoints — is a strong hint that two temperature-dependent behaviors are crossing: something like a bias current and a leakage current, or a mechanical tolerance and a thermal expansion, that are individually fine at the extremes but coincide in the middle. My approach would be to stop testing at setpoints and instead run a slow thermal ramp while continuously logging the relevant signals, so I can see exactly where in the ramp the failure appears and whether it's repeatable at that temperature. I'd instrument the suspect node directly — an analog output, a reference voltage, a comparator threshold — and compare its behavior against the datasheet curves for the parts in that path. If the failure is a threshold crossing, I'd look for two parameters with opposite temperature coefficients meeting in that band. I'd also consider whether the "band" is actually a rate effect rather than a temperature effect: a slow ramp gives the device time to reach thermal equilibrium internally, while a fast ramp might not, so I'd vary the ramp rate to separate the two. The goal is to convert "fails in a band" into a specific measurable condition I can reproduce on demand, because only then can I confirm a root cause rather than a correlation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine temperature effect from a rate-of-change effect? What would you do if the band shifted between units?

## Q5: You're leading a failure investigation and you've gathered strong evidence pointing to a root cause, but implementing the corrective action would require a design change that affects the schedule significantly. A stakeholder is pushing to ship a firmware workaround instead. How would you handle this?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a decision that needs to be made on evidence and risk, not on schedule pressure alone, and my job is to make the trade-off explicit rather than to win the argument. First I'd make sure the root cause is actually established to the level where a corrective action is justified — a workaround is only defensible if it genuinely addresses the mechanism, not just the symptom, and I'd want to state clearly what the workaround does and does not fix. Then I'd lay out the options side by side: the design change with its schedule and validation cost, the firmware workaround with its residual risk and any conditions under which it could fail, and a phased approach where the workaround buys time while the design change is validated. For a medical device, the residual-risk question is the one that has to be answered honestly, because a workaround that masks a hardware margin issue may not hold across the full population and operating envelope. I'd bring the discussion back to the evidence — what we know, what we don't, and what would have to be true for the workaround to be safe — and I'd want the decision documented with its rationale so it's traceable later. If the stakeholder still wants to proceed, I'd want the residual risk explicitly accepted by the right owner rather than absorbed silently by the team.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you validate that a firmware workaround is actually effective rather than just hiding the symptom? How do you keep the team motivated when the technically correct path is the slower one?
