# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 48

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a medical device where the failure occurs only when the device is connected to a specific model of external equipment — for example, a particular patient monitor — but never with other equipment or test instruments?

**Answer:** I'd start by treating the external equipment as part of the system under test rather than assuming it's innocent. The first step is to characterize exactly what's different about that specific model — is it the ground reference, the isolation scheme, the cable pinout, the data protocol, or something about its internal power supply? I'd begin by documenting the electrical interface between the two devices: pin-by-pin signal definitions, grounding topology, and whether either device is earth-referenced or floating. A common culprit in medical devices is a ground loop or a leakage current path between two pieces of patient-connected equipment — one device may have a different earth ground impedance or a different isolation barrier configuration. I'd measure the voltage difference between the grounds of the two devices under operating conditions, and look at common-mode voltage on the signal lines. I'd also check whether the failure correlates with the cable length, cable shielding termination, or the specific pinout of that monitor's connector. If the interface is digital, I'd capture the actual waveforms on the communication lines when connected to the problem monitor versus a known-good one — looking for timing margin, signal levels, and noise. The key principle is to isolate the variable: swap one element at a time (cable, adapter, interface board) between the two configurations to narrow down whether the problem is in the monitor's output stage, the cable, or the interaction between the two devices' ground systems. I'd also review both devices' electrical safety specifications — particularly leakage current limits and isolation ratings — because a medical device that's compliant on its own can still interact badly with another compliant device.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the issue is a safety concern requiring immediate mitigation versus a compatibility issue that can be documented and managed? What measurements would you take to confirm a ground loop hypothesis?

## Q2: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** This is a classic case where the stack trace tells you where the crash happened, but not necessarily why. The fact that the fault consistently points to a memory copy operation with valid addresses suggests the problem is likely a corrupted state that manifests during the copy, rather than a fundamental addressing error. I'd approach this systematically. First, I'd examine the fault status registers to determine the exact cause of the hard fault — is it a bus fault, a usage fault, an alignment violation, or an undefined instruction? That information narrows the investigation considerably. If it's a bus fault during a read or write, the address in the fault status register might reveal which memory region was actually being accessed when the fault occurred — it may differ from what the stack trace suggests. Next, I'd look at the memory copy operation itself: is it using DMA, a hardware memcpy accelerator, or a software loop? If it's a software loop, I'd check whether the loop counter or pointer variables could be corrupted by an interrupt or another task. A common cause is a stack overflow in a different context — the stack trace may be misleading because the corruption happened elsewhere and only manifested during the copy. I'd check the stack pointer at the time of the fault and compare it to the stack limits. I'd also examine whether the memory regions involved could be affected by a DMA operation running concurrently — if a DMA transfer is writing to the source or destination buffer while the copy is executing, you can get intermittent corruption that's very hard to reproduce. I'd review the interrupt priorities and whether any ISR could preempt the copy operation and modify the memory contents. Another angle is to check whether the fault is actually a hard fault from an unhandled exception — for example, if a divide-by-zero or an undefined instruction occurs elsewhere, the hard fault handler's stack trace may point to the wrong location entirely. I'd also consider whether the memory copy is operating on memory that's been optimized by the compiler in a way that assumes alignment — if the source or destination is misaligned, some architectures will fault. Finally, I'd try to capture the fault in a reproducible way: enable the fault handler to log the full register state, the fault status registers, and the stack contents to non-volatile memory before resetting. That data often reveals the true story — the stack trace alone is just the starting point.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether this is a stack overflow issue versus a memory corruption issue from a rogue pointer? What debugging tools or techniques would you use to catch the corruption at the moment it happens?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's battery charging circuit overheats, but only when the device is placed on a specific type of conductive surface (like a metal cart)? The charger IC is not damaged, and the device charges normally on a non-conductive surface.

**Answer:** This is a fascinating failure mode because it suggests an interaction between the device's charging circuit and its thermal or electrical environment. I'd start by considering what's different about a conductive surface. The most obvious hypothesis is thermal: a metal surface can act as a heat sink, which should actually help cooling — so the fact that it overheats on a conductive surface is counterintuitive. That points me toward an electrical mechanism rather than a thermal one. A conductive surface could create an unintended electrical path — for example, if the device's enclosure has any exposed metal, or if there's a ground plane or chassis connection that contacts the surface, the metal cart could create a ground loop or a short circuit path. I'd examine the device's mechanical design: is there any exposed metal on the bottom of the enclosure? Could the metal cart be bridging two points on the PCB through the enclosure? Another possibility is electromagnetic coupling — the metal surface could be acting as a reflector or antenna that changes the device's RF environment, but that's less likely to cause charging circuit overheating. I'd also consider whether the conductive surface is creating a capacitive coupling path that increases common-mode current on the charging cable — if the device is plugged into a wall adapter while sitting on the metal cart, the cart could provide a lower-impedance path to earth ground, altering the charging circuit's behavior. I'd start by measuring the device's temperature profile on both surfaces using a thermal camera to confirm where the heat is generated — is it the charger IC, the battery, the charging cable connector, or something else? Then I'd measure the charging current and voltage waveforms on both surfaces to see if the charging algorithm is behaving differently. I'd also check whether the metal cart is connected to earth ground or floating — that distinction matters. If the cart is grounded, I'd measure the voltage between the device's ground and the cart surface. I'd also inspect the device's enclosure for any conductive path — even a small gap or a screw head that contacts the surface could create the problem. Finally, I'd review the charging circuit's thermal design: is the charger IC's thermal pad properly connected to a copper pour? Is there adequate thermal relief? The conductive surface might be changing the thermal path in an unexpected way — for example, if the surface creates a hot spot by reflecting heat back or by creating a localized short that increases current in a specific trace.

**Possible follow-ups:** What safety considerations would you prioritize in this investigation given that it involves a battery charging circuit? How would you determine whether this is a design flaw requiring a hardware change or an environmental condition that needs to be addressed through labeling or instructions for use?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's analog front-end shows an increased noise floor only when the device is connected to a specific patient monitor model, but not when connected to other monitors or test equipment?

**Answer:** This is fundamentally an electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) and grounding interaction problem. The key observation is that the noise is specific to one model of patient monitor, which tells me the problem is in the interaction between the two devices rather than a defect in either one alone. I'd structure the investigation around three main hypotheses: conducted noise through the cable, radiated coupling, and ground-related issues. First, I'd characterize the noise itself — what's its frequency content, amplitude, and waveform shape? Is it broadband or narrowband? Does it correlate with the monitor's processing activity, display refresh, or communication bursts? I'd use a spectrum analyzer or FFT on the analog front-end output to identify the noise signature. If the noise has a specific frequency, I can often trace it back to a clock or switching frequency in the monitor. Next, I'd examine the cable connection between the two devices — is it a shielded cable? How is the shield terminated? Is there a ground loop through the cable shield and the monitor's ground? A common issue is that the patient monitor has a different ground reference than the medical device, creating a ground loop that injects common-mode noise into the analog front-end. I'd measure the common-mode voltage between the two devices' grounds and look at whether the noise correlates with that voltage. I'd also test with a ground isolation transformer or a different cable to see if the noise changes. For radiated coupling, I'd use near-field probes to scan the cable and the device enclosure to identify where the noise is entering the analog front-end. The patient monitor might be radiating energy that's coupling into the device's analog circuitry — either through the cable acting as an antenna or directly into the PCB. I'd also check whether the monitor's power supply is the source — some monitors have switching power supplies that generate significant conducted and radiated emissions. If the noise is coming through the monitor's power supply, I'd see it on the monitor's own ground and possibly on the medical device's ground if they share a power outlet. I'd also consider whether the patient monitor is creating a leakage current path through the patient — in a medical context, this has safety implications beyond just signal quality. I'd involve the EMC engineer early and consider whether this needs to be addressed through filtering, shielding, cable changes, or a design change to the analog front-end's input stage.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether this is a conducted or radiated coupling issue? What design changes would you consider to make the analog front-end more immune to this type of interference?

## Q5: How would you handle a situation where you're leading a failure investigation, and you discover that the root cause points to a design decision made by a senior engineer who is still on the team — and the finding could reflect poorly on them or create tension within the group?

**Answer:** This is a situation where the technical investigation is straightforward, but the human dynamics require careful handling. The priority is to keep the investigation objective and focused on the facts, not on assigning blame. I'd start by making sure the evidence is solid — before raising the finding with anyone, I'd want to be confident that the root cause is well-supported by data and that I can explain the chain of reasoning clearly. Once I'm confident, I'd approach the senior engineer directly and privately, before sharing the finding more broadly. The goal is to give them the opportunity to engage with the evidence and contribute their perspective — they may have context about the design decision that I don't have, such as constraints that existed at the time or trade-offs that were made deliberately. I'd frame the conversation as a technical discussion, not an accusation: "We've traced this failure to a design decision that was made during the original development. I'd like to walk you through the evidence and get your input on whether there's something we're missing." This respects their expertise and gives them a chance to be part of the solution rather than being blindsided by the finding. If they disagree with the conclusion, I'd listen to their reasoning and evaluate it on the merits — they might have valid points that change the analysis. If they agree, I'd work with them to develop the corrective action, which gives them ownership of the fix. When presenting the findings to the broader team or to management, I'd focus on the technical root cause and the corrective action, not on who made the original decision. In a healthy engineering culture, design reviews and failure investigations are opportunities for learning, not blame. I'd also consider whether there's a systemic issue — for example, if the design review process didn't catch the issue, that's a process gap that needs to be addressed. The goal is to ensure the fix is implemented correctly and that similar issues are prevented in the future, while maintaining a collaborative team environment.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the senior engineer becomes defensive or dismissive of the evidence? How would you handle presenting the finding to management if the senior engineer disagrees with the conclusion?
