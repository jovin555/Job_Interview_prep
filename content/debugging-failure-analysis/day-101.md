# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 101

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?
**Answer:** The key insight is that the offset appears only when two variables change together — mechanical assembly and mounting position — so the first job is to separate them. I'd build a test matrix: open bench with harness, board in enclosure but not mounted, enclosure mounted in the final orientation, and enclosure mounted with the final cable routing. Measuring the offset at each step tells me whether it's assembly-induced (enclosure, shielding, cable dress) or installation-induced (mounting surface, grounding path, nearby metal). Since the offset is consistent rather than noisy, I'd suspect a DC-level mechanism rather than an AC coupling one: a changed ground reference, a shield now tied to chassis at a different point, a cable routed near a magnetic or conductive surface altering a bridge sensor's excitation return, or mechanical stress on a component shifting its value. I'd measure the analog front-end's reference and ground potentials relative to the enclosure at each configuration, and compare the sensor excitation voltage — a millivolt-level shift in excitation or reference shows up as a scaled offset. I'd also check whether the offset scales with the sensor's full-scale output (gain error) or is fixed in absolute terms (true offset), because that distinction narrows the mechanism considerably. Once I can reproduce the offset reliably in one configuration, I'd change one variable at a time — shield bonding point, cable route, mounting fastener torque — to confirm the mechanism before proposing a fix.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a ground-reference shift from mechanical stress on the sensor itself? If the offset only appears with the final cable, how would you verify the cable is the cause rather than the enclosure?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?
**Answer:** This is a load-transient and power-distribution problem, so I'd start by characterizing it properly rather than guessing at components. First, measure the ripple at the load — right at the switching subsystem's supply pins — not just at the regulator output, because the difference between those two points is the distribution impedance and tells me whether the problem is the regulator's transient response or the impedance between regulator and load. I'd capture the transient with a wide-bandwidth probe and a short ground lead, and look at the shape: a fast spike suggests ESL in the decoupling path or via inductance, a slower droop suggests insufficient bulk capacitance or regulator loop bandwidth, and ringing suggests a resonance between the bulk cap and the distribution inductance. I'd also check whether the ripple is differential (measured across the rail and its return) or common-mode (appearing on both), since common-mode ripple points to ground bounce and return-path problems rather than decoupling. Then I'd verify the switching subsystem's own input filtering — is it drawing a pulsed current that the upstream network wasn't designed for? The fix space is: more or better-placed decoupling, reduced loop area in the return path, added bulk capacitance, or a change to the regulator's compensation — but which one is right depends on whether the measurement says the problem is source impedance, distribution impedance, or load behavior.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure the distribution impedance between the regulator and the load? What would a resonance between bulk capacitance and trace inductance look like on the scope, and how would you confirm it?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?
**Answer:** The hardest part of this class of bug is that nothing in the system flags it, so the investigation has to start by making the failure observable. I'd first characterize the error from the offline data: is the wrong value a single sample or a run of samples, does it correlate with a particular operating mode, temperature, or time since power-on, and is the error pattern consistent with a bit flip, a stale read, a scaling error, or a timing issue where the firmware latched the value mid-conversion? That characterization usually points at the layer. Then I'd add instrumentation to catch it in the act — log the raw ADC output alongside the processed value, timestamp the read against the sensor's data-ready signal, and if possible capture the bus transaction so I can see whether the sensor sent the wrong value or the firmware misread a correct one. A common mechanism in this scenario is reading the sensor outside its conversion window, or a race between the data-ready interrupt and the read routine, which produces a plausible but stale or partially updated value. Another is a scaling or sign-extension bug that only manifests for certain input ranges. I'd build a fault-injection or stress harness — sweep the input across its range, vary timing, run at temperature extremes — to raise the occurrence rate so I can iterate quickly. The principle is: convert an invisible, rare failure into a visible, reproducible one before theorizing about root cause.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the sensor sent a bad value versus the firmware misreading a good one? What instrumentation would you add without perturbing the timing you're trying to observe?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?
**Answer:** When two hypotheses survive, the productive move is not to debate them but to design a discriminating test — an experiment whose outcome differs depending on which hypothesis is true. I'd frame it explicitly: for each candidate cause, what would we expect to observe if it's correct, and what would we expect if it's wrong? Then I'd look for the cheapest, fastest test that separates those predictions. If no single test discriminates, I'd pursue both in parallel with clearly assigned owners and a defined time-box, so the split doesn't stall progress. I'd also make the evidence explicit — a simple matrix of observations against each hypothesis, marking what's confirmed, what's contradicted, and what's simply unknown — because teams often split because each side is weighting different evidence, and putting it on one page exposes the actual gaps. I'd be honest about decision criteria: if the discriminating test isn't feasible in the time available, we choose the hypothesis with the stronger mechanistic support, implement a fix that's safe under either cause if possible, and define what field or test data would confirm or refute the choice afterward. What I'd avoid is letting the split harden into a factional argument or letting schedule pressure force a premature conclusion without a documented rationale.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive and the schedule won't allow it? How do you keep the team aligned when you decide to pursue one hypothesis over the other?

## Q5: A junior engineer on your team has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?
**Answer:** The pattern here — components pass in isolation, the system fails — usually means the bug lives in an interaction, not in any single part, so the first thing I'd do is help the engineer reframe the problem rather than push harder on the same approach. I'd sit down with them and ask what they've established versus what they've assumed, and specifically whether they've reproduced the failure in a controlled way; if they can't reproduce it on demand, that's the real blocker, and the priority becomes building a harness or stress setup that makes it occur reliably. I'd also ask what they've measured at the system level — not component-level pass/fail, but the actual signals at the boundary between the suspect subsystems, under the conditions where the failure occurs. Often the isolation testing is thorough but the system-level instrumentation is thin. I'd resist the temptation to just take over; instead I'd pair with them on the next experiment, model the reasoning out loud, and let them drive so the skill transfers. On the schedule side, I'd be transparent with stakeholders about where we are and set a checkpoint, rather than letting the pressure push the engineer toward a guess-and-patch cycle. And I'd acknowledge the frustration directly — being stuck for days is genuinely demoralizing, and naming it helps more than pretending the schedule is the only issue.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you help them build a reliable reproduction when the failure is rare? What would you do if, after pairing, you concluded their approach was fundamentally sound but the bug was simply hard — how do you manage the schedule conversation then?
