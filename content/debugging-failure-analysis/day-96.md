# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 96

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a failure where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The key observation is that the offset is deterministic and tied to mechanical state, not to time or temperature — that points away from drift mechanisms and toward something that changes when the assembly is closed or mounted. I'd start by treating "bench harness" versus "assembled and installed" as two distinct configurations and enumerating every difference between them: cable routing and length, grounding topology, shielding continuity, mechanical stress on the PCB, proximity to the enclosure or mounting hardware, and the presence of the final power source rather than a lab supply. Then I'd change one variable at a time — run the unit open but in its final mounting position, then closed but on the bench, then closed and mounted — to isolate which of those differences correlates with the offset appearing.

A very common mechanism here is mechanical: mounting screws or enclosure features flexing the board and inducing strain in a solder joint, a connector, or a precision resistor network, or altering the contact resistance of a connector that isn't fully seated until the enclosure is closed. Another is grounding: the enclosure may bond to the board at a point that creates a ground loop or shifts the reference potential the front-end sees, which shows up as a fixed offset rather than noise. I'd measure the actual node voltages at the amplifier input and reference pins in both configurations with a high-impedance probe, and compare against the schematic's intended bias points. If the offset tracks a mechanical state, I'd also do a strain check — flex the board gently with the unit powered and watch the reading — and inspect connector seating and fastener torque. The fix usually falls out of the mechanism: a grounding change, a connector retention change, a stiffener, or a re-layout of the affected trace.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset disappears when you loosen the mounting screws, what does that tell you, and what would you check next? How would you distinguish a ground-reference shift from a genuine sensor bridge imbalance in this scenario?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Load-dependent ripple is almost always about the interaction between the switching load's current profile and the impedance the rail presents at those frequencies — so I'd start by characterizing both sides rather than guessing at a component. First, I'd capture the load current waveform itself with a current probe or a shunt, to understand the di/dt, the repetition rate, and whether the disturbance is a single edge or a sustained oscillation. Then I'd measure the rail voltage at multiple points: at the regulator output, at the bulk capacitor, and at the decoupling capacitors closest to the load. The difference between those measurement points tells you where the impedance is too high — if the ripple is much worse at the load than at the regulator, the problem is in the distribution network between them, not the regulator.

Probing technique matters enormously here. I'd use a short-ground-tip probe or a coaxial pigtail, because a long ground lead will pick up switching noise and magnetic coupling and make you chase a problem that's partly an artifact. I'd also check whether the ripple is truly on the rail or is common-mode pickup on the probe. On the design side, the usual suspects are insufficient bulk capacitance for the transient, high ESR or ESL in the capacitor bank, a ferrite bead or filter component whose impedance peak lands right at the load's switching frequency, or a layout where the high-current return path shares impedance with the analog reference. I'd verify the regulator's loop stability under the actual load step too — a marginally stable loop can ring badly only at certain load conditions. The corrective action follows the measurement: more or better-placed bulk capacitance, a revised filter, a layout change to separate the return paths, or a compensation adjustment.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a regulator loop-stability problem and a decoupling-network impedance problem from the scope trace alone? What would you check first if the ripple frequency doesn't match the load's switching frequency?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** This is one of the harder classes of failure because there's no fault signal to anchor on — the system believes it's healthy. The first thing I'd do is turn a silent error into a detectable one, because you can't debug what you can't see. That means adding instrumentation: a checksum or CRC over the sensor data path, a plausibility check against a redundant or slower reference measurement, and timestamped logging of the raw value alongside the processed value so you can tell whether the corruption happens at acquisition, in the processing chain, or in storage and transmission. If the device has any spare bandwidth, I'd log the raw ADC counts and the intermediate values, not just the final result.

Then I'd think about where a plausible-but-wrong value can originate. Candidates include a race condition where the firmware reads a multi-byte value while it's being updated (a classic torn read), a buffer that's reused before it's fully populated, a scaling or calibration constant that's occasionally loaded from uninitialized or stale memory, an ADC conversion that completes but is read one sample late, or a communication transfer that silently drops or duplicates a byte without a framing error. The fact that the value is plausible argues against gross corruption and toward a subtle timing or indexing issue. I'd try to reproduce it under stress — high processor load, concurrent communication, temperature extremes — and use a debugger or trace facility to watch the data path when it happens. Once I have a candidate mechanism, I'd write a targeted test that forces the suspected timing window and confirms the failure appears and then disappears when the fix is applied.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the plausibility check so it doesn't mask the very errors you're trying to find? If the corruption only appears in the logged data but the live display looks correct, where would you focus?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** The worst outcome here is letting the team split into camps and argue from opinion, so my job is to convert the disagreement into a testable question. I'd start by writing down both hypotheses explicitly — the proposed mechanism, the evidence for each, and critically, the observation that would distinguish them. If both hypotheses predict the same observable behavior, then neither is specific enough yet and the real task is to find a discriminating measurement. Often there is one: the two mechanisms differ in some dimension — timing, temperature dependence, unit-to-unit variability, sensitivity to a specific configuration — and a well-designed experiment can separate them.

I'd then prioritize by cost and information gain: which test is fastest and most decisive? I'd assign owners to each hypothesis so both get pursued in parallel rather than sequentially, which also keeps the team engaged rather than feeling their view was dismissed. I'd set a decision point — a date or a data threshold — at which we reconvene and let the evidence decide, and I'd be explicit that we're not voting, we're testing. If the evidence genuinely can't separate them in the time available, I'd consider whether a containment or corrective action can address both mechanisms safely, but I'd be clear that's risk mitigation, not root-cause closure, and I'd keep the investigation open. Throughout, I'd document the reasoning so the eventual conclusion is traceable and defensible, which matters a lot in a regulated medical device context.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or takes weeks, and the schedule won't allow it? How do you keep the team from treating the parallel investigation as a competition between individuals?

## Q5: A junior engineer has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The pattern here — everything passes in isolation, the system fails — is a strong signal that the debugging strategy itself is the problem, not the engineer's effort. Component-level testing assumes the fault lives in a component, but an intermittent system-level failure usually lives in an interaction: timing, grounding, power distribution, or a condition that only exists when everything is connected and running. So my first move is to reframe the problem with them rather than take it over. I'd ask what they've ruled out and how, and then walk through what the isolation testing actually proves — it proves the components are good, which is useful, but it doesn't exercise the interactions.

Then I'd help them shift to a strategy that captures the failure in the act: instrument the system so the failure leaves a trace when it happens, rather than trying to catch it by inspection. That might mean adding logging, using a trigger-based capture on a scope, or setting up a long-duration stress loop that reproduces the condition faster. I'd also encourage them to write down the failure's characteristics precisely — how often, under what conditions, what correlates with it — because a vague "intermittent" is very hard to attack, while "fails roughly once every few hours, more often when warm" is a testable statement. On the human side, I'd normalize the frustration: being stuck for days is a normal part of hard debugging, not a sign of failure, and the fix is usually a change of method, not more hours. I'd check in regularly without hovering, and make sure they know asking for a second pair of eyes is expected practice, not an admission of weakness.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when to step in and take a more hands-on role versus continuing to coach? What would you do if the engineer resisted changing their approach because they were sure the component testing would eventually find it?
