# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 52

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a device that fails only when two subsystems are active at the same time — for example, a wireless radio transmitting while a high-current actuator is switching — but neither subsystem alone produces any failure?

**Answer:** This is a classic interaction fault, and the key insight is that the failure lives in the coupling between the two subsystems, not inside either one. I'd start by characterizing each subsystem's behavior in isolation with good instrumentation — current draw, switching edges, radiated emissions — so I have a baseline. Then I'd bring them up together and look specifically at the shared resources they compete for: the power rail, the ground return path, the clock tree, and any shared bus. The most common mechanisms are supply droop from combined load transients, ground bounce where the actuator's return current flows through a path shared with sensitive analog or RF circuitry, and conducted or radiated coupling from the switching node into the radio's front end. I'd use a scope with a good differential probe to look at the rail right at the victim's decoupling capacitor, not at the regulator output, because the regulator output can look clean while the local rail is collapsing. I'd also try to break the coupling deliberately — add bulk capacitance locally, reroute the return path, temporarily shield or move the antenna — and see which intervention changes the symptom. The one that changes it tells me the dominant mechanism. Throughout, I'd change one variable at a time and keep a written log, because interaction bugs are easy to chase in circles.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether the fix belongs in hardware or firmware?
- If adding capacitance reduced but didn't eliminate the failure, what would that tell you?

## Q2: A device passes all functional tests at the module level and at the system level on the bench, but fails intermittently only after a firmware update that changed the order of peripheral initialization. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The correlation with initialization order is the strongest clue, so I'd treat the firmware change as the prime suspect while still keeping an open mind about a latent hardware marginality it exposed. First I'd get a clean reproduction: run the new firmware and the previous firmware side by side on identical hardware under the same conditions, ideally in a loop, to confirm the failure tracks the firmware and not the unit. Then I'd diff the initialization sequence carefully — not just the order of calls, but the timing between them. Reordering init often changes when a peripheral is taken out of reset, when its clock is enabled, when its pins are configured, and how long after power-up it's first accessed. A peripheral that's now initialized earlier might be accessed before its supply has stabilized, or a bus might be driven before a pull-up has taken effect, or an interrupt might be enabled before its handler's dependencies are ready. I'd instrument the boot sequence with GPIO toggles or a logic analyzer on the relevant buses to see the actual timing, and compare it against each peripheral's datasheet requirements — power-up delay, clock stabilization, reset pulse width. I'd also check for ordering hazards like configuring an output pin before setting its level, which can produce a brief glitch. The goal is to find the specific timing or sequencing requirement the new order violates, then fix it explicitly rather than just reverting the order, because the underlying marginality may still be there.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you make the initialization sequence robust against future reordering?
- What would you do if the failure only reproduced on a fraction of units?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The fact that it's consistent and position-dependent points toward a mechanical or environmental coupling that only exists in the assembled state — something the bench setup doesn't reproduce. I'd first confirm the offset is real and repeatable by measuring at the same point in the signal chain in both configurations, so I'm comparing like with like. Then I'd think about what changes when the enclosure goes on and the unit is mounted: cable routing and strain, grounding and shielding paths, proximity to metal structures, thermal environment, and mechanical stress on the PCB or sensor. A common cause is a ground reference shift — when the board is fastened into a metal chassis or mounted on a conductive surface, the ground return path changes and a small potential difference appears between the analog ground reference and the ADC's ground, which shows up as an offset. Another is mechanical stress on a strain-sensitive component or on solder joints, which can shift a bridge sensor's output. I'd test these hypotheses by reproducing the assembled condition incrementally: mount the board in the enclosure without the cover, then with the cover, then in the final fixture, measuring the offset at each step. I'd also measure the ground potential difference between the analog reference and the ADC ground with a differential probe in the assembled state. If it's thermal, the offset would track warm-up; if it's mechanical, it would track mounting torque or position. Isolating which step introduces the offset narrows the mechanism quickly.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish a ground-shift offset from a genuine sensor offset?
- What design changes would you consider to make the front-end insensitive to mounting position?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is to argue about which is more likely and let the loudest voice win. Instead I'd reframe the question: what observation or test would give a different result depending on which hypothesis is true? That's the discriminating experiment, and finding it is the fastest way out. I'd sit down with the team and, for each hypothesis, write down the specific prediction it makes that the other does not — a signature in the data, a behavior under a particular condition, a part that would or wouldn't fail. Then I'd design the cheapest, fastest test that probes that difference, and run both hypotheses against it rather than trying to prove one right. If no single test discriminates, I'd run them in parallel with clear ownership and a time box, so we're not serializing the work. I'd also be explicit about what evidence would falsify each hypothesis, because people get attached to their theory and stop seeing contradicting data. Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative — the goal is to find the truth, not to be right — and I'd document the reasoning so the eventual conclusion is defensible. If the schedule forces a decision before the investigation closes, I'd be honest about the residual uncertainty and choose the mitigation that's safe under both hypotheses where possible.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you keep the team motivated if the discriminating test is inconclusive?
- When would you escalate the decision rather than continue investigating?

## Q5: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's power rail shows a periodic voltage dip — a few hundred millivolts lasting tens of microseconds — that occurs at a regular interval, and the device occasionally resets during the dips but not consistently?

**Answer:** The periodicity is the most useful clue, because a regular interval usually maps to a specific recurring event in the system — a switching regulator's cycle, a periodic firmware task, a communication beacon, or a sensor sampling burst. I'd start by correlating the dip's timing with everything else happening in the system, using a scope with multiple channels to capture the rail alongside the suspected aggressor's control signal or enable line. If the dip lines up with a known periodic event, I've likely found the load transient source. Then I'd measure the rail at the point of load — right at the victim's decoupling capacitor — rather than at the regulator output, because a dip that's visible at the load but not at the regulator points to impedance in the distribution path, while a dip at both points points to the regulator's transient response or current limit. I'd look at the regulator's loop response and whether the dip is a load-transient response, a current-limit hiccup, or a control-loop instability. The fact that resets are inconsistent is important: it suggests the dip only causes a reset when it coincides with a marginal condition elsewhere — the processor near its brownout threshold, or the dip deeper than usual on some units. I'd characterize the dip's depth and duration against the processor's brownout reset threshold and the regulator's specified transient response, and check whether the bulk and decoupling capacitance is adequate for the transient. I'd also verify the measurement itself — a scope probe with a long ground lead can show ringing that isn't real, so I'd use a proper low-inductance ground connection before trusting the waveform.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you tell whether the dip is caused by the load or by the regulator?
- What would you check to explain why only some units reset during the dip?
