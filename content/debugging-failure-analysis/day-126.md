# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 126

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and the error is only caught later when the data is reviewed offline?

**Answer:** The defining difficulty here is that nothing in the system's own validation layer is firing, so the investigation has to start by building observability that doesn't currently exist. I'd begin by characterizing the error statistically before touching any hardware: how often does it occur, is it correlated with a particular operating mode, temperature, time-in-service, or data value, and is the wrong value a single-bit flip, a stale sample, or a plausible-but-shifted reading? Those three hypotheses point in very different directions — a bit flip suggests a memory or bus integrity issue, a stale sample suggests a timing or buffer-management bug, and a shifted reading suggests an analog or calibration path problem.

Next I'd try to reproduce it under instrumentation rather than waiting for it to happen in the field. That means adding a checksum or sequence counter at each stage of the data path — at the ADC output, after the firmware's conversion, at the point of storage, and at the transmit boundary — so that when a bad value appears I can see exactly which stage introduced it. If the value is correct at the ADC and wrong after the firmware conversion, the problem is in software; if it's wrong at the ADC output, it's analog or timing. I'd also log the raw ADC samples alongside the processed value so I can replay the conversion offline.

A key discipline is to avoid the trap of assuming the error is where it's observed. A plausible-but-wrong value that passes range checks is often a data-integrity problem upstream — a buffer that wasn't fully written before being read, a DMA transfer that raced with a read, or a shared variable accessed without proper synchronization. I'd review the data path for concurrency hazards and check whether the error correlates with interrupt load or with other subsystems being active. If I can't reproduce it in the lab, I'd add persistent logging to the field units so the next occurrence captures the full context, and I'd treat the absence of a self-test flag as a gap to close — adding a plausibility or cross-check test that would catch this class of error going forward.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to add a runtime plausibility check versus fixing the underlying cause? And how would you distinguish a single-bit flip from a stale-sample bug using only the logged data?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a classic load-transient and power-distribution problem, and the first thing I'd do is separate the question of *where* the ripple originates from *where* it's being observed. I'd probe the rail at multiple points simultaneously — at the regulator output, at the bulk decoupling, and at the pins of the load — because a rail that looks clean at the regulator but noisy at the load is telling me the problem is in the distribution impedance between them, not in the regulator's control loop. I'd use a short-ground-tip probe or a proper coaxial probe to avoid picking up radiated noise on the probe itself, since a long ground lead will happily invent ripple that isn't there.

Next I'd characterize the transient: what's the amplitude, duration, and repetition rate, and does it align in time with the subsystem's switching edges? If the ripple is synchronized to the switching event, I'd look at the load's current draw profile — a high-current subsystem turning on can demand a fast current step that the bulk capacitance can't supply, causing a voltage droop until the regulator loop responds. That points to insufficient bulk or decoupling capacitance, excessive ESR, or a layout with too much inductance between the cap and the load. I'd also check whether the ripple is differential (across the rail and its return) or common-mode (appearing on both), since common-mode ripple usually means a grounding or return-path problem rather than a decoupling problem.

I'd then test hypotheses one at a time: add temporary bulk capacitance close to the load and see if the ripple changes; check the regulator's loop stability and phase margin, since a marginally stable loop can ring under fast transients; and inspect the return path for shared impedance between the switching subsystem and the sensitive load. Throughout, I'd keep the measurement setup identical between tests so I'm comparing like with like, and I'd document the before/after waveform for each change rather than relying on memory.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a decoupling problem and a regulator loop-stability problem from the waveform shape alone? And what would you do if adding capacitance reduced the ripple but didn't eliminate it?

## Q3: A medical device passes all functional and safety tests at design verification, but during a 30-unit pilot production run, three units fail a 72-hour burn-in test with the same symptom: the device stops communicating after roughly 48 hours and requires a power cycle to recover. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes design verification but fails in a pilot run tells me the failure is either build-related (a process or component variation that the DV units didn't have) or time/temperature-related (something that only manifests after extended operation). The 48-hour mark and the consistency across three units is the most valuable clue — it suggests a cumulative mechanism rather than a random defect, so I'd resist the temptation to treat the three units as independent failures and instead look for what they share.

I'd start by preserving the failed state: rather than power-cycling immediately, I'd capture whatever diagnostic information is available — current draw, rail voltages, temperature, any debug logs, and whether the communication peripheral is still alive at the register level. If the device has a debug port, I'd attach and inspect the state of the communication controller and the firmware's task state before resetting. That single decision often separates a firmware hang from a hardware lockup.

In parallel I'd compare the three failed units against the passing units on the dimensions that differ between DV and pilot: component lots, assembly house, firmware version, and test setup. I'd also look at whether the failure correlates with a specific build date or component supplier. Then I'd try to accelerate the failure — running units at elevated temperature or with a stressed communication load — to see whether the 48-hour mark shortens, which would confirm a cumulative mechanism and give me a faster reproduction loop. If the communication peripheral is a bus device, I'd specifically check for a bus lockup condition, a buffer that fills over time, or a counter that overflows after a predictable number of transactions. The 48-hour figure is suspiciously close to a round number of transactions or a timer rollover, so I'd do the arithmetic on the firmware's counters and timeouts before assuming a hardware cause.

**Possible follow-ups:** If you can't reproduce the failure on the bench, how would you instrument the pilot units to capture the failure state without affecting the test? And how would you decide whether to halt the pilot run while the investigation proceeds?

## Q4: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The deadlock usually isn't a technical problem so much as an evidence problem — each team is reasoning from its own domain and neither has data that the other accepts as decisive. My first move is to reframe the question away from "whose fault is it" toward "what single measurement would discriminate between the two hypotheses," and then get that measurement made jointly. I'd convene both teams and ask each to state, explicitly, what evidence would change their mind — that converts a positional argument into a testable set of predictions.

Then I'd design a discriminating experiment. For a communication failure, that typically means instrumenting the physical layer and the firmware simultaneously: a logic analyzer or scope capture on the bus lines, time-correlated with firmware trace points. If the bus shows a valid transaction that the firmware fails to process, the problem is on the firmware side; if the bus shows a malformed or missing transaction, it's hardware or timing. The key is that both teams agree in advance on how the capture will be interpreted, so the result settles the question rather than being argued about afterward.

I'd also look for the conditions under which the failure occurs and make sure both teams are testing under the same conditions — a frequent cause of these deadlocks is that hardware is testing on the bench and firmware is testing in a different configuration, so they're literally not observing the same failure. I'd standardize the test setup and the definition of "failure" so both teams are counting the same events. Finally, I'd keep the tone collaborative: the goal is to find the root cause, not to assign blame, and I'd make that explicit so neither team feels the investigation is a contest. If the discriminating experiment is inconclusive, I'd escalate to a joint review of the interface contract — the timing requirements, the bus specification, and the firmware's assumptions — because disagreements often trace back to an underspecified interface that both sides interpreted differently.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating experiment shows the failure is genuinely a interaction between both domains rather than one side? And how would you prevent this kind of deadlock on future projects?

## Q5: A junior engineer on your team has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd address is the methodology, not the technical problem. Testing components in isolation and finding everything passes is a sign that the engineer is validating parts rather than reproducing the failure — and an intermittent system-level failure usually lives in the interactions between parts, not in any single part. I'd sit down with them and ask a few structured questions: Can you reproduce it on demand? What's the failure rate? What conditions were present when it last occurred? If the answer to the first is no, the priority is to build a reliable reproduction before doing any more component testing, because without a reproduction every test is a coin flip.

I'd help them shift from "test the parts" to "characterize the failure": capture the conditions — temperature, supply, load, timing, sequence of operations — and try to narrow the envelope until the failure becomes reproducible. I'd also suggest instrumenting the system so the failure captures its own context, rather than trying to catch it by watching. And I'd encourage them to write down their hypotheses explicitly and design a test that discriminates between them, rather than trying changes and seeing what happens — the latter is what leads to days of unfocused effort.

On the human side, I'd normalize the frustration: intermittent failures are genuinely hard, and several days without progress is not a sign of incompetence. I'd pair with them for a session rather than taking over, so they build the skill instead of feeling sidelined, and I'd help them break the problem into smaller questions so they get some forward motion. Given the schedule pressure, I'd also make a explicit call about when to escalate or bring in a second pair of eyes, and I'd communicate status upward honestly rather than letting the pressure silently distort the investigation. The goal is to protect both the engineer's confidence and the integrity of the root-cause process.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer resisted the change in approach because they were sure their component-level testing was close to a breakthrough? And at what point would you reassign the investigation versus continuing to coach?
