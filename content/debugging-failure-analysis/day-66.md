# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 66

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a device that only fails when it is powered from its internal battery, but passes every test when powered from a bench supply at the same nominal voltage?
**Answer:** The first principle is that "same nominal voltage" is not the same electrical source, so I'd treat the supply itself as the variable under test rather than assuming the device is at fault. A bench supply is typically a low-impedance, well-regulated source with a short, thick cable and a large output capacitor; a battery has finite internal resistance that rises with age and cold temperature, a different transient response, and a different return path. I'd instrument both cases identically — a scope probe at the device's input connector and at the far end of the on-board rail, plus a current probe or shunt to capture load transients — and compare the two under the same operating sequence. The key measurements are the voltage at the point of load during current steps (radio transmit bursts, motor or pump switching, flash writes), the depth and duration of any droop, and whether the device's brown-out or reset threshold is being grazed. I'd also check the battery path specifically: connector and contact resistance, protection FET or fuel-gauge sense resistor voltage drop, and whether the battery's own protection circuit is momentarily cutting out under inrush. If the failure follows the source rather than the board, that points to source impedance and decoupling; if it follows the board regardless of source, that points to a marginal threshold or a firmware assumption about supply behavior. I'd confirm by adding bulk capacitance at the point of load as a diagnostic (not a fix) — if the symptom moves, I've localized it to supply impedance and transient response.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a battery internal-resistance problem from a board-level decoupling problem if both produce a similar droop? What would you check in the battery's protection or fuel-gauge circuitry before concluding the cell itself is degraded?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?
**Answer:** This is a load-transient and power-integrity problem, so I'd start by characterizing it properly rather than jumping to a fix. I'd measure the rail at the point of load — right at the decoupling capacitors and the IC's supply pins — not at the regulator output, because the two can look very different once you include trace and via impedance. I'd use a short-ground-spring probe or a properly compensated differential probe to avoid picking up radiated switching noise as if it were real ripple, and I'd trigger the scope on the subsystem's switching edge so the transient is captured repeatably. Then I'd separate the contributors: is the ripple the regulator's loop response to a fast load step (insufficient phase margin, too little output capacitance, or excessive ESR), or is it IR/inductive drop across the power distribution network between the regulator and the load (trace width, via count, plane splits), or is it ground bounce on the return path? A useful discriminator is to measure the regulator output and the point-of-load voltage simultaneously — if the regulator output is clean and the load sees ripple, the PDN is the problem; if the regulator output itself is moving, it's loop response or bulk capacitance. I'd also check whether the switching subsystem is injecting noise conductively through a shared ground or supply node, which is common when a high-current path shares a return with sensitive analog. Only after localizing the mechanism would I consider remedies, and I'd verify each change with the same triggered measurement.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between insufficient bulk capacitance and a regulator loop-stability issue from the transient waveform shape? What role does the return-path layout play, and how would you probe for ground bounce without introducing it yourself?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?
**Answer:** The hard part here is that the failure is silent and the evidence is stale, so the first job is to make the failure observable and reproducible before theorizing about cause. I'd start by defining what "plausible but wrong" means physically — is it a small offset, a scaling error, a stale sample, a value from the wrong channel, or a value that's correct but attributed to the wrong timestamp? That distinction narrows the search enormously. Then I'd build a way to capture the raw data path end to end: the sensor output at the analog front end, the ADC conversion result, the value as stored in memory, and the value as transmitted or logged, ideally with a common timestamp or trigger so I can align them. If the error is intermittent, I'd run a long-duration capture with a reference input or a known-good stimulus so any deviation is immediately visible, and I'd log both the raw and processed values so I can tell whether the corruption happens in acquisition, in processing, or in transport. I'd also look at whether the error correlates with anything — temperature, time since power-up, a particular operating mode, a concurrent subsystem activity — because a plausible-but-wrong value often comes from a race, a buffer being read while it's being written, or a shared resource being accessed without proper protection. Finally, I'd check the self-test itself: if the self-test passes on a wrong value, the self-test's coverage is part of the problem, and I'd want to understand what it actually validates versus what it assumes.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a self-test or plausibility check that would have caught this class of error earlier? If the corruption turns out to be a race condition, how would you confirm that without being able to reproduce it on demand?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?
**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is to keep arguing about which is more likely; the productive move is to find the discriminating test — the observation that would be true under one hypothesis and false under the other. So I'd reframe the discussion away from "which do you believe" toward "what would we expect to see if each were true, and what's the cheapest experiment that separates them?" I'd write both hypotheses down explicitly with their predicted signatures, then design a test, a measurement, or a targeted data pull that distinguishes them, even if it means instrumenting a unit or running a controlled experiment rather than continuing to reason from existing evidence. If the two causes could be interacting rather than mutually exclusive, I'd say so and test for the interaction rather than forcing a binary choice. On the team side, I'd keep the tone collaborative — the goal is to converge on evidence, not to win — and I'd make sure the person advocating the losing hypothesis feels their reasoning was taken seriously, because the same rigor will be needed on the next problem. If a decision has to be made before the discriminating test is feasible, I'd be explicit about the residual uncertainty, choose the option that's reversible or that addresses both causes if possible, and document what would trigger a revisit.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or slow and the schedule won't allow it? How do you keep a team from treating a hypothesis as a personal position rather than a working theory?

## Q5: A junior engineer has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?
**Answer:** The pattern here — components pass in isolation, the system fails — usually means the investigation is testing the wrong thing: the failure depends on the interaction between parts, or on a condition that the isolated test doesn't reproduce. So my first move would be to sit with the engineer and reframe the problem rather than add more effort to the same approach. I'd ask what the failure actually looks like at the system level, how it's being detected, and whether it can be reproduced on demand or only occasionally — because if it can't be reproduced reliably, no amount of component testing will converge. I'd help them shift from "test each part until one fails" to "characterize the failure and find the variable that controls it": change one condition at a time (load, temperature, timing, a concurrent operation, a specific cable or accessory) and see what makes the failure appear or disappear. I'd also encourage them to instrument the system rather than the components — capture the signals at the boundary where the failure manifests, with a trigger, so the intermittent event is caught rather than chased. On the human side, I'd acknowledge that several days without progress is genuinely frustrating, normalize it as a normal part of hard intermittent problems, and make it clear that asking for help earlier is a strength, not a failure. I'd stay involved enough to keep them moving but let them own the investigation, and I'd check in on the schedule pressure honestly so they're not carrying it alone.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you help them decide when to stop an unproductive line of investigation and switch approaches? What would you do if the failure still can't be reproduced after reframing, and the schedule pressure keeps building?
