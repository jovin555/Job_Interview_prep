# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 94

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The key insight is that the offset appears only when two variables change together — mechanical assembly and final mounting — so I'd first separate those two variables rather than treating "assembled and installed" as one condition. I'd build a test matrix: bench with harness, bench with enclosure but not mounted, enclosure plus mounting in the final orientation, and finally the full installed configuration. That isolates whether the offset comes from the enclosure (cable routing, connector seating, shielding contact, thermal environment) or from the mounting position (mechanical stress on the PCB, changed ground reference, proximity to a metal surface).

Once I know which transition introduces the offset, I'd measure at the source: probe the analog front-end output and the ADC input directly in the failing configuration, not just read the firmware value. A consistent offset that appears with assembly often traces to a ground reference shift — a mounting screw or chassis contact changing the return path, or a shield now tied to a different point — or to mechanical stress on a connector or flex altering contact resistance. If the offset is stable and repeatable, that points to a DC path issue (ground, connector, or bias network) rather than noise. I'd also check whether the offset scales with gain or is fixed, since a fixed offset suggests a bias/reference problem while a gain-proportional error suggests a feedback network being disturbed. Throughout, I'd keep one change at a time and log the configuration for each measurement so the result is traceable.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset disappears when you loosen the mounting screws, what does that tell you and where would you look next? How would you distinguish a ground-reference shift from a genuine sensor bias error in this setup?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Load-dependent ripple points at the power delivery network's response to a transient, so I'd start by characterizing the disturbance rather than the symptom. I'd measure the rail at the load's own decoupling capacitors with a short-ground-tip probe — not at the regulator output and not with a long ground lead, since both will lie to you about high-frequency content. I'd capture the switching edge and look at the ripple's frequency content: is it at the subsystem's switching frequency, at a harmonic, or is it a damped transient from the load step itself?

Then I'd separate the two mechanisms. If the ripple is a load-transient response, the question is whether the regulator's loop is stable and fast enough and whether there's enough bulk and high-frequency decoupling close to the load — I'd check the transient response with a controlled electronic load step and compare against the regulator's expected behavior. If the ripple is switching noise coupling in, I'd look at the layout: the high-di/dt loop area of the subsystem, shared return paths between the subsystem and the analog rail, and whether the decoupling capacitors are actually effective at the frequencies involved (their self-resonance matters). Ground bounce on a shared return is a common culprit — probing across the ground plane between the subsystem and the sensitive load will reveal it.

I'd verify each hypothesis with a measurement rather than a guess: add temporary bulk capacitance at the load to see if the transient shrinks, or temporarily reroute the return to see if the noise drops. One change at a time, measured at the same probe point, so the comparison is valid.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell whether the ripple is coming from the regulator's control loop versus from the load's own switching current? What role does decoupling capacitor placement and self-resonance play here, and how would you verify it empirically?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** This is one of the harder classes of bug because there's no fault flag to anchor on — the system believes the value is valid. My first move is to make the failure observable: add instrumentation that captures the raw ADC samples, the timestamp, the conversion path, and the exact bytes transmitted, so I can compare what the sensor produced against what the firmware reported and what left the device. Without that chain, I'm guessing.

Then I'd look for the mechanism that can produce a plausible-but-wrong value. Candidates include a stale sample being used because a read completed late or a buffer wasn't refreshed, a scaling or calibration constant applied incorrectly under some condition, a race between the sampling interrupt and the consumer reading the shared buffer, or a data-integrity issue on the bus that happens to land on a valid-looking value. The fact that self-tests pass tells me the sensor and its basic interface are healthy, so I'd focus on the path between conversion and reporting — timing, buffering, and concurrency.

I'd try to reproduce it under controlled stress: increase sampling rate, add bus traffic, vary temperature, and run long enough to catch the event with the instrumentation in place. If I can correlate the bad value with a specific event — a late interrupt, a buffer overwrite, a retry — I have a root cause. I'd also check whether the error is a single-bit flip or a whole-value substitution, since that distinguishes a data-integrity problem from a logic/timing problem. The corrective action needs to be verifiable, so I'd want a test that fails before the fix and passes after.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the instrumentation so it doesn't itself perturb the timing you're trying to observe? If the bad value correlates with a specific interrupt latency, how would you confirm that's causal rather than coincidental?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is to let the team argue about which is more likely. I'd reframe the discussion around discriminating evidence: for each hypothesis, what observation would be true if it were the cause and false if it weren't? Then I'd design the cheapest, fastest test that separates them — ideally one that gives a clear yes/no rather than more ambiguous data. That converts a debate into an experiment.

I'd also make the decision criteria explicit up front: what evidence threshold would let us close each hypothesis, and what's the cost of being wrong in each direction. If one hypothesis, if true, implies a safety or regulatory concern, it gets priority regardless of which the team finds more elegant. I'd assign owners to run the discriminating tests in parallel where possible, set a time-box, and agree in advance what we do if the tests are inconclusive — escalate, widen the sample, or bring in outside expertise.

Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative rather than adjudicating a winner. The goal isn't for one camp to be right; it's for the evidence to decide. I'd document the reasoning and the decision so the conclusion is defensible in a design review or a regulatory audit, and so the team can see why the chosen path was taken even if their hypothesis wasn't the one confirmed.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is inconclusive and the schedule won't allow more investigation? How do you keep the team that "lost" the argument engaged and bought into the final corrective action?

## Q5: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** A reversible, temperature-correlated offset is a strong clue that the mechanism is thermal, so my first job is to confirm and localize it rather than assume. I'd run the device in a controlled thermal environment — an oven or chamber if available, otherwise instrumented continuous operation — and log the offset against measured temperatures at several points: the sensor, the amplifier, the reference, and the ADC. Correlating the offset curve with a specific component's temperature tells me where to focus.

Then I'd separate the possible causes. A growing offset that tracks temperature could come from a component's own thermal drift (an amplifier's input offset voltage drift, a reference's temperature coefficient, a resistor network's mismatch), from thermoelectric effects at dissimilar-metal junctions or connectors, or from mechanical/thermal stress changing a contact or a solder joint's resistance. I'd check the datasheets against the observed magnitude — if the drift is larger than the sum of the components' specified tempcos, the cause is probably not intrinsic drift but something like a thermal gradient across the board or a stressed connection.

I'd verify by targeted experiment: cool or heat individual components with a localized source while watching the offset, or swap a suspect component for a low-drift alternative to see if the behavior changes. If it's intrinsic drift, the fix is component selection or compensation; if it's thermal EMF or mechanical stress, the fix is layout, mounting, or thermal design. I'd also confirm the offset is truly zero at cool-down and not just below the measurement resolution, since that affects whether it's a reversible thermal effect or something with hysteresis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish intrinsic component tempco from a thermal gradient or thermoelectric effect across the board? If the drift is within each component's datasheet but the system still fails, where does the problem actually lie and how would you address it?
