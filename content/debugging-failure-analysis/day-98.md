# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 98

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The key observation is that the offset appears only when two variables change together — the mechanical assembly and the mounting position — so the first job is to separate them. I'd build a test matrix: open bench with harness, open bench with the real patient cable, board inside the enclosure but not mounted, enclosure mounted in the final orientation, and finally fully installed. That isolates whether the offset comes from the enclosure itself (shielding, grounding, cable routing, proximity to a metal chassis), from the mounting position (mechanical stress on the board or connectors, orientation-dependent thermal gradients, or a changed ground reference), or from the interaction of both.

Mechanically, I'd look for board flex and connector strain — a mounting screw torquing the PCB can shift a solder joint or a connector contact resistance, which shows up as a small DC offset rather than an outright failure. Electrically, I'd check whether the enclosure changes the ground topology: a chassis tied to analog ground at one point versus floating, or a shield that now provides a return path that didn't exist on the bench. I'd measure the offset at the ADC input with a scope in each configuration, not just read the firmware value, so I know whether the error is introduced before or after the ADC. If the offset is stable and repeatable per unit, that points to a systematic design or assembly effect; if it varies unit to unit, I'd suspect tolerance stack-up or connector contact resistance. Throughout, I'd change one variable at a time and log the offset magnitude for each configuration so the conclusion is evidence-based rather than a guess.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset disappears when you loosen the mounting screws, what does that tell you and what would you check next? How would you distinguish a grounding-related offset from a mechanical stress effect using only electrical measurements?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Load-dependent ripple almost always comes down to the interaction between the load transient and the power delivery network, so I'd characterize it in the time and frequency domains rather than just reading an RMS number off a meter. First, I'd probe the rail directly across the decoupling capacitor closest to the load with a short ground lead — a long scope ground lead will pick up its own loop inductance and show ripple that isn't really there. I'd capture the switching event itself: the edge rate of the load current, the magnitude of the transient, and the rail's response — how far it dips, how fast it recovers, and whether it rings.

Then I'd work through the likely causes systematically. If the dip is large and slow to recover, the bulk capacitance or the regulator's loop bandwidth may be insufficient for that transient. If there's high-frequency ringing on the edges, I'd look at the decoupling network's impedance at those frequencies — capacitor ESL, placement, and via inductance — and whether the load's return current path is well-controlled. If the ripple is periodic and synchronized with the switching itself rather than the transient edges, I'd suspect ground bounce or a shared impedance between the load's return path and the regulator's reference. I'd also check whether the regulator is entering a mode change or current limit during the transient. The fix depends on which mechanism it is: more bulk or better-placed high-frequency decoupling, layout changes to shorten the return path, adjusting the regulator's compensation, or slowing the load's edge rate if the application allows. I'd verify each change with the same measurement setup so I'm comparing like with like.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between insufficient decoupling and a regulator loop-stability problem from the scope waveform alone? What measurement mistakes most commonly make ripple look worse than it is?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** This is one of the harder classes of bug because every layer of defense — range checks, self-tests, plausibility limits — passes, so the error is hiding inside the "valid" envelope. My first step is to make the failure observable: add timestamped logging of the raw ADC or bus data alongside the processed value, plus the configuration and state at the moment of the read, so that when a bad value is eventually caught offline I can reconstruct what the device was doing. Without that, I'm investigating a memory of an event rather than the event.

Next I'd narrow where the corruption enters the chain. Is the raw sensor data already wrong at the bus or ADC, or is the raw data correct and the processing wrong? I'd compare the logged raw value against the reported value for the same sample — if they diverge, the bug is in scaling, calibration lookup, unit conversion, or a buffer/indexing error in firmware. If the raw value itself is wrong but plausible, I'd look at sampling timing: reading the sensor during a conversion, reading a stale register, a race between an interrupt-driven update and the main-loop read, or a shared buffer being overwritten mid-use. Plausible-but-wrong values also commonly come from off-by-one indexing, sign-extension errors, or a calibration coefficient applied twice or not at all under a specific code path.

I'd try to reproduce it under accelerated conditions — higher sample rate, more channel switching, induced bus traffic — and use fault injection where possible, such as deliberately corrupting a buffer or delaying an interrupt, to confirm the mechanism. Once I have a candidate mechanism, I'd write a targeted test that fails before the fix and passes after, so the corrective action is verifiable rather than assumed.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the raw logged value matches the reported value but both are wrong, where would you look next? How would you design the logging so it doesn't itself change the timing behavior you're trying to catch?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is letting the team argue about which is more likely instead of designing the experiment that separates them. My first move is to reframe: instead of "which cause is right," ask "what observation would be true under one hypothesis and false under the other?" That converts a debate into a test. I'd write both hypotheses down explicitly with their predicted signatures — what each one says we should see in the data, in which conditions, and at what rate — and then look for a discriminating measurement we haven't made yet.

If a single decisive test isn't feasible, I'd run both lines in parallel with clear owners and a time box, and I'd prioritize whichever hypothesis is cheaper to falsify. I'd also make sure the team isn't anchored: sometimes the split reflects that each camp has been looking at different evidence, and putting all the data on one wall reveals that the two hypotheses aren't actually mutually exclusive — there may be a common upstream cause producing both symptoms. I'd keep the investigation honest by requiring that any proposed fix be tied to evidence that confirms the mechanism, not just correlation, especially in a regulated device where the corrective action has to be traceable to a verified root cause. And I'd communicate a decision timeline to stakeholders so the pressure to "just pick one" is managed with a plan rather than a guess.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or takes weeks, and the schedule won't allow it? How do you keep the losing hypothesis's owner engaged rather than demoralized?

## Q5: A junior engineer has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The pattern here — everything passes in isolation, the system still fails — usually means the bug lives in an interaction, and isolation testing by definition can't find it. So my first job is to help the engineer change the question, not to take the debugging away from them. I'd sit down with them and ask them to walk me through what they've ruled out and, more importantly, how they ruled it out, because "the component tests fine" often means "it tests fine under conditions that don't match the failing system."

I'd steer toward reproducing the failure reliably first — an intermittent bug you can't reproduce is nearly impossible to fix, so capturing it, even at a low rate, is the priority. Then I'd help them build a system-level observation plan: instrument the actual failing configuration, log the state of the interacting subsystems around the failure, and use divide-and-conquer at the system boundary rather than the component boundary — swap subsystems, bisect the signal chain, change one environmental or timing variable at a time. I'd also check whether they've fallen into a loop of re-running the same test expecting a different result, which is a common trap when someone is frustrated.

On the people side, I'd be careful to preserve their ownership and confidence — frame it as "let's look at this together" rather than "you've been doing it wrong." I'd set a short checkpoint, maybe a day, to review what the new instrumentation shows, and make sure they know it's fine to escalate early. Frustration plus schedule pressure is exactly when someone stops thinking systematically, so the most useful thing I can do is restore structure and a sense of forward progress.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if, after a day of joint debugging, the engineer still resists changing their approach? What would you do differently if the failure rate were so low that reproduction in a reasonable time wasn't possible?
