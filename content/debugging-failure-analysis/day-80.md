# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 80

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?
**Answer:** The key observation is that the offset appears only when two variables change together — the enclosure is closed and the unit is in its final mounting position — so the first step is to separate those two variables rather than treat "assembled and installed" as one condition. I'd build a test matrix: bench with harness, board in enclosure but not mounted, board mounted but enclosure open, and fully assembled and mounted. If the offset appears at the "enclosure closed" step, the cause is likely mechanical or thermal — a cable or connector being pinched, a shield or bracket touching a component, or the board flexing against a standoff and changing a strain-sensitive component's value. If it appears only at the "mounted" step, I'd suspect a ground-path or common-mode issue: the mounting position may couple the unit to a chassis or surface that introduces a ground loop or a different reference potential, which shows up as a DC offset in a high-impedance front-end. I'd measure the front-end's input and output with a high-impedance probe in each configuration, and also measure the board's local ground reference relative to the system ground, because a small ground potential difference is a common source of exactly this kind of consistent offset. I'd also check whether the offset tracks with temperature (enclosure traps heat) or with mechanical stress (flexing changes resistor or capacitor values slightly). The fix depends on the mechanism: strain relief or a mechanical redesign for flex, a better ground/reference scheme for the ground-loop case, or thermal compensation if it's temperature-driven. Throughout, I'd keep one change at a time so I can attribute the effect correctly.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a thermal offset from a mechanical-stress offset if both are plausible? What would you do if the offset is within the component's tolerance but still unacceptable to the system algorithm?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?
**Answer:** This is a load-transient and power-integrity problem, so I'd start by characterizing the transient rather than the steady state. I'd probe the rail directly at the load's decoupling capacitors with a short ground lead — a long ground clip on a scope probe adds inductance and can make ripple look worse than it is, or hide the real waveform. I'd capture the switching event with enough bandwidth to see the edge, and measure three things: the magnitude of the voltage dip or spike, its duration, and how quickly the rail recovers. That tells me whether the regulator's loop is too slow, whether there's insufficient bulk capacitance, or whether the decoupling network has an impedance peak at the switching frequency. I'd also check the return path — a high-current switch can cause ground bounce if the return current shares an impedance with the analog or logic ground, which shows up as ripple that isn't really on the rail itself. Next I'd look at the layout: loop area of the switching current, placement of the bulk cap relative to the load, and whether the regulator's feedback trace is picking up switching noise. If the transient is faster than the regulator can respond, the fix is usually local — more or better-placed bulk capacitance, a lower-ESR capacitor, or a small series ferrite to isolate the noisy load. If the regulator itself is oscillating under load, that's a compensation or stability issue. I'd verify each change with the same measurement setup so I'm comparing like with like.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell whether the ripple is coming from the regulator or being coupled in from the load's return current? What role does the feedback trace layout play in this kind of problem?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?
**Answer:** The hardest part of this class of bug is that nothing in the system flags it, so the first job is to make the failure observable. I'd add temporary instrumentation that logs the raw sensor data alongside the processed value, with timestamps and a sequence counter, so I can see whether the corruption happens at the sensor, in the transfer, or in the processing. A plausible-but-wrong value usually points to one of a few mechanisms: a stale read (the firmware reads a buffer before the new conversion completes), a data-transfer error that flips bits in a way that stays in range (a single-bit error in a multi-byte value often does), a race between an interrupt updating the value and the main loop reading it, or a units/scaling error that only manifests under certain conditions. I'd check the timing first — does the error correlate with a specific event, like a conversion starting while a read is in progress, or a communication frame arriving during a critical section? I'd also look at whether the value is consistent with a bit flip by comparing the wrong value to the expected one in binary; a single-bit difference is a strong clue toward a transfer or memory-integrity issue. If it's a race, the fix is usually a proper atomic read or a double-buffer with a validity flag. If it's a transfer error, I'd look at the bus timing, pull-ups, and error-checking. The important discipline is to reproduce it under controlled conditions — inject the suspected condition deliberately — rather than wait for it to happen again.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the logging so it doesn't itself perturb the timing you're trying to observe? If you suspect a single-bit flip, what would you check in the memory or bus design?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?
**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is to keep arguing about which is more likely instead of designing a test that distinguishes them. My first move is to reframe the question: what observation would be true under one hypothesis and false under the other? That turns a debate into an experiment. I'd list the discriminating evidence for each — not the supporting evidence, but the evidence that would rule one out — and then design the cheapest, fastest test that produces that evidence. Sometimes that's a targeted measurement; sometimes it's a controlled reproduction where I force one condition and hold the other constant. I'd also be explicit with the team that we're not choosing a favorite, we're buying information, and that a test which eliminates one hypothesis is a success even if it doesn't confirm the other. If the two causes could be interacting — which is common — I'd test them in combination rather than assuming they're mutually exclusive. Throughout, I'd keep a written record of what each test showed and what it ruled out, so the conclusion is traceable to evidence rather than to who argued hardest. If the schedule forces a decision before the test completes, I'd be honest about the residual uncertainty and propose a containment or monitoring action rather than claiming a root cause I haven't demonstrated.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the discriminating test is expensive or slow to run? What do you do if the evidence points to a cause that's outside your team's area of ownership?

## Q5: A junior engineer has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?
**Answer:** The pattern here — components pass in isolation, the system fails — usually means the bug lives in an interaction, not in a component, so the debugging strategy itself needs to change rather than the effort increase. I'd start by sitting with the engineer and asking them to walk me through what they've tried and what they've learned, not to audit them but to understand where the investigation is stuck. Often the issue is that they've been testing the wrong level of the system: isolating a component removes the very conditions that cause the failure. I'd help them reframe toward reproducing the failure reliably first, because an intermittent bug you can't reproduce is very hard to fix — even a crude reproduction (forcing the suspected condition, running longer, stressing the system) is worth more than another round of isolated tests. Then I'd help them form a small number of specific hypotheses and design a test for each, rather than continuing to probe broadly. I'd also normalize the frustration: intermittent system-level bugs are genuinely hard, and several days without progress is not a sign of failure, it's a sign the approach needs a reset. Practically, I might pair with them for a session, or suggest they write down the timeline of what they know and what they don't — that often reveals the gap. The goal is to get them unstuck and back to making progress, while keeping the schedule pressure from turning into panic.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when to step in and take over versus coach them through it? What would you do if the reproduction itself is too expensive to run frequently?
