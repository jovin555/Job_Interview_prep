# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 128

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** When the faulting instruction is a memory copy and the addresses look valid, the first instinct is to trust the stack trace too literally. A hard fault on a copy operation with valid pointers usually means the fault isn't really about the copy itself — it's about the *state* the copy ran in. I'd work through several hypotheses systematically.

First, I'd question whether the captured stack trace is trustworthy. If the fault handler runs on a corrupted stack, or if the fault occurred during an interrupt that preempted the copy, the reported PC and stack frame may be misleading. I'd check whether the fault is a bus fault, memory management fault, or usage fault, and read the fault status registers to see exactly what the processor objected to — an unaligned access, a permission violation, or a bus error on a region that *looks* valid in the debugger but isn't actually mapped or is protected.

Second, I'd consider concurrency. A copy that's valid in isolation can fault if another context — an ISR, a DMA engine, or a second task — is modifying the memory map, remapping a region, or invalidating a cache line mid-copy. If the device uses an MPU or an MMU, a region that's valid at the start of the copy could become inaccessible partway through. I'd look at whether the fault correlates with specific interrupt activity or DMA transfers.

Third, I'd verify the addresses are valid *at the moment of the fault*, not just when the debugger inspects them afterward. A pointer that was valid when the copy started could point to freed or reallocated memory by the time the copy executes — a classic use-after-free or buffer-reuse bug that only manifests under specific timing. I'd add instrumentation to log the actual source/destination/size at the point of the copy, and compare against the fault registers.

Finally, I'd try to make the failure reproducible: run under a debugger with a breakpoint on the fault, enable fault logging to a persistent buffer, and stress the system to increase the fault rate. Once I can catch it live, the fault status registers and the live memory map usually tell the real story.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a genuine bus fault and a stack-corruption-induced false trace? What would you log in the fault handler to make the next occurrence diagnosable without a debugger attached?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and power-distribution problem, so I'd separate the question into "is the regulator responding poorly?" versus "is the ripple being coupled or measured incorrectly?"

First, I'd characterize the ripple properly. Where am I probing? Ripple measured at the bulk input capacitor looks very different from ripple at the load's decoupling capacitors, and a long ground lead on the probe will pick up switching noise that isn't really on the rail. I'd use a short-ground-tip probe, probe differentially where possible, and measure at several points: the regulator output, the bulk cap, and right at the load's local decoupling. That tells me whether the ripple is generated at the source or coupled along the distribution path.

Second, I'd look at the transient itself. When the high-current subsystem switches on, it presents a fast current step. The regulator's loop bandwidth and output capacitance determine how much the rail sags before the loop responds. I'd capture the transient with a fast timebase and look at the shape: a sharp dip that recovers quickly suggests insufficient local decoupling or high ESR; a sustained oscillation suggests a loop-stability problem; a dip that never fully recovers suggests the regulator is current-limiting or the bulk capacitance is inadequate.

Third, I'd check the distribution impedance. High di/dt through PCB traces and vias creates IR drop and inductive spikes. If the subsystem is far from the regulator, the trace inductance between them matters. I'd look at whether adding local bulk capacitance at the load reduces the ripple — if it does, the problem is distribution, not the regulator.

Fourth, I'd consider whether the switching subsystem is injecting noise back onto the rail through a shared ground return. Ground bounce from a high-current switch can appear as ripple everywhere the ground reference is shared. I'd probe ground at multiple points to see if there's a ground-potential difference during switching.

Only after ruling out measurement artifacts and distribution issues would I start questioning the regulator design itself — compensation, output cap selection, or current-limit behavior.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a loop-stability problem from a decoupling problem using only scope captures? What's your approach to choosing where to add bulk versus ceramic capacitance?

## Q3: A medical device has been in production for two years with a low field failure rate. In the last three months, the failure rate has increased fivefold, and the returned units all show the same symptom: a specific voltage regulator output is out of specification (reading low). The regulator and surrounding components are unchanged in the BOM. How would you approach this emerging failure trend?

**Answer:** A sudden trend change with an unchanged BOM points away from design and toward something that changed in manufacturing, supply, or use environment. I'd treat this as a trend investigation, not a single-unit debug.

First, I'd establish the timeline precisely. When did the failures start, and what else changed around that time? I'd pull the manufacturing records — component lot changes, supplier changes, process changes, test-equipment changes, even facility changes — and correlate them against the failure onset. A fivefold increase over three months usually has a traceable trigger.

Second, I'd look at the failed units themselves. "Regulator output low" is a symptom, not a cause. I'd do failure analysis on the regulator and its passives: is the regulator damaged, or is it being asked to deliver more current than it can? Are the passives — especially capacitors — degraded or out of tolerance? A capacitor that has drifted low in capacitance or high in ESR can cause exactly this symptom, and a bad capacitor lot is a classic cause of a delayed failure trend.

Third, I'd check incoming inspection and lot traceability. If the failures cluster by component lot or by production date, that's a strong signal. I'd compare the failing units' lot codes against the passing population.

Fourth, I'd consider whether the *use* environment changed — a new accessory, a firmware update that changed load behavior, a change in how customers deploy the device. A regulator that was marginal under the old load profile could fail under a new one even though nothing in the BOM changed.

Fifth, I'd set up a controlled comparison: take known-good units from before the trend and units from after, and run them through the same accelerated stress to see if the newer units fail earlier. That isolates whether the change is in the product or the environment.

The output of this is a corrective action tied to the actual trigger — a supplier containment, a lot recall, a process fix — with verification that the failure rate returns to baseline.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the lot-traceability analysis if the failing units span multiple production dates? What would you do if the failure analysis on the regulator shows no damage at all?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** A temperature-dependent, reversible offset points to a thermal coefficient problem somewhere in the signal chain, and the reversibility is actually helpful — it means I can reproduce it on demand by cycling temperature.

First, I'd characterize the drift precisely. How much offset, over what temperature range, and does it track temperature linearly or nonlinearly? I'd log the offset against a measured board temperature (not just ambient) so I can see the relationship. A linear drift suggests a single component's tempco; a nonlinear or threshold-like drift suggests something changing state — a solder joint, a connector, or a component approaching a thermal limit.

Second, I'd localize the source. The offset could originate in the sensor, the instrumentation amplifier, the ADC reference, or the passive network around them. I'd use a thermal camera or a thermocouple to map where heat is concentrated, then apply targeted heating and cooling — a heat gun or freeze spray on individual components — to see which one moves the offset. If heating the amplifier alone reproduces the drift, I've localized it.

Third, I'd check the obvious tempco suspects: the reference voltage (a reference with poor tempco drifts the whole measurement), resistor networks (mismatched tempco in a differential front-end creates offset), and the amplifier's own offset drift. I'd compare measured drift against datasheet tempco specs to see whether the observed drift is explainable by component specs or exceeds them — the latter suggests a layout or assembly issue rather than a component selection issue.

Fourth, I'd consider mechanical and assembly effects. A solder joint with a void, a connector with differential thermal expansion, or a PCB flexing with heat can all produce reversible offset changes. I'd look at whether the drift correlates with a specific thermal gradient across the board rather than absolute temperature.

Finally, I'd verify the fix by cycling temperature on the modified unit and confirming the offset stays within spec across the full operating range, not just at the endpoints.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a component tempco problem from a PCB layout problem using thermal imaging? What would you do if the drift only appears when the enclosure is assembled, not on an open board?

## Q5: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** When two teams are each confident the other side owns the problem, the deadlock is usually a symptom of the investigation being organized around ownership rather than around evidence. My job as lead is to change the frame from "whose fault is it" to "what would each hypothesis predict, and how do we test both cheaply?"

First, I'd get both teams to write down their hypotheses as falsifiable predictions. If the hardware team believes it's a marginal timing or signal-integrity issue, what would that predict about failure rate versus temperature, cable length, or bus loading? If the firmware team believes it's a race condition or a protocol-handling bug, what would that predict about failure rate versus CPU load, interrupt timing, or specific command sequences? Writing these down forces specificity and often reveals that the two hypotheses predict *different* observable behaviors.

Second, I'd design experiments that discriminate between the predictions rather than experiments that each team runs to confirm its own view. A good discriminating test is one where the two hypotheses predict opposite outcomes. For example, if I can capture the bus traffic with a logic analyzer at the moment of failure, I can see whether the transaction was malformed at the electrical level (hardware) or whether the firmware issued an invalid sequence (firmware). Instrumentation that both teams agree is neutral is key.

Third, I'd set up a shared data repository and a single source of truth for the failure signature — what exactly constitutes a "failure," how it's detected, and what's logged. A lot of cross-team deadlocks dissolve when both teams realize they've been looking at different failure modes or different definitions of failure.

Fourth, I'd run a structured fault-injection or stress campaign that both teams co-design, so neither feels the test is rigged. If the failure rate changes predictably with a variable one team controls, that's evidence, regardless of whose hypothesis it supports.

Finally, I'd keep the tone collaborative and evidence-focused. The goal isn't to declare a winner but to find the root cause, and often the answer is "both" — a marginal hardware condition exposed by a firmware timing assumption. Framing it that way keeps both teams engaged rather than defensive.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating experiment is inconclusive and both hypotheses remain viable? How do you keep a cross-functional investigation on schedule when the deadlock persists?
