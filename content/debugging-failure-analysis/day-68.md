# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 68

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The key insight is that the failure correlates with mechanical state, not electrical state, so the investigation should treat the enclosure and mounting as part of the circuit. I'd start by reproducing the offset reliably and quantifying it — magnitude, sign, and whether it's stable or drifts — because a consistent offset points toward a static effect (ground shift, leakage path, mechanical stress on a component) rather than a noise or timing issue.

From there I'd divide the problem: measure the analog front-end at the ADC input with the board on the bench, then repeat with the board inside the enclosure but not mounted, then mounted in its final position. That three-point comparison isolates whether the enclosure itself, the mounting hardware, or the installation environment introduces the offset. Common mechanisms to check: a mounting screw or standoff creating an unintended ground path or shorting a guard trace; the enclosure pressing on a connector or flex cable and changing contact resistance; a chassis-ground connection that shifts the reference potential; or mechanical stress on a strain-sensitive component like a ceramic capacitor or a sensor die.

I'd also verify the reference and ground at the point of measurement, not just the signal, since a "sensor offset" is often actually a ground-reference shift. If the offset tracks the mounting, I'd instrument the suspect path — measure voltage between board ground and chassis ground, check for current flowing through an unintended path, and inspect the mechanical assembly for anything contacting the PCB. The fix is usually a layout or mechanical change (grounding scheme, isolation, strain relief), and I'd confirm it by re-running the same three-point test.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a ground-shift offset from a true sensor offset? What would you do if the offset only appeared in the customer's installation and not in your own fixture?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and power-distribution problem, so I'd approach it as a power integrity investigation rather than a component fault. First I'd characterize the ripple properly: probe at the load with a short ground lead (or a proper tip-and-barrel probe) to avoid picking up radiated noise, and capture the transient with enough bandwidth to see the actual waveform — amplitude, frequency content, and whether it's synchronous with the switching event or a resonant ring afterward.

Then I'd separate the two possible mechanisms. If the ripple is a direct response to the load step, it's likely insufficient bulk capacitance, high ESR/ESL in the decoupling network, or a regulator with inadequate transient response or loop bandwidth. If it's a ringing at a specific frequency, it's more likely an LC resonance between the regulator's output inductance and the decoupling capacitance, or a layout issue — long traces, poor ground return, or a via/plane discontinuity adding parasitic inductance.

I'd measure at multiple points along the rail — at the regulator output, at the bulk cap, at the load's decoupling cap — because the ripple amplitude growing toward the load tells you the impedance between those points is the problem. I'd also check the ground return path, since a high-current switching event can cause ground bounce that shows up as apparent rail ripple. Practical next steps: add or reposition bulk capacitance, verify the regulator's compensation and transient spec, check the switching loop area, and confirm the load's own input capacitance isn't resonating with the source. The fix should be validated under the worst-case load step, not just nominal.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell whether the ripple is coming from the regulator or from the load itself? What role does the ground return path play in what you measure?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** The hardest part here is that the failure is silent — no fault flag, no out-of-range value — so the investigation has to start by making the invisible visible. I'd first define what "wrong" means precisely: is it a fixed offset, a scaling error, a stale value, a value from the wrong channel, or a plausible-but-corrupted number? The pattern of the error usually points to the mechanism, so I'd gather as many instances as possible and look for correlation with operating conditions, timing, or sequence of events.

Then I'd add instrumentation that captures the raw data path, not just the final reported value. That means logging the ADC raw counts, the timestamp, the channel index, and the firmware state at the moment of conversion, so I can compare what the hardware produced against what the firmware reported. If the raw value is correct but the reported value is wrong, the bug is in the firmware pipeline — a buffer index, a scaling constant, a shared variable updated by an interrupt, or a stale read. If the raw value itself is wrong, it's a hardware or timing issue — settling time, multiplexer switching, reference drift, or a conversion started before the input settled.

I'd also consider whether the error is deterministic or random. A repeatable offset suggests a configuration or calibration issue; an intermittent one suggests a race condition, a timing violation, or a marginal analog condition. Fault injection — deliberately forcing the suspect condition — can help confirm the mechanism. The corrective action should include a self-check that would have caught the error, so the same class of silent failure can't recur undetected.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a self-test that catches a plausible-but-wrong value without false positives? What's the difference between a stale value and a corrupted value, and how would you tell them apart?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the temptation is to argue about which is more likely — but the productive move is to design a test that discriminates between them. I'd reframe the disagreement as a question: what observation would be true under one hypothesis and false under the other? That test, even if it's a small experiment or a targeted measurement, is worth more than further debate.

Practically, I'd lay out both hypotheses with their supporting and contradicting evidence on a shared board, so the team is working from the same facts rather than competing narratives. Then I'd identify the cheapest, fastest discriminating test and assign it clearly. If the test is inconclusive, I'd look for whether the two causes could be interacting — sometimes the split is a false dichotomy, and the real root cause is a combination or a common upstream factor.

I'd also manage the human side: the goal is not to prove one engineer right, but to converge on the correct cause. I'd keep the tone evidence-driven, credit contributions from both sides, and set a decision point — if the discriminating test doesn't resolve it, we escalate to a broader test or bring in additional data rather than letting the investigation stall. Documenting the reasoning and the decision criteria matters, because in a regulated context the path to the conclusion is as important as the conclusion itself.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or slow? How do you keep the team aligned when the evidence ultimately favors one person's hypothesis over another's?

## Q5: A junior engineer has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd recognize is that the engineer isn't failing — they're using a debugging strategy that works for deterministic faults but doesn't fit an intermittent, system-level problem. Testing components in isolation assumes the fault lives in a component; if every component passes alone, the fault is likely in the interaction between them, or in a condition that only exists at the system level. So my job is to help them change the question, not to take over.

I'd sit down with them and ask what they've ruled out and how, then help them reframe: instead of "which component is broken," ask "what condition is present when it fails that isn't present when it passes?" That usually points toward timing, temperature, load, sequence of events, or an environmental factor. I'd suggest instrumenting the system to capture the state at the moment of failure — logging, a trigger on the fault, or a way to reproduce it on demand — because an intermittent fault you can't observe is very hard to fix.

I'd also acknowledge the frustration directly and normalize it: intermittent system-level bugs are genuinely hard, and several days without progress is common. I'd offer to pair on it for a session, not to solve it for them but to bring a fresh perspective and keep them moving. And I'd protect the schedule by making sure the investigation has a clear next step rather than open-ended testing. The goal is to leave them more capable and less stuck, not to have rescued them.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you help them build a reproduction that triggers the fault on demand? What would you do if the schedule pressure meant the team needed a workaround before the root cause was found?
