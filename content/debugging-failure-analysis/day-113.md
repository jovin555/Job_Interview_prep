# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 113

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline?

**Answer:** The defining challenge here is that every automated guard has already passed, so the error is hiding inside the "valid" envelope. I'd start by treating it as a data-integrity problem rather than a sensor problem, and work backward along the signal chain: sensor → analog front-end → ADC → firmware acquisition → storage → transmission → offline review. The first job is to make the failure observable in real time, because right now the only detector is a human reading a spreadsheet weeks later. I'd add instrumentation that captures the raw ADC counts alongside the processed value and a timestamp at the moment of acquisition, so I can tell whether the wrong value was ever physically present at the ADC or whether it was corrupted after conversion. That single split — "wrong at the converter" versus "wrong after the converter" — divides the investigation cleanly.

If the ADC counts are correct but the reported value is wrong, I'd look at the firmware path: buffer handling, type conversions, scaling arithmetic, shared-memory access between an ISR and the main loop, or a stale value being read because a "data ready" flag was consumed by the wrong consumer. If the ADC counts themselves are wrong, I'd look at sampling timing relative to switching events, reference voltage stability, channel multiplexing settling time, or a conversion being triggered before the previous one completed. Because the error is plausible rather than wild, I'd also suspect a partial or single-bit corruption — a race on a shared buffer, a misaligned read, or a value from the previous sample being latched. I'd try to reproduce it under stress: temperature, supply variation, and heavy interrupt load, since marginal timing bugs often only surface when the CPU is busy. Throughout, I'd keep a log of every hypothesis and the measurement that would confirm or kill it, so the investigation converges instead of sprawling.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the instrumentation so it doesn't itself perturb the timing you're trying to catch? If the corruption only appears in stored data and never on the live bus, where would you look next?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a classic load-transient and power-distribution problem, and the key is to separate "the regulator can't respond fast enough" from "the delivery network can't supply the transient." I'd first characterize the ripple properly — probe at the load, not just at the regulator output, and at the decoupling capacitors themselves, using a short ground lead or a proper probe tip so I'm not measuring the probe's own loop inductance. I'd capture the ripple synchronously with the switching event, using the subsystem's enable or gate signal as a trigger, so I can see the timing relationship between the current step and the voltage deviation. That tells me whether the dip is a high-frequency spike (inductive, layout or decoupling related) or a longer sag (regulator loop bandwidth or bulk capacitance related).

Then I'd measure the load transient directly — a current probe on the subsystem's supply, or a shunt — to quantify the di/dt and the total charge the rail has to supply during the step. With that, I can reason about whether the local decoupling is adequate, whether the bulk capacitance is positioned too far away, whether the regulator's loop is stable under the step, or whether there's a shared-impedance path — a common ground or supply trace — coupling the switching current into the sensitive rail. I'd also check for ground bounce: if the subsystem's return current shares a path with the analog or logic ground reference, the "ripple" may actually be a ground shift. Fixes follow from the diagnosis: better local decoupling and tighter loop area for high-frequency content, more or better-placed bulk capacitance for the sag, layout changes to separate the high-current return path, or a regulator/compensation change if the loop itself is the limit. I'd verify each change with the same triggered measurement so I'm comparing like with like.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between insufficient decoupling and a regulator loop-stability problem from the waveform alone? What would you check first if the ripple frequency matches the switching frequency rather than the subsystem's event rate?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** A reversible, temperature-correlated offset points to a thermally-driven parameter shift somewhere in the analog chain, and the fact that it recovers on cooldown tells me it's not permanent damage — it's a drift mechanism. I'd start by mapping the thermal profile: thermocouples or a thermal camera on the front-end components, the reference, and the board around them, so I know which parts are actually heating and by how much, and whether there's a thermal gradient across the board rather than a uniform rise. Then I'd try to localize the source by substitution and by isolation: does the offset track the amplifier, the voltage reference, the ADC, or the sensor bridge excitation? I can often separate them by measuring the reference and excitation directly while the offset develops, and by seeing whether the offset appears at the ADC input or only in the digitized result.

Common culprits are the voltage reference's temperature coefficient, input offset drift in the instrumentation amplifier, resistor tempco mismatch in a differential or bridge network, thermocouple effects at solder joints between dissimilar metals, or mechanical stress from board flexing as things expand. I'd also check whether the "offset" is actually a gain error in disguise — a reference that drifts changes the full-scale, which can look like an offset at mid-scale. Once I have a candidate, I'd confirm it by controlled heating of individual components (hot air or a thermal chuck) while watching the output, and by comparing against the datasheet tempco to see whether the observed drift is even plausible for that part. The corrective action depends on the mechanism: a better-tempco reference or matched resistor network, chopper-stabilized or auto-zeroed amplification, thermal isolation or layout changes to spread heat away from sensitive nodes, or firmware-side compensation if the drift is well-characterized and the application allows it. I'd verify by running the same continuous-operation profile and confirming the offset stays within budget across the full temperature range.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine offset drift from a gain drift using only measurements at two temperatures? If the drift is well-characterized but the hardware can't be changed, what are the risks of compensating in firmware?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** When the fault lands on a memcpy but the addresses and bounds all check out, the copy is usually the victim rather than the cause — it's just the first instruction that touches memory that something else has already corrupted or made inaccessible. I'd resist the temptation to keep staring at the memcpy and instead ask what could make a valid-looking copy fault: a corrupted stack or link register that sends execution somewhere unexpected, a buffer that was freed or reallocated underneath the copy, a stack overflow that has quietly eaten into an adjacent region, a misaligned access on a core that faults on unaligned copies, or a peripheral/DMA engine writing into the same region concurrently. The "valid addresses" in the trace may themselves be misleading if the fault handler's unwinding is reading a corrupted stack.

My approach would be to make the fault reproducible and observable: enable a fault handler that captures the full register set, the fault status registers (which distinguish bus fault, memory management fault, usage fault, and so on), and a snapshot of the stack, and log or retain that across resets. The fault status register is often the single most informative piece — it tells me whether this is an access violation, an unaligned access, or an escalation from a lower-priority fault. Then I'd look for the real culprit with tools rather than guesswork: stack high-water-mark monitoring and canary values to catch overflow, MPU regions to trap writes outside intended buffers, watchpoints on the source and destination regions to catch who else is touching them, and heap/allocator instrumentation if dynamic memory is involved. If DMA is in the picture, I'd check for a race between the CPU copy and a DMA transfer into overlapping memory. I'd also review the copy's callers for lifetime bugs — a pointer to a buffer that's valid at call time but whose owner has already released or reused it. The fix follows from whichever mechanism the evidence supports, and I'd confirm it by running the same stress profile that previously triggered the fault.

**Possible follow-ups:** What do the fault status registers tell you that the stack trace alone cannot? How would you use the MPU to catch the corruption at the moment it happens rather than at the memcpy?

## Q5: You're leading a cross-functional failure investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The deadlock usually isn't a lack of skill on either side — it's that each team is reasoning from its own model of the system and neither has produced evidence the other accepts. My first move is to reframe the question away from "whose fault is it" toward "what single measurement would discriminate between the two hypotheses," and then get both teams to agree on that measurement before anyone runs it. That agreement is what makes the result credible to both sides; if one team designs the test alone, the other will discount the outcome. I'd write down both hypotheses explicitly, with the predicted observable for each — for example, "if it's a hardware timing margin issue, the failure rate should scale with temperature or cable length; if it's a firmware race, it should scale with interrupt load or task scheduling" — so the test has a clear pass/fail meaning for each.

Then I'd instrument the boundary itself: capture the bus traffic, timing, and any error flags at the interface between the two domains, synchronized to a common timebase, so the evidence sits in neutral territory rather than in either team's lab. I'd also push for reproducibility — a stress setup that makes the failure frequent enough to study — because intermittent arguments thrive on rare events. Structurally, I'd run short, frequent joint reviews where each side presents evidence rather than conclusions, keep a shared hypothesis log with what's been ruled out, and explicitly assign ownership of the discriminating experiments rather than of the blame. If the two hypotheses genuinely can't be separated with available evidence, I'd escalate to a designed experiment that varies one factor at a time — temperature, load, cable, firmware build — until the correlation points one way. The goal is to make the investigation converge on evidence both teams helped generate, so the conclusion is owned jointly and the fix doesn't get relitigated.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or slow to run and the schedule is tight? How do you keep the teams collaborative when the evidence eventually does point clearly at one side?
