# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 123

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** When the faulting instruction and the addresses both look legitimate, the fault is almost never about the *values* being copied — it's about the *state of the system* at the moment of the copy. I'd start by treating the fault as a symptom of something that happened earlier, not at the memcpy itself. The first thing I'd want is the full fault context: the fault status register (bus fault, memory management fault, usage fault, hard fault escalation), the stacked PC/LR, and the contents of the stack frame at the time of the fault. On Cortex-M parts, the hardware-stacked frame tells you which instruction faulted and what the SP was — that's often more informative than the trace itself.

Then I'd ask: what kind of fault is it? A bus fault on a valid-looking address can mean the address is valid *in the linker map* but the memory region isn't actually enabled or powered at that moment — for example, an MPU region that got reconfigured, a peripheral clock that got gated, or a memory region that's been remapped. A usage fault on a memcpy can mean an unaligned access, which is easy to miss if the compiler emitted a word-aligned copy but the pointer arithmetic produced an odd address. An imprecise bus fault is the nastiest case, because the reported PC is not the faulting instruction — it's wherever the pipeline happened to be when the fault was taken. In that case I'd look at write buffers and posted writes: the fault may have been *caused* by an earlier store that only completed later.

I'd also look hard at the copy itself. Is it a `memcpy` from the C library, a compiler-emitted inline copy, or a DMA transfer? If it's DMA, the "memcpy" in the trace may be the CPU waiting on a transfer that the DMA engine is doing — and the fault could be a bus contention or a peripheral that's mid-transfer when its clock is removed. If it's a library memcpy, I'd check whether the length argument could be corrupted: a length that's huge but the source/destination pointers are fine will still fault once it walks off the end of a valid region. That's a classic case where the addresses in the trace look fine but the *count* is wrong.

The other angle is concurrency. If the copy runs in an ISR or a task and another context can modify the source buffer or free the memory being copied, you can get a fault that looks impossible in a single-threaded trace. I'd check for reentrancy, for buffers shared between an ISR and main context without protection, and for any place where a pointer is cached and then the underlying buffer is reallocated or moved. A memory-protection unit or a canary around the buffer can catch this quickly.

Practically, my sequence would be: capture the fault registers and stacked frame, decode the fault type, check alignment and length, check whether the memory region is actually accessible at that moment (MPU, clock gating, power), then check for concurrency and DMA. If I can't reproduce it, I'd add lightweight instrumentation — a fault handler that dumps the full context to non-volatile memory, plus counters around the copy — and let it run until it trips. The key discipline is not to "fix" the memcpy; the memcpy is doing what it was told. The bug is in whatever made the system state wrong when it ran.

If the fault only appears after hours of operation, what would you add to the firmware to capture the state without perturbing timing?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and loop-stability problem, and the first thing I'd do is separate the two: is the ripple the regulator failing to respond to a fast load step, or is it noise coupling from the switching subsystem into the rail through a shared impedance? Those have different fixes, so I'd want to measure before changing anything.

I'd start by probing the rail at the point of load, not at the regulator output — with a short ground lead, ideally a coax pigtail or a probe tip with a ground spring, because a long ground clip will pick up exactly the switching noise I'm trying to characterize and lie to me. I'd capture the ripple in both time and frequency domain: time domain to see whether it's a transient droop-and-recovery synchronized with the load step, or a continuous oscillation; frequency domain to see whether the ripple sits at the regulator's switching frequency, at a harmonic, or at some lower frequency that suggests a control-loop instability.

If it's a transient response issue, I'd look at the regulator's output capacitance and its ESR/ESL, the inductor value and saturation, and the compensation network. A load step that's faster than the loop can respond will always produce a droop — the question is whether the droop exceeds the rail's tolerance and whether the recovery rings. I'd check the datasheet's recommended output cap range and ESR window; many regulators are only stable within a specific ESR band, and a cap that's "better" (lower ESR) can actually destabilize a loop that was compensated for a lossier cap. I'd also check whether the high-current subsystem has local bulk capacitance — if it's drawing a fast transient and the only bulk cap is at the regulator, the trace inductance between them will produce a dip at the load that the regulator can't see in time.

If it's coupling rather than loop response, I'd look at shared ground and shared supply impedance. The switching subsystem's return current flows through a common ground path; if that path has inductance, the switching current develops a voltage across it that appears on the analog rail's reference. I'd check the ground return topology — is the high-current subsystem's return separate from the sensitive rail's return until a single point? — and I'd look at whether the rail's decoupling caps are placed to keep the high-frequency loop small. A near-field probe scan can show whether the ripple is radiating from the switching node into the rail's routing.

The measurement discipline matters here: I'd verify the ripple with the subsystem's switching disabled but a resistive load emulating the same average current, to confirm the ripple is transient-related and not just a function of DC load. And I'd check the ripple at multiple points along the rail — at the regulator, at the load, and at the decoupling caps — because the difference between those tells you whether it's a source problem or a distribution problem.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the regulator's compensation is marginal without a network analyzer? If the ripple is within the regulator's datasheet transient spec but still corrupts an ADC reading, where would you look next?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** A reversible, temperature-correlated offset points to a thermal coefficient somewhere in the signal chain, and the job is to find which component's drift dominates and whether it's being compensated or amplified by the topology. The fact that it returns to zero on cooldown is actually helpful — it means it's a parametric drift, not damage, so I can reproduce it deliberately.

I'd start by characterizing the drift properly: run the device in a thermal chamber or under its own self-heating, log the offset against measured board temperature (not ambient — I'd place a thermocouple or use an on-board temperature sensor near the analog front-end), and see whether the curve is linear, quadratic, or has a knee. A knee often indicates something crossing a threshold — an amplifier leaving its linear region, a reference starting to drift, or a component whose tempco changes sign. I'd also check whether the drift tracks a specific component's temperature or the overall board temperature; if I can localize it with a thermal camera or a spot cooler/heater on individual parts, that narrows it fast.

Then I'd walk the signal chain and compare each stage's expected tempco against what I'm seeing. The usual suspects: the voltage reference (many references have a specified tempco in ppm/°C, and a 10 ppm/°C reference over a 30°C rise is a real offset), the instrumentation amplifier's offset drift and gain drift (both specified, and the gain drift can matter more if the front-end has high gain), the sensor bridge itself (bridge sensitivity and offset both drift), and the resistors setting gain (matched resistor networks drift together; discrete resistors don't). I'd compute the worst-case sum from datasheets and see whether the observed drift is within that budget — if it is, the fix is a better component or a calibration scheme; if it's larger, something is behaving outside spec or the topology is amplifying a drift I didn't account for.

I'd also look at the topology for drift amplification. A front-end that derives its excitation from the same rail as its reference can have ratiometric behavior that cancels some drift — or, if the reference and excitation drift differently, doubles it. A chopper-stabilized or auto-zeroed amplifier will have much lower offset drift than a standard precision op-amp, but its input bias current tempco can still matter with high source impedance. And I'd check thermal EMFs: dissimilar metal junctions at connectors or solder joints generate microvolts per degree, which is negligible at DC but can look like offset drift if the board has a thermal gradient.

The corrective action depends on what I find. If it's a reference or amplifier tempco within spec, I'd either specify a tighter part, add a temperature compensation term in firmware calibrated per unit, or change the topology to be ratiometric. If it's a thermal gradient issue, I'd look at copper balancing and component placement. And I'd verify the fix by repeating the same warm-up profile and confirming the drift is now within the error budget across the full operating temperature range, not just at the endpoints.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine sensor drift from a drift in the electronics driving it? If the drift is within the datasheet error budget but still fails the system's accuracy requirement, what are your options?

## Q4: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The deadlock usually isn't a technical disagreement — it's that each team is looking at the problem through its own instrumentation and neither has a shared, falsifiable model of the failure. My job as lead is to convert "your side is wrong" into "here's a test that will tell us which hypothesis is correct," and to make sure both teams agree on what the result would mean *before* the test runs.

I'd start by getting both teams to write down their hypotheses explicitly, with the mechanism and the predicted evidence. "Firmware timing issue" is not a hypothesis; "the firmware's bus recovery routine issues a clock pulse before the peripheral is ready, which the hardware sees as a start condition" is. Once each side has a mechanism, I can design a test that distinguishes them. The key move is to agree in advance: if we see X, the firmware hypothesis is supported; if we see Y, the hardware hypothesis is. That prevents the result from being reinterpreted after the fact.

Then I'd instrument the boundary. The communication failure happens at the interface between firmware and hardware, so I want visibility on both sides of that interface simultaneously. On the hardware side, a logic analyzer or scope on the bus lines with a deep capture buffer, triggered on the error condition. On the firmware side, timestamped logging of every transaction, every retry, and every state transition, with a shared time base if possible. If I can correlate the firmware's view of the transaction with the actual bus waveform at the moment of failure, the disagreement usually dissolves — one side's assumption about what the other was doing turns out to be wrong.

I'd also look for a way to make the failure more frequent, because intermittent failures that occur once a day are hard to argue about. Temperature, supply voltage, bus load, and timing stress are the usual knobs. If I can get the failure rate up, both teams can iterate quickly and the evidence accumulates. If I can't, I'd set up a long-running capture with automated triggering and let it run.

Throughout, I'd keep the framing collaborative rather than adversarial. Both teams are usually right about something — the firmware team may correctly observe that the error only happens under a certain timing pattern, and the hardware team may correctly observe that the bus margins are marginal. The root cause is often the interaction, not one side. I'd make it clear that the goal is a shared model of the failure, and that whoever's hypothesis is disproven has contributed just as much by narrowing the space. And I'd document the investigation as it goes — hypothesis, test, result, conclusion — so the eventual corrective action is traceable and reviewable.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the instrumentation itself perturbs the timing and the failure stops occurring? How would you decide when to stop investigating and implement a mitigation that covers both hypotheses?

## Q5: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's firmware occasionally fails to complete a flash write operation, and the failure rate increases as the device's internal temperature rises — but the flash device is rated well above the operating temperature range?

**Answer:** A temperature-correlated failure in a part that's rated well above the operating range usually means the part isn't the problem — the *conditions* it's seeing are outside what the datasheet assumes, or the timing margins have collapsed. I'd treat this as a margin problem, not a component problem, and I'd want to measure the actual conditions at the flash device rather than trusting the ambient or the board-level temperature.

First, I'd verify the temperature at the part. A thermal camera or a thermocouple on the flash package tells me the case temperature; from that I can estimate junction temperature using the package thermal resistance and the device's power dissipation during a write. If the device is in a sealed enclosure with a nearby heat source — a regulator, a charging circuit, a processor — the local temperature can be well above the board average. I'd also check whether the write operation itself is self-heating: flash writes dissipate power, and a burst of writes can raise the die temperature above what a steady-state measurement shows.

Then I'd look at the supply and timing margins at temperature. Flash write operations have tight timing requirements — program pulse widths, setup and hold times on the interface, and supply voltage tolerances during the write. If the supply rail sags during a write (because the write draws a current burst and the decoupling is marginal), the write can fail even though the average rail voltage is fine. I'd probe the supply at the flash device's pins during a write, at temperature, with a short-ground probe, and look for droop or noise. I'd also check the interface timing: if the flash is on SPI or a parallel bus, the clock and data margins shrink with temperature-driven impedance changes and with the increased propagation delay of the driver. A setup/hold violation that's invisible at room temperature can become marginal at elevated temperature.

I'd also consider the possibility that the failure isn't the flash at all but the *sequence* around it. If the firmware writes flash while another operation is in progress — an interrupt that services a high-priority task, a DMA transfer, a power-management event — the write can be interrupted or the supply can be disturbed at a critical moment. Temperature can change the timing of those interactions: an oscillator drifts, a regulator's transient response changes, a processor's execution speed shifts. I'd look at whether the failures correlate with specific firmware activity, not just temperature.

The corrective action depends on what I find. If it's supply droop, I'd add local decoupling or sequence the write to avoid coinciding with high-current events. If it's timing margin, I'd slow the interface clock or add wait states. If it's local heating, I'd look at thermal design — copper pour, thermal vias, separation from heat sources. And I'd verify the fix by repeating the write stress at the worst-case temperature, not just at the temperature where the failure was first seen, because the margin at the edge is what matters.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a supply-margin failure from a timing-margin failure when both produce the same "write failed" symptom? If the flash vendor's datasheet guarantees operation to a higher temperature than you're seeing, how would you escalate or investigate whether the part is being used outside its intended conditions?
