# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 129

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** A fault in a memory copy where both pointers are valid and in-bounds usually means the fault isn't really about the copy itself — it's about something the copy depends on that isn't captured in the stack trace. I'd start by separating the three things a memcpy actually touches: the source region, the destination region, and the CPU state (alignment, MPU/MMU configuration, stack pointer, interrupt context). The first question is whether the fault is a bus fault, a usage fault, or a memory-management fault — the fault status registers usually tell you which, and that narrows the hypothesis space considerably.

If the addresses are valid at the moment of the fault, I'd suspect one of a few things: an alignment issue (some architectures fault on unaligned access depending on configuration), a region that's valid in the linker map but not actually backed by initialized memory, an MPU region that's been reconfigured by another task or ISR, or a stack overflow that has corrupted the frame the copy is operating on. I'd check the stack pointer against the stack limits, look at whether the fault is reproducible with the MPU disabled, and inspect whether the copy is happening in an interrupt context where the stack budget is tighter than in thread mode.

The other angle is timing: if the copy is long and the fault is intermittent, I'd look at whether something else is modifying the region concurrently — a DMA engine, another ISR, or a lower-priority task that the copy preempted. A memory copy that's correct in isolation but faults under concurrency often points to a missing critical section or a cache-coherency issue rather than a bad pointer. I'd instrument with a fault handler that dumps the full register set, the fault status registers, and a small window of memory around both pointers, then try to reproduce under load rather than on an idle bench.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a stack overflow from an MPU misconfiguration as the cause, given both can produce a fault in a copy routine? What would you change in the fault handler to make the next occurrence more diagnosable?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The fact that the ripple appears only under load switching tells me the problem is almost certainly in the power delivery network's response to a transient, not in the regulator's steady-state regulation. I'd start by characterizing the ripple properly rather than just looking at its amplitude: what's the frequency content, is it synchronized to the switching edge, and is it present at the regulator output, at the load, or both? Probing at the decoupling capacitors with a short ground lead — not the long ground clip on a scope probe — matters here, because a long ground lead will pick up radiated switching noise and make the ripple look worse than it is.

Once I have a clean measurement, I'd separate the causes: insufficient bulk or decoupling capacitance for the transient, excessive ESR/ESL in the capacitor network, a layout issue where the high-current return path shares impedance with the sensitive rail, or the regulator's loop response being too slow for the load step. A load-transient test with a controlled current step, viewed on the scope, tells me whether the rail recovers cleanly or rings — ringing points to loop stability or excessive inductance, while a slow sag points to insufficient capacitance or a current-limit response.

I'd also check whether the "ripple" is actually ground bounce: measuring the rail relative to the IC's ground pin rather than the board's ground plane can reveal that the rail is fine and the reference is moving. If the switching subsystem has a high di/dt loop, the fix is often layout — tightening the loop area, improving the return path, adding a local high-frequency decoupling cap right at the load — rather than changing the regulator. I'd verify any change with the same load-transient setup so I'm comparing like with like.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between ripple caused by the regulator's control loop and ripple coupled in from the load's return path? What measurement setup mistakes most often lead engineers to overestimate ripple?

## Q3: A medical device passes all functional and safety tests at design verification, but during a 30-unit pilot production run, three units fail a 72-hour burn-in test with the same symptom: the device stops communicating after roughly 48 hours and requires a power cycle to recover. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The pattern — passes DV, fails only in a small fraction of pilot units, fails at a consistent elapsed time, and recovers on power cycle — points to something that accumulates or drifts over time rather than a hard design defect. I'd treat the three failing units as the most valuable evidence I have and resist the urge to immediately re-test them in a way that erases the failure state. First step is to capture the state at failure: what does the communication interface look like electrically, is the peripheral still powered, is the firmware hung or is the bus locked up, and are there any error counters or logs that survived the power cycle.

The 48-hour consistency is a strong clue. It could be a thermal accumulation effect (the unit reaches a steady-state internal temperature after many hours and something drifts out of spec), a counter or buffer that overflows after a predictable number of transactions, a watchdog or timeout that only trips under sustained load, or a leakage/aging effect in a component. I'd instrument a failing unit to log bus activity, temperature, and supply rails continuously through the burn-in so I can see what happens in the hours before the failure rather than only after.

I'd also compare the three failing units against passing units from the same run: same component lots? Same assembly line? Same firmware build? If the failures cluster by lot or by build, that redirects the investigation toward a supplier or process issue. And I'd try to accelerate the failure — run a unit at elevated temperature or with a higher transaction rate — to see whether the 48-hour figure shrinks proportionally, which would confirm an accumulation mechanism and make the investigation tractable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to root-cause this before shipping or to add a recovery mechanism as an interim mitigation? What would you log during burn-in to make the next failure diagnosable without a power cycle?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** A reversible, temperature-dependent offset is a strong signal that the mechanism is thermal, and the fact that it returns to zero on cooldown tells me it's not a permanent drift or damage — it's a parameter shifting with temperature. I'd start by mapping the offset against temperature rather than against time: run the device in a chamber or with controlled heating and record offset at several stable temperatures, both on warm-up and cool-down. If the curve is hysteretic, that points to something mechanical or to a component with thermal memory; if it's repeatable, it's a straightforward temperature coefficient issue.

The candidates are the sensor bridge itself, the instrumentation amplifier's offset drift, the reference voltage's tempco, or a thermocouple effect from dissimilar metals in the signal path (connectors, solder joints, PCB traces). I'd isolate by substituting a stable resistive source for the sensor and seeing whether the offset still appears — if it does, the problem is in the amplifier/reference chain; if not, it's in the sensor or its interface. Measuring the reference voltage and the amplifier's input offset separately across temperature narrows it further.

I'd also look at the thermal gradient across the board rather than just the ambient: a temperature difference between the sensor connector and the amplifier inputs can generate a small thermoelectric voltage that looks exactly like an offset. Layout, copper balancing, and thermal isolation of the sensitive nodes matter here. The fix depends on which element is drifting — component selection with tighter tempco, a compensation scheme in firmware, or a layout change to reduce gradients — but I'd want the temperature-vs-offset curve in hand before choosing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a thermoelectric effect from a genuine component tempco? If the drift is in the sensor itself, what options do you have short of changing the sensor?

## Q5: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is to let the team argue about which is more likely rather than designing a test that discriminates between them. My first move is to reframe the question: instead of "which cause is it," ask "what observation would be true under one hypothesis and false under the other?" That usually reveals a cheap, decisive experiment that neither camp has proposed because each has been building evidence for its own case rather than trying to falsify it.

I'd structure it explicitly: write down both hypotheses, the evidence for each, and the predictions each makes that the other doesn't. Then assign the discriminating test to someone — ideally not the person most invested in either hypothesis — and set a clear criterion in advance for what result would confirm or eliminate each. Pre-committing to the interpretation before running the test prevents the result from being argued about after the fact.

If a single decisive test isn't feasible, I'd run both lines in parallel with a time-box, and I'd be explicit with the team about what "enough evidence" looks like for each path. I'd also keep the corrective-action decision separate from the root-cause decision: if one hypothesis implies a cheaper or faster mitigation, it may be worth implementing as a containment while the investigation continues, as long as we're honest that containment isn't confirmation. The goal is to converge on evidence, not on consensus — and to make sure the team leaves the room knowing what the next test is and who owns it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the discriminating test is inconclusive? What would you do if the person most invested in one hypothesis is also the person best placed to run the test?
