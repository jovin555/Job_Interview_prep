# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 124

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline?

**Answer:** The defining challenge here is that every automated check passes, so the fault is invisible to the device's own validation logic. I'd start by treating this as a data-integrity problem rather than a sensor problem, and work backwards along the signal chain: sensor → analog front-end → ADC → firmware acquisition → storage → transmission. The first step is to make the failure observable. If the device doesn't already log raw ADC counts alongside the processed value, I'd add temporary instrumentation that captures both, plus a timestamp and the acquisition context (which channel, which mode, what else was running). Without that, you're comparing a number you can't explain to a number you can't explain.

Next I'd characterize the error statistically: is the wrong value a small offset, a scaled version of the true value, a stale value from a previous sample, or a bit-flipped version? Each points somewhere different. A stale value suggests a buffer or DMA sequencing issue; a scaled value suggests a gain or reference problem; a bit flip suggests memory or bus integrity. I'd also check whether the errors cluster in time, correlate with other activity (radio transmit, motor drive, flash writes), or correlate with temperature or supply voltage.

A key technique is fault injection: deliberately corrupt or delay a known sample and confirm the pipeline handles it the way I think it does. Often the investigation reveals that the "plausible but wrong" value is actually a correctly-acquired sample from the wrong moment — a race between the ADC conversion-complete flag and the read — which no range check would ever catch. The corrective action then targets the acquisition sequencing, not the sensor.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to add permanent raw-data logging versus temporary instrumentation, given the regulatory and storage constraints on a medical device? What would you do if the error only appeared in units that had been in the field for months, not in fresh production units?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a classic load-transient and power-distribution problem, and the fact that it's load-dependent tells me the regulator's loop and the decoupling network aren't keeping up with a fast current step. I'd first measure properly: probe the rail directly across the decoupling capacitors with a short ground lead (a pigtail will pick up its own loop inductance and lie to you), and capture the transient with enough bandwidth to see the actual edge. I'd look at where the ripple appears — at the regulator output, at the load, or both — because that distinguishes a regulator loop response problem from a distribution impedance problem.

Then I'd characterize the load step itself: how fast is the current edge, how large, and how repetitive? A slow, large step stresses the bulk capacitance and the regulator loop; a fast, small step stresses the high-frequency decoupling and the PCB parasitics. I'd check the regulator's datasheet for its load-transient response and compare against what I'm seeing — if the deviation exceeds the datasheet's expected transient, the issue is likely layout or component selection; if it matches, the rail may simply be under-decoupled for this load.

On the layout side, I'd examine the current return path: is the high-current subsystem's ground return shared with the sensitive rail's reference, creating common-impedance coupling? Ground bounce from a switching load can appear as ripple on an unrelated rail if the return paths aren't separated. I'd also verify the bulk and high-frequency capacitors are actually where they need to be, not just present on the schematic. The fix usually comes down to one of: better decoupling placement, a feed-forward capacitor or loop compensation tweak, a ferrite or LC filter isolating the noisy load, or rerouting the return path.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish ripple caused by the regulator's own switching from ripple coupled in from the load's ground return? If the datasheet transient response matches what you measure, does that mean the design is acceptable or that you've chosen the wrong regulator?

## Q3: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** Deadlocks like this usually persist because each team is testing in its own domain and neither has produced evidence that the other team accepts as relevant. My first move is to reframe the question from "whose fault is it" to "what single measurement would discriminate between the two hypotheses?" That converts an ownership argument into an experiment.

I'd get both teams in a room and have each state their hypothesis as a falsifiable prediction: if it's a hardware timing margin issue, we'd expect the failure rate to change with temperature, supply voltage, or cable length; if it's a firmware race, we'd expect it to change with interrupt load, task priority, or timing of other operations. Then I'd design a test matrix that varies those factors independently and captures bus-level data — a logic analyzer or scope on the actual lines, timestamped, correlated with firmware event logs. The point is to produce evidence both teams can read from the same capture.

I'd also insist on a shared reproduction: a test setup that fails reliably enough to be studied, owned jointly, with a defined pass/fail criterion. If the failure can't be reproduced on demand, I'd instrument the system to catch it in the act — bus captures, GPIO toggles marking firmware state transitions, supply monitoring. And I'd set a rule that no fix gets implemented until the discriminating evidence is in hand, because a speculative fix from either side just muddies the signal. Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative: both teams are usually partly right, and the goal is a shared model of the failure, not a verdict.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is inconclusive and the schedule forces a decision? How do you keep a cross-functional team from re-litigating ownership once evidence starts pointing one way?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** The reversibility is the most informative clue: it rules out permanent damage and points to a temperature-dependent parameter shift somewhere in the signal chain. I'd treat it as a thermal characterization problem first. Using a thermal chamber or controlled heating, I'd sweep temperature slowly and log the offset against measured board temperature (not ambient — the difference matters), looking for the shape of the curve: linear, exponential, or a step at a particular temperature. A step often indicates a specific component crossing a threshold; a smooth curve suggests a drift in a parameter like offset voltage, bias current, or a resistor ratio.

Then I'd localize it. I'd measure at multiple points in the chain — sensor bridge output, amplifier input and output, ADC reference, ADC input — to see where the offset first appears. If the offset is present at the amplifier output but not its input, the amplifier or its feedback network is implicated; if it appears at the ADC but not the amplifier output, the reference or ADC itself is suspect. I'd also check the obvious thermal suspects: a resistor with a poor temperature coefficient in a divider or gain-setting network, a reference with inadequate tempco, a solder joint or connector with a thermal EMF, or a mechanical stress effect on a sensor.

A subtle but common cause is thermal gradients across the board rather than absolute temperature — a power component heating one end of the analog section creates a gradient that a single temperature sensor won't capture. Thermal camera imaging during warm-up helps here. The corrective action depends on the finding: component selection with better tempco, layout changes to spread heat or isolate the analog section, or — if the drift is inherent and within the sensor's spec — a firmware compensation calibrated over temperature, provided that's acceptable for the device's accuracy claims.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine component tempco problem from a thermal EMF generated at a dissimilar-metal junction? If the drift is within the sensor's datasheet limits but still trips your system's fault threshold, is that a hardware problem or an algorithm problem?

## Q5: A junior engineer on your team has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The pattern here — components pass in isolation, system fails — is a strong signal that the debugging strategy, not the engineer, is the problem. Isolated component testing can't reproduce a failure that depends on interactions, so the engineer is working hard in a frame that structurally can't find the answer. My first job is to make that visible without making them feel like they've wasted days, because they haven't — they've eliminated a lot of possibilities, which is real progress.

I'd sit down with them and ask them to walk me through what they've ruled out and what hypotheses remain. Then I'd help them reframe: instead of "which component is bad," ask "what condition exists at the system level that doesn't exist in isolation?" That usually means power delivery, grounding, timing, thermal, or interaction between subsystems. I'd work with them to build a reproduction that exercises the full system and fails often enough to study, and to instrument it so the failure is captured rather than just observed — logging, bus captures, supply monitoring.

On the people side, I'd be careful not to take the keyboard. The goal is to teach a method they can reuse, not to solve this one for them. I'd pair on the next experiment, let them drive, and check in at short intervals so frustration doesn't build. I'd also acknowledge the schedule pressure honestly and help them communicate status upward — "we've eliminated X and Y, we're now testing Z" is a legitimate update that buys credibility. And afterward, I'd want a short retrospective on what made the failure hard to reproduce, because that knowledge is worth more than the fix itself.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer resisted the reframe and wanted to keep testing components? What would you change about how the team scopes intermittent failures in the future so this situation is less likely to recur?
