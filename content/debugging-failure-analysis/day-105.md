# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 105

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The key observation is that the offset is deterministic and tied to a physical configuration change, not to time or temperature — so I'd treat it as a mechanical/electrical coupling problem rather than a component tolerance problem. My first step is to reproduce the failure in a controlled way and then bisect the assembly: measure the analog front-end output with the board on the bench, then with the board in the enclosure but the lid off, then with the lid on but the unit not mounted, then fully installed. That sequence isolates whether the offset comes from the enclosure itself (shielding, grounding, capacitive coupling to a metal chassis), from the mounting position (mechanical stress on the PCB, flexure changing a solder joint or a connector contact resistance), or from the installation environment (a nearby ground plane, a mounting bracket that creates a ground loop).

Once I know which stage introduces the offset, I'd instrument it: measure the front-end output and the reference voltage simultaneously, check whether the offset appears at the amplifier output or is already present at the ADC input, and verify the ground reference at the analog ground star point relative to the chassis. A common cause is that the enclosure or mounting bracket introduces an additional ground path, so the analog ground reference shifts slightly relative to the ADC's reference — which shows up as a fixed offset. Another is mechanical stress on a precision resistor or a flexing connector that changes contact resistance. I'd also check whether the offset scales with the input signal (gain error) or is constant (offset error), because that points to different mechanisms.

The fix depends on the mechanism: if it's a ground loop, I'd look at the grounding scheme and whether the chassis should be tied to analog ground at one point only; if it's mechanical stress, I'd look at mounting and board stiffening; if it's capacitive coupling, shielding or guard traces. Crucially, I'd verify the fix by repeating the full assembly sequence, not just the bench test, and I'd add a production test that measures the front-end in the final enclosure so this class of failure is caught before shipping.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a ground-reference shift from a genuine sensor offset in this scenario? What production test would you add to catch enclosure-dependent offsets without adding excessive test time?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and power-distribution problem, so I'd start by characterizing the ripple properly rather than trusting a single probe reading. I'd measure at the decoupling capacitors closest to the load, using a short ground lead or a coaxial probe tip to avoid picking up radiated switching noise in the probe loop itself — a long ground clip can make a clean rail look noisy. I'd capture the ripple synchronously with the switching event, using the subsystem's enable or gate-drive signal as a trigger, so I can see whether the disturbance is a transient response (dip and recovery) or continuous ripple at the switching frequency.

Then I'd separate the mechanisms. If the ripple is at the subsystem's switching frequency, it's likely conducted ripple from the subsystem's own supply or ground return — I'd check the return path and whether the subsystem's ground current is flowing through a shared impedance with the sensitive rail. If it's a transient dip at the switching edge, it's the regulator's loop response to a fast load step — I'd look at the regulator's output capacitance, ESR, and compensation, and whether the load step exceeds what the regulator was designed for. If it's high-frequency ringing, it's likely parasitic inductance in the PDN — I'd check the placement and via structure of the decoupling caps and whether the loop area between cap and load is small.

I'd also verify the measurement itself: probe the rail at multiple points (at the regulator output, at the load, at the far end of the plane) because a rail can look clean at one point and noisy at another, which tells you whether the noise is being generated locally or is a distribution problem. The corrective action follows from the mechanism — more bulk or ceramic decoupling, a ferrite bead to isolate the subsystem's supply, a layout change to tighten the return path, or a regulator compensation change — and I'd verify it under the worst-case load profile, not just a single switching event.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between adding bulk capacitance versus a ferrite bead versus a layout change? What would you check first if the ripple appears at the load but not at the regulator output?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** The hardest part here is that the failure is silent — no fault flag, no out-of-range value — so the investigation has to start with making the failure observable. My first move is to add instrumentation that captures the raw data path: log the ADC raw counts alongside the converted value, the timestamp, the channel configuration, and any state that could affect the reading (gain setting, multiplexer state, reference status). If the wrong value is plausible, the corruption is likely happening somewhere between the physical sensor and the stored value, and I need to see where the two diverge.

Then I'd think about the classes of mechanism that produce plausible-but-wrong values. One is a stale or misaligned read: the firmware reads a buffer before the ADC conversion completes, or reads the wrong channel because a multiplexer switch hasn't settled, or a DMA transfer lands out of order. Another is a data-integrity issue: a single-bit flip in memory or on the bus, a race between an interrupt updating a shared variable and the main loop reading it, or a buffer overwrite. A third is a sensor-side issue: the sensor itself returns a valid but incorrect value under certain conditions (saturation recovery, self-test mode, a communication glitch that returns the last value).

I'd try to reproduce it under accelerated conditions — run the device continuously, vary temperature and supply, and stress the bus — while logging enough to catch the event. If I can catch a bad sample, I'd compare the raw and converted values to localize the corruption. If I can't reproduce it, I'd add a lightweight integrity check (a checksum or a plausibility cross-check against a second measurement) so the next occurrence is flagged and captured, and I'd review the firmware's concurrency and buffer handling for the classic race and alignment bugs. The corrective action has to address the mechanism, and I'd verify it by running the same stress test long enough to have confidence the event rate has dropped.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a plausibility check that catches wrong-but-in-range values without generating false positives? If the corruption is a single-bit flip, how would you determine whether it's a memory issue or a bus issue?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is make sure the split is about evidence, not about ownership or seniority. I'd ask each side to state, explicitly, what observation would be true if their hypothesis were correct and false if the other were correct — a discriminating test. Often the disagreement persists because both hypotheses explain the existing data equally well, which means the existing data can't resolve it and we need a new experiment, not more argument.

I'd then design the cheapest, fastest experiment that discriminates between the two. That might be a targeted measurement, a controlled fault injection, a swap test, or a temporary instrumentation build. I'd assign it clearly, set a time box, and agree in advance what result would support which hypothesis — so the outcome is decided by the data, not by whoever argues hardest afterward. If both hypotheses survive the first test, I'd run a second discriminating test rather than defaulting to the more popular one.

In parallel, I'd keep the team aligned by framing it as "we have two candidates and we're going to let the evidence choose," which depowers the disagreement. I'd also consider whether the two causes could both be contributing — sometimes the split is a false dichotomy and the real answer is a combination, or one cause is primary and the other is a contributing factor. I'd document the decision and the evidence in the investigation record, because in a regulated medical device context the rationale for the conclusion matters as much as the conclusion. If a schedule pressure forces a decision before the evidence is conclusive, I'd be explicit about the residual uncertainty and what monitoring or verification would close it.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or takes weeks to run? How would you handle a situation where the team's preferred hypothesis turns out to be wrong?

## Q5: A junior engineer on your team has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The pattern here — everything passes in isolation, the system fails — usually means the engineer is testing the wrong thing: they're validating components against their own specifications rather than reproducing the system-level interaction that causes the failure. My first step is to sit down with them and ask what the failure actually looks like when it occurs, how often it occurs, and whether they can reproduce it on demand. If they can't reproduce it reliably, that's the real blocker, and the priority is to build a reproduction — a stress setup, a scripted test sequence, or instrumentation that captures the failure when it happens — before doing any more component-level testing.

I'd then help them reframe: instead of "which component is bad," ask "what condition is present when the system fails that isn't present when it passes." That shifts the search from components to interactions — timing, power sequencing, grounding, shared resources, state that accumulates over time. I'd walk them through a divide-and-conquer approach at the system level: change one variable at a time, log everything, and narrow the failure window rather than testing parts in isolation.

On the people side, I'd be careful not to take over the debugging — that undermines them and doesn't build their capability. I'd coach, ask questions, and let them drive, but I'd make sure they have the instrumentation and the time-boxed plan they need. I'd also acknowledge the frustration directly and normalize it: intermittent system-level bugs are genuinely hard, and several days without progress is not a sign of failure. If the schedule is tight, I'd help them communicate status honestly and re-scope what "done" looks like for the next checkpoint, so the pressure doesn't push them into a premature fix.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you balance coaching versus stepping in if the deadline is at risk? What would you do if the engineer's isolation-testing approach is actually the right one and the failure is genuinely component-level but rare?
