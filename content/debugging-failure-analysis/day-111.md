# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 111

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline?

**Answer:** The defining characteristic here is that every automated check passes, so the investigation has to start by making the failure observable rather than by chasing a hypothesis. I'd first characterize the error from the offline data: is the wrong value a fixed offset, a scaled version of the true value, a stale sample from a previous reading, or a value that looks like a different channel's data? That classification narrows the suspect list enormously — a stale sample points at buffering or DMA sequencing, a scaled value points at a format or fixed-point conversion bug, a cross-channel value points at an indexing or multiplexer addressing error, and a fixed offset points at calibration or initialization order.

Next I'd build a way to catch it in the act. Since the device's own self-tests don't flag it, I'd add temporary instrumentation: log the raw ADC output alongside the post-processed value and a timestamp, and log the sequence of reads so I can tell whether the wrong value was ever actually present at the ADC or was introduced afterward. If the raw value is correct and the processed value is wrong, the bug is in firmware data handling. If the raw value is already wrong, it's in the acquisition path — timing, settling, multiplexer switching, or a conversion started before the previous one completed.

I'd also look hard at the conditions under which it occurs. Plausible-but-wrong values that appear only occasionally usually correlate with something: a concurrent operation (radio transmit, motor drive, flash write), a particular operating mode, temperature, or a specific timing relationship between the sampling loop and another task. I'd try to reproduce it deliberately by stressing those conditions — running the acquisition loop at a different rate, adding load, forcing concurrent activity — because a reproducible failure can be bisected, and an unreproducible one can only be speculated about.

Finally, I'd treat the absence of a self-test flag as a finding in itself. If the system has any plausibility or cross-check logic, I'd ask why it didn't catch this class of error, and whether a rate-of-change check, a redundant read, or a range-of-delta check would have caught it. That's a corrective-action item independent of the root cause.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a stale sample from a genuinely wrong conversion if you can only log the final value? What would you change in the firmware architecture to make this class of error detectable in the field rather than only in offline review?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The fact that it's load-dependent tells me the problem is in the delivery path's response to a transient, not in the regulator's steady-state regulation. I'd start by measuring properly rather than trusting a single probe point. Ripple measured at the load is what matters, but I'd measure at several points — the regulator output, the bulk cap, the decoupling caps at the load, and the ground return — because the difference between those measurements localizes the problem. If the ripple is large at the regulator but small at the load, the filtering between them is doing its job and the issue is elsewhere. If it's small at the regulator and large at the load, the impedance of the intervening path is the problem.

I'd then look at the transient itself: what's the di/dt, what's the duration, and what's the repetition rate? A fast, high-amplitude transient that the regulator can't respond to in time points at insufficient bulk capacitance or excessive ESR/ESL in the delivery path. A slower transient that the regulator should handle points at loop stability, insufficient phase margin, or a control loop that's being disturbed by the switching event — possibly through a shared reference, a shared ground, or a control signal that couples into the feedback node.

Probing technique matters a lot here. I'd use a short-ground-spring probe or a coaxial connection rather than the long ground lead on a standard probe, because a long lead picks up switching noise and can make a clean rail look terrible. I'd also check whether the "ripple" is actually differential ripple on the rail or common-mode noise between the measurement point and the probe ground — those have completely different fixes.

Once I understand the transient, the fixes are usually one of: add or resize bulk capacitance close to the load, improve the decoupling network's impedance across the relevant frequency band, tighten the physical loop area of the high-current path, separate the noisy subsystem's return current from the sensitive rail's reference, or slow the transient at the source if the subsystem allows it. I'd verify each change with the same measurement setup so I'm comparing like with like.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the ripple is differential or common-mode? If adding bulk capacitance reduces the ripple amplitude but doesn't eliminate the resets, what would you look at next?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The bench-to-installed delta is the whole story here, so the first job is to enumerate what changes between those two states and then test each change independently. The candidates are: mechanical (enclosure, mounting, cable routing, connector mating), electrical (different power source, different grounding, additional loads active), thermal (self-heating in an enclosed space), and environmental (proximity to other equipment, different reference potentials). I'd build a matrix of these and try to reproduce the offset by introducing them one at a time on the bench — put the board in the enclosure without installing it, install it without the enclosure, power it from the installed supply, route the cable as installed, and so on. The first configuration that reproduces the offset tells me which variable matters.

A consistent offset specifically suggests a DC-level effect rather than noise: a ground potential difference between the sensor reference and the ADC reference, a change in the reference voltage itself, a thermal EMF from a dissimilar-metal junction that only exists in the assembled configuration, or a mechanical stress effect on a component (some sensors and references are stress-sensitive, and mounting torque can shift them). I'd measure the reference voltage and the ground potential at both ends of the signal path in the installed configuration and compare them to the bench values — if the offset tracks a ground or reference shift, that's the mechanism.

I'd also check whether the offset is truly constant or has a warm-up component. If it grows over the first several minutes after assembly, thermal effects or stress relaxation are more likely than a pure ground offset. If it's present immediately and stable, it's more likely a DC path issue.

The corrective action depends on the mechanism: a ground offset calls for a better reference scheme or a differential measurement; a thermal EMF calls for eliminating the junction or balancing it; a stress effect calls for changing the mounting or the component. In all cases I'd want a verification method that tests the unit in its installed configuration, because a bench-only acceptance test would never have caught this.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a ground offset from a reference voltage shift if you can only measure at the ADC input? What would you change in the design verification plan so this class of failure is caught before field deployment?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is reframe the situation: two live hypotheses with partial evidence is not a stalemate, it's a normal mid-investigation state, and the team's split is useful information rather than a problem to be suppressed. What's missing is a discriminating test — an observation whose outcome would be different under hypothesis A versus hypothesis B. So I'd ask the team to articulate, for each hypothesis, what evidence would confirm it and what evidence would falsify it, and then look for the cheapest, fastest test that separates them.

If such a test exists, we run it and let the data decide. If it doesn't exist yet, that's the real finding — it means we don't understand either mechanism well enough, and the next step is to build the capability to discriminate, whether that's instrumentation, a targeted experiment, or a destructive analysis on a limited number of returned units. I'd be explicit about the cost and time of each path and make a decision about which to fund rather than letting the debate continue indefinitely.

I'd also guard against the failure mode where the team pursues both hypotheses in parallel and neither gets enough rigor. Parallel investigation is fine when resources allow, but each track needs a clear owner, a clear question, and a deadline, and the results need to be compared on the same evidence standard. And I'd keep the team aligned on the decision rule in advance — agreeing now that "if test X shows Y, we go with A" prevents the result from being argued about after the fact.

Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative rather than adjudicative. The goal isn't to declare a winner among the engineers; it's to let the evidence pick the mechanism. If the evidence ultimately supports the hypothesis of the person who was in the minority, that needs to be a normal, non-punitive outcome, or people will stop offering dissenting views in future investigations.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is destructive and you only have two returned units? How do you keep a parallel investigation from doubling cost without doubling insight?

## Q5: A junior engineer on your team has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The pattern here — components pass in isolation, system fails — is a strong signal that the debugging strategy, not the engineer, is the problem. Isolation testing is the right instinct, but it can't find failures that only exist in the interaction between components, so the engineer is systematically testing the one thing that's already known to be fine. I'd start by acknowledging that their work isn't wasted — it's ruled out a whole class of causes — and then redirect toward system-level observation.

Concretely, I'd sit down with them and ask three questions: what does the failure actually look like when it happens (not "it fails" but the specific observable), under what conditions does it occur and not occur, and what have they changed so far. Often the answers reveal that the failure has never been properly characterized — it's been observed but not measured. I'd help them build a way to catch it in the act: instrumentation, logging, a trigger on the failing condition, or a stress setup that makes it happen more often. An intermittent failure that occurs once a day is nearly impossible to debug; one that occurs once a minute can be bisected.

I'd also model the divide-and-conquer approach explicitly: rather than testing components, change one thing at a time in the failing system and see whether the failure follows. Swap a subsystem, bypass a stage, force a condition, and observe. The goal is to shrink the space of possible causes with each experiment, and to make sure each experiment actually discriminates between hypotheses rather than just confirming that something works.

On the human side, I'd check in more frequently for a short period rather than issuing advice and walking away — frustration compounds when someone feels stuck alone. I'd pair them with someone for a session, or work alongside them myself, and I'd make sure the schedule pressure is being managed at my level rather than pushed down onto someone who's already stuck. If the schedule genuinely can't absorb the investigation, that's a decision for me to make and communicate, not a reason to rush a junior engineer into a guess.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between an engineer who needs a new strategy and one who needs more time with the current one? What would you put in place so the next intermittent failure on the team gets characterized before it gets debugged?
