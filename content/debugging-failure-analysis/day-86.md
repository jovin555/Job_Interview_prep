# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 86

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a device that passes every functional test on the bench but fails only when powered from its internal battery, at the same nominal voltage as the bench supply?

**Answer:** The first thing to recognize is that "same nominal voltage" is not the same as "same source." A bench supply and a battery differ in ways that matter even when the DC level matches: source impedance, transient response under load steps, noise and ripple content, grounding topology, and behavior at the moment of inrush or brownout. So I'd treat the supply itself as the variable to isolate rather than assuming the failure is elsewhere.

A structured approach: first, confirm the failure is genuinely supply-dependent by reproducing it on battery and then on a bench supply with a series resistor or electronic load that emulates the battery's source impedance — if the failure follows the impedance, that points to a load-transient or brownout issue rather than a logic bug. Second, instrument the actual rail at the point of load, not at the supply terminals, and capture load-transient response with a scope on a fast timebase; a battery with higher internal resistance will sag more during a current step, and a marginal regulator or an undersized bulk capacitor will let that sag propagate. Third, check the ground path — a battery-powered unit often has a different ground reference and return path than a bench setup, which can change noise coupling into sensitive analog or reset circuitry. Fourth, look at the power-up and power-down sequencing: battery insertion or a soft-start can produce a slow ramp that a bench supply doesn't, and a reset supervisor or brownout detector with the wrong threshold can misbehave on a slow ramp.

Throughout, I'd change one variable at a time — impedance, then ramp rate, then grounding — and keep a written log of what each change did to the failure rate. If the failure is intermittent, I'd want a repeatable trigger before drawing conclusions, because chasing a non-reproducible symptom wastes time.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you emulate a battery's source impedance accurately enough to trust the comparison?
- If the failure disappears when you add bulk capacitance, how do you decide whether that's the real fix or just masking a deeper issue?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a classic load-induced power integrity problem, and the key is to separate the source of the ripple from the path it travels. The switching subsystem is the aggressor, but the ripple appearing on the rail could be coming from the regulator's inability to respond to the load step, from the decoupling network being inadequate at the switching frequency, or from the ripple coupling through a shared ground or supply impedance rather than being generated on that rail at all.

I'd start by measuring correctly: probe the rail right at the decoupling capacitors with a short ground lead or a proper tip-and-barrel probe, not a long ground clip, because a long ground lead will pick up loop inductance and show you ringing that isn't really on the rail. Then I'd characterize the ripple's frequency content — is it at the switcher's fundamental, at a harmonic, or at the load's step rate? That tells you whether you're looking at conducted switching noise or a transient response problem.

Next, I'd look at the load transient response directly: apply a step load at the subsystem's switching rate and watch how the regulator recovers. If the recovery is slow or overshoots, the issue is loop bandwidth or output capacitance. If the rail is clean at the regulator but noisy at the load, the problem is the impedance between them — trace inductance, insufficient local decoupling, or a ground return that's shared with the switching current. I'd also check whether the ripple is common-mode or differential by probing the rail and its return together.

The fix depends on which of those it is: more or better-placed local decoupling for a path problem, a regulator compensation or bulk-capacitance change for a response problem, or layout changes to separate the high-current return from sensitive references. I'd verify the fix with the same measurement setup and confirm it holds across load and temperature.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you tell the difference between ripple generated by the regulator and ripple coupled in from a shared ground?
- What layout changes would you prioritize if the problem is a shared return path?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** The hardest part of this class of bug is that nothing in the system flags it, so the investigation has to start by making the invisible visible. A plausible-but-wrong value means the failure is silent — it passes range checks and plausibility checks — so the first goal is to build instrumentation that captures the raw data path, not just the final value.

I'd approach it in layers. First, establish where in the chain the corruption happens: is the raw sensor reading already wrong, or is the value correct at the sensor and corrupted somewhere between acquisition, storage, and transmission? That means logging the value at multiple points — at the ADC or sensor interface, after any filtering or scaling, and at the point it's stored or sent — so you can bisect the pipeline. Second, look for a pattern in the bad values: are they off by a fixed amount, a fixed ratio, a bit-flip, a stale value from a previous sample, or a value that looks like it came from a different channel? Each pattern points to a different mechanism — a scaling or calibration error, a memory or buffer issue, a race between acquisition and readout, or a multiplexer or channel-selection problem.

Third, consider timing and concurrency: if the value is read while it's being updated, or if a DMA transfer and a CPU read overlap, you can get a torn or stale value that's still plausible. Fourth, check the boundary conditions — does it happen more at temperature extremes, at certain sample rates, or after long runtimes? That can point to drift, leakage, or a counter that wraps.

Because the error is only caught offline, I'd also want to improve the system's own detection: add a cross-check or a redundant read so the device can flag implausible values itself, which both helps the investigation and is good practice for a medical device. The corrective action should trace back to the specific mechanism, not just to "add a filter."

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish a stale value from a genuinely miscalculated one?
- What kind of cross-check would you add without introducing false positives?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the temptation is to argue about which is more likely, but that's not a productive use of the team's time. The better move is to reframe the question: what evidence would distinguish the two, and can we design a test that produces that evidence? If a test can't distinguish them, then the hypotheses may not actually be in conflict — they might both be contributing, or one might be a symptom of the other.

So I'd start by writing down, for each hypothesis, what it predicts and what it doesn't. Then I'd look for a discriminating test — a fault-injection, a controlled change, a measurement at a specific point — that gives a different result depending on which hypothesis is true. If the team is split, I'd assign each hypothesis an owner and ask them to design the test that would falsify their own view, which tends to reduce defensiveness and surface assumptions. I'd also set a time-box: if the discriminating test is inconclusive, we escalate to a more invasive measurement or bring in additional expertise rather than letting the debate run indefinitely.

Throughout, I'd keep the focus on the evidence and the decision, not on who's right. And I'd be explicit about the cost of being wrong in each direction — for a medical device, the corrective action has to address the actual mechanism, so a wrong conclusion isn't just inefficient, it's a safety and regulatory problem. If the evidence genuinely can't separate the two, the honest answer may be to address both with a combined corrective action and verify it under conditions that would have triggered either.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the discriminating test is too expensive or slow to run?
- What would you do if the team's split reflects a deeper disagreement about the system's design rather than just this failure?

## Q5: A junior engineer has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the technical problem from the human one, because they need different responses. Technically, the pattern — components pass in isolation, system fails — almost always means the failure depends on an interaction or a condition that isolation testing removes: a shared supply or ground, a timing relationship, a thermal or mechanical condition, or a load that only exists in the full system. So the debugging strategy needs to shift from "test the part" to "reproduce the system condition and vary one thing at a time."

I'd sit down with the engineer, not to take over, but to review what's been tried and what's been ruled out, and to help them see the gap in their approach. Often the issue is that they've been changing multiple things or testing without a repeatable reproduction, so the results aren't conclusive. I'd help them build a reproduction that's as close to the real failure condition as possible, then instrument the system to capture what's happening at the moment of failure rather than testing parts afterward.

On the human side, several days without progress is demoralizing, and frustration makes debugging worse — people start changing things randomly. I'd acknowledge the effort, normalize that intermittent system-level bugs are genuinely hard, and give them a concrete next step rather than a vague "keep at it." I'd also consider pairing them with someone for a fresh perspective, or taking a structured break to step back and re-examine assumptions. If the schedule is tight, I'd help them time-box the current approach and define a fallback, so they're not stuck indefinitely. The goal is to get them unstuck and keep them engaged, not to signal that they've failed.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide when to step in directly versus coaching them through it?
- What would you do if the engineer resists changing their approach?
