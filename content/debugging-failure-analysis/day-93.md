# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 93

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a device whose failure only reproduces on the production test fixture but never on the bench with a lab supply and jumper wires?

**Answer:** The fixture itself is a variable, so the first step is to characterize what's different about it rather than re-testing the DUT. Differences typically fall into a few buckets: power delivery (the fixture may use a switching supply with different transient response, longer leads with more inductance, or a shared rail feeding multiple test points), grounding (fixture ground return path, earth reference, or a ground loop through the test PC), and loading (the fixture may present different impedance on I/O lines, or hold lines in states the bench setup doesn't). I'd start by instrumenting the fixture interface — scoping the supply rails right at the DUT connector, not at the supply, to see whether transients or ripple differ from the bench. Then I'd check ground continuity and impedance from the DUT ground to the fixture's reference, and look for any earth-referenced instrument (scope, DAQ, PC via USB) creating a return path. If the failure is digital, I'd compare the actual signal levels and edge rates at the DUT pins between the two setups. A useful technique is to bisect the fixture: replace its supply with a known-good bench supply while keeping everything else, then swap the harness, then the load board — one change at a time — until the failure follows a specific element. I'd also ask whether the fixture runs a different test sequence or timing than the bench, since a marginal timing or initialization-order dependency can look like a fixture-only failure.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the failure follows the fixture's supply, what specifically would you measure to confirm it's transient response rather than steady-state accuracy? How would you decide whether to fix the fixture or treat the DUT as marginal?

## Q2: You're investigating a field return where the device works correctly on arrival but fails after a few hours in the customer's environment. How would you structure the investigation so you're not just chasing a unit that currently passes?

**Answer:** The core risk is that a passing unit tempts you to close the ticket, so I'd treat "passes on arrival" as a data point, not a conclusion. First, I'd preserve evidence before powering it: photograph as-received condition, log any stored fault codes and operating hours, and check for environmental indicators (dust, corrosion, connector wear, evidence of drop or liquid). Then I'd run it under conditions that approximate the customer's environment rather than the bench — extended soak at the reported ambient, the same accessory cables and power source type, and the same duty cycle — while logging continuously so a failure that occurs hours in is captured rather than missed. I'd instrument the suspect subsystems with periodic health checks (rail voltages, communication error counters, temperature) so I can see a trend developing before the functional failure. In parallel I'd pull the customer's usage context: what else is nearby, how it's mounted, what the power quality is like, and whether other units at that site show the same behavior. If the unit won't fail, I'd compare its behavior against a known-good unit under identical soak conditions — sometimes the returned unit is subtly different (a marginal component, a calibration drift) even if it hasn't crossed the failure threshold yet. The goal is to convert an intermittent field symptom into a reproducible stress condition before drawing any root-cause conclusion.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you log during the soak to catch a failure that only manifests as a functional symptom hours in? How would you handle the case where the customer's environment can't be replicated in the lab at all?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** A consistent offset that appears only after assembly and mounting points to a mechanical or environmental coupling rather than a component tolerance — the electronics haven't changed, so something about the physical configuration has. I'd separate the two variables first: test the fully assembled unit on the bench before mounting, then mount it without the enclosure, to determine whether the enclosure or the mounting position is the trigger. Common mechanisms are mechanical stress on the PCB or sensor (board flex changing a bridge sensor's output, connector or solder joint stress), a changed thermal environment (the enclosure trapping heat and shifting the offset via temperature coefficient), a changed ground reference (mounting to a chassis or frame introducing a ground path or loop), or a changed electromagnetic environment (the mounting position near a motor, transformer, or power conductor). I'd measure the offset's polarity and magnitude and check whether it tracks temperature by logging the AFE output alongside a thermocouple during warm-up. If it's thermal, the offset should correlate with temperature and reverse on cooldown; if it's mechanical, gently flexing or re-seating the board in the enclosure should reproduce it. I'd also verify the reference and ground at the AFE input with a scope in both configurations, since a ground shift from a chassis connection can look exactly like a sensor offset.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset correlates with temperature inside the enclosure, how would you distinguish a sensor TC issue from an amplifier or reference TC issue? How would you confirm a mechanical-stress hypothesis without damaging the unit?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and impedance problem, so I'd measure it as one. First, I'd scope the rail at the load — right at the decoupling capacitors and the IC's supply pins, using a short ground lead or a proper probe tip — because ripple measured at the regulator output can look fine while the rail at the load is not, and vice versa. I'd characterize the ripple's frequency and shape: if it's at the switching frequency of the high-current subsystem, it's likely conducted or radiated coupling or a shared-impedance issue; if it's a transient dip and recovery at each switching edge, it's the regulator's loop response to a fast load step. Next I'd check the power distribution impedance: bulk and decoupling capacitor placement and values, trace and via inductance between the regulator and the load, and whether the high-current return path shares ground with sensitive circuitry. A useful test is to add temporary bulk capacitance right at the load — if the ripple collapses, the problem is distribution impedance, not the regulator. I'd also verify the regulator's stability margin under the actual load step (load transient response, phase margin if measurable) and check whether the high-current subsystem's switching edges are coupling into the rail through a shared trace or a ground plane split. Only after localizing whether it's source, distribution, or coupling would I consider a fix — more local decoupling, layout change, snubbing the switching edge, or separating the return path.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish conducted ripple from radiated coupling into the rail? If adding local bulk capacitance fixes it on the bench, what would you check before accepting that as the production fix?

## Q5: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is letting the team argue about which is more likely instead of designing a test that separates them. My first move is to reframe: what observation would be true under hypothesis A and false under hypothesis B, and can we make that observation? I'd write both hypotheses down with their predicted signatures — what each predicts about timing, temperature dependence, unit-to-unit variation, or which subsystem is implicated — and then look for a discriminating experiment, even a small one, that the whole team agrees would be decisive. If no single test separates them, I'd run both lines in parallel with clear owners and a time box, rather than serializing and burning schedule on debate. I'd also make sure the disagreement is about evidence, not about ownership or prior experience — sometimes a hypothesis is being defended because of who proposed it, and naming that explicitly and depersonalizing the discussion helps. Throughout, I'd keep a written record of what's been ruled out and why, so the team isn't re-litigating settled points, and I'd set a decision point: if the discriminating test is inconclusive by a certain date, we escalate to a broader diagnostic (e.g., fault injection or a design-of-experiments sweep) rather than continuing to argue. The conclusion has to be evidence-driven and documented, because in a medical device context the corrective action and its traceability depend on it.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or requires hardware you don't have for weeks? How do you keep a team member whose hypothesis is eliminated engaged and constructive afterward?
