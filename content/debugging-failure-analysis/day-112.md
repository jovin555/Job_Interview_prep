# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 112

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The key observation is that the offset appears only when two variables change together — the enclosure is fitted and the unit is mounted in its operating position. I'd treat those as separate variables and isolate them rather than testing the fully assembled unit as one lump. First, reproduce the offset with the enclosure fitted but the unit still on the bench, so I know whether the enclosure alone is sufficient to trigger it. If it is, the mechanism is likely mechanical or electrical coupling introduced by the enclosure: a ground reference shifting because a mounting point now ties the analog ground to chassis at a different location, a cable or flex routing that changed when the lid went on, or a connector that seats differently under compression. If the offset only appears once the unit is in its final mounting position, I'd look at what the installation changes — chassis grounding through the mount, proximity to a metal surface altering stray capacitance, or mechanical stress on the PCB or sensor that shifts a bridge or reference.

From there I'd measure rather than speculate: probe the analog front-end's reference and ground at the ADC pins with the unit in each configuration, and compare the actual differential voltage at the input against what the firmware reports. That tells me whether the offset originates in the analog domain (a real voltage shift) or downstream (a reference or conversion issue). I'd also check whether the offset is stable and repeatable or drifts, since a stable offset points to a fixed parasitic or grounding path while a drifting one points to thermal or mechanical stress. The corrective direction depends on the mechanism — a grounding/return-path fix, a mechanical decoupling of the sensor, or a shielding change — but I wouldn't commit to a fix until I can turn the offset on and off by changing exactly one variable.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset disappears when you loosen the enclosure screws, what does that tell you about the mechanism? How would you distinguish a grounding-induced offset from a genuine sensor offset in the assembled unit?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Ripple that appears only under a switching load is almost always a load-transient or impedance problem rather than a steady-state regulator problem, so I'd start by characterizing the disturbance itself. I'd probe at the load — right at the decoupling capacitors of the subsystem being switched — not just at the regulator output, because the two can look very different and the difference tells you where the impedance is too high. Using a short-ground-tip probe or a proper coax connection to avoid picking up probe-loop artifacts, I'd capture the transient with enough bandwidth to see the edge, and measure three things: the amplitude of the dip or spike, its duration, and how quickly it recovers. That recovery profile separates a bulk-capacitance problem (slow sag, slow recovery) from a high-frequency decoupling or loop-stability problem (fast spike, ringing).

Next I'd check the obvious contributors in order of likelihood: insufficient bulk capacitance or excessive ESR local to the load, a decoupling network whose resonance sits near the switching frequency, a ground return path shared with the switching subsystem so the transient couples into the rail's reference, or the regulator's control loop being too slow to respond to the step. I'd also verify the measurement isn't an artifact — ground-lead inductance on the probe can manufacture ripple that isn't really there. If the ripple is real, I'd test the hypothesis cheaply before changing the design: add temporary bulk capacitance at the load, or temporarily power the subsystem from a separate supply, and see whether the ripple on the main rail collapses. That confirms whether it's a shared-impedance problem. The fix then follows the mechanism — more local bulk, better decoupling placement, a dedicated return path, or a regulator/compensation change — and I'd re-measure at the load to confirm.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between ripple caused by the regulator's loop response and ripple caused by shared ground impedance? What measurement mistakes most commonly make ripple look worse than it is?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** The hard part here is that the failure is silent — nothing in the system flags it, so I have no event to trigger on. My first move is to make the failure observable before trying to explain it. That means adding instrumentation that captures the raw path: log the ADC raw counts alongside the converted value, timestamp each sample, and record the sensor's status/ready flags at the moment of read. If the wrong value is plausible, the corruption is probably happening somewhere between the physical sensor and the stored/transmitted value, and I need to narrow which stage. Comparing raw ADC counts against the reported engineering value tells me whether the error is in the conversion math, the data handling, or upstream in the acquisition itself.

Then I'd look for a pattern in the captured data: does the wrong value correlate with a particular operating state, a bus transaction, a timing relationship to another peripheral, or a specific sequence of reads? A plausible-but-wrong value often points to a stale sample (reading the previous conversion because the ready flag was checked incorrectly), a race between the read and an update, a byte-ordering or sign-extension error on a specific code path, or a buffer that occasionally holds a value from a different channel. I'd also check whether the error is a single-bit flip, a swapped field, or a value that belongs to a different sensor — each points to a different class of bug. Because it's intermittent, I'd try to increase the failure rate deliberately: stress the timing, run at temperature extremes, or inject load on the bus, so I can reproduce it on demand and test candidate causes one at a time rather than waiting for it to recur.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the raw ADC counts are correct but the reported value is wrong, where would you focus next? How would you design the logging so it doesn't itself perturb the timing you're trying to catch?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is letting the team argue about which is more likely instead of designing a test that discriminates between them. My job as lead is to reframe the question from "which do we believe" to "what evidence would prove one and disprove the other." I'd get the team to state each hypothesis as a falsifiable prediction: if cause A is true, what should we see that cause B would not produce, and vice versa? That usually exposes that one or both hypotheses are vague enough to explain anything, which is itself a finding — a hypothesis that can't be falsified isn't ready to act on.

Then I'd prioritize by cost and speed: is there a single measurement, a targeted experiment, or a fault-injection test that separates the two quickly? Often there is, and the disagreement dissolves once someone frames it that way. If the discriminating test is expensive or slow, I'd run both lines in parallel with clear owners and a defined decision point, rather than serializing and stalling. I'd also keep the team's disagreement visible and respectful — split teams often reflect genuinely ambiguous evidence, and the person advocating the "losing" hypothesis may be seeing something real. Throughout, I'd document the reasoning and the evidence for both paths, because if the investigation later needs to support a corrective action or a regulatory submission, the traceability of why we ruled out the alternative matters as much as the conclusion. The goal is to converge on evidence, not on consensus or seniority.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is inconclusive and you're under schedule pressure to pick one? How do you keep the team that backed the rejected hypothesis engaged afterward?

## Q5: A medical device passes all functional and safety tests at design verification, but during a 30-unit pilot production run, three units fail a 72-hour burn-in test with the same symptom: the device stops communicating after roughly 48 hours and requires a power cycle to recover. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Three units failing the same way at roughly the same point in burn-in is a strong signal — identical symptom and similar time-to-failure suggests a systematic, time-dependent mechanism rather than random component defects. I'd resist the urge to treat it as a one-off and instead treat the pilot run as an unplanned experiment that's giving me data. First I'd gather everything the failing units can tell me: were they from the same build lot, the same component batches, the same test fixture position? Do the passing units differ in any traceable way? That separates a design-margin problem from a manufacturing or supplier-lot problem. Then I'd look hard at what "48 hours" means physically — is there a counter, a log buffer, a memory leak, a thermal soak, a wear mechanism, or a state that only accumulates over time? A communication stop that clears on power cycle points toward a firmware state machine wedged by an unhandled condition, a buffer or resource that fills over time, or a hardware condition (thermal drift, a marginal connection, an oscillator) that crosses a threshold after sustained operation.

I'd instrument the failing units to capture the state at the moment communication stops — watchdog flags, bus error counters, stack/heap usage, temperature — rather than only observing the symptom after the fact. Because it's reproducible on a burn-in timescale, I can afford to run instrumented units and even deliberately accelerate the suspected mechanism. I'd also check whether the failure is truly time-based or event-count-based by running units under different activity levels. The corrective action has to be validated against the same burn-in that caught it, and because this is a medical device, I'd make sure the investigation and the fix are documented in a form that supports the design history and any regulatory follow-up — a pilot-run failure that reaches the field is exactly the kind of thing that needs a traceable root cause, not just a patch.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to halt the pilot run or continue it while investigating? If the failure turns out to be a firmware resource leak, how would you verify the fix without running another full 72-hour burn-in on every unit?
