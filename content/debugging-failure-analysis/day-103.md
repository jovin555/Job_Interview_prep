# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 103

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a device where the failure only reproduces when the unit is powered from its internal battery, but passes every test on a bench supply at the same nominal voltage?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd establish is that "same nominal voltage" is not the same electrical environment, so I'd stop treating the bench supply as a valid control and start enumerating the ways the two sources actually differ. A bench supply is a low-impedance, well-regulated source with a short, thick cable and often a remote-sense line; a battery pack presents a finite source impedance that rises as state-of-charge drops and as the cell ages, has a protection circuit (often a MOSFET pair with its own RDS(on) and a current-limit trip), and may have a fuel-gauge shunt in the return path. Any of those can turn a marginal load transient into a rail droop that never appears on the bench.

Practically, I'd instrument both configurations identically: a scope probe at the load point (not at the supply terminals) with a short ground lead, AC-coupled, triggered on the transient I suspect. I'd compare the transient response of the rail under the same firmware workload in both cases — same boot sequence, same radio transmissions, same motor or actuator events. If the battery case shows deeper or longer droops, I'd then separate source impedance from protection-circuit behavior by inserting a known series resistance in the bench setup to emulate the battery's ESR and see whether the failure reappears. That's the divide-and-conquer step: if I can reproduce it on the bench by degrading the source, I've isolated the mechanism to supply dynamics rather than something intrinsic to the battery.

I'd also check the things that only exist in the battery path — inrush at power-on through the protection FETs, the fuel gauge's sense resistor, any soft-start behavior, and whether the firmware's brown-out detection threshold is being grazed. And I'd verify the battery's actual voltage under load with a separate meter, because a pack that reads 3.7V open-circuit can sag well below that during a transmit burst. The key discipline is one variable at a time: don't change the firmware and the source simultaneously, or you lose the ability to attribute the result.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a source-impedance problem from a brown-out detector that's simply set too close to the operating rail? What would you do if the failure only appears at low state-of-charge and you can't easily hold the pack at that condition for repeated tests?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Ripple that appears only under a switching load points at the interaction between the load's current steps and the power delivery network's impedance, so I'd approach it as a loop-response and impedance problem rather than a "bad regulator" problem. First I'd characterize the ripple properly: probe directly across the output capacitor with the shortest possible ground connection — a pigtail ground lead will pick up switching noise and mislead you — and look at both the amplitude and the frequency content. Is it at the regulator's switching frequency, at the load's switching frequency, or at a beat between them? That single observation splits the diagnosis significantly.

If it's at the regulator's own switching frequency and grows with load steps, I'd look at the control loop's phase margin and the output capacitor's ESR and ESL — a cap that's fine at DC can be ineffective at the transient's edge. I'd also check whether the load step is faster than the regulator's loop can respond, in which case the bulk capacitance is doing the work and its placement and via count matter more than its nominal value. If it's at the load's frequency, I'd suspect ground bounce or a shared return path: the high-current subsystem's return current flowing through a common impedance can modulate the reference the regulator or the ADC sees. That's where I'd measure the ground potential difference between the regulator's ground reference and the load's ground return — a differential probe across two ground points often reveals more than any single-ended measurement.

I'd also verify the input side, not just the output: a load step can couple back through the regulator's input and cause the output to misbehave if the input decoupling is inadequate. And I'd confirm the measurement itself isn't the artifact — bandwidth limiting, probe loading, or a ground loop through the scope can all manufacture ripple that isn't there. The systematic approach is to move the probe point along the delivery path (regulator output, bulk cap, local decoupling at the load, load pins) and see where the ripple is largest; the gradient tells you where the impedance is.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the fix belongs in the regulator's compensation network or in the decoupling and layout? What measurement would you make to confirm ground bounce is the mechanism rather than output ripple?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** The hard part here is that the failure is invisible at the time it happens, so the first objective isn't to find the bug — it's to make the failure observable. A plausible-but-wrong value that passes every range check means the error is small enough to survive validation, which suggests either a marginal analog condition, a data-handling defect (scaling, byte order, a stale buffer, a race between the ADC read and the value being consumed), or a single-bit corruption that lands within the valid range. I'd start by adding instrumentation that captures the raw ADC output alongside the processed value and a timestamp, so I can tell whether the corruption happens before or after the conversion and processing chain. That single split — raw vs. processed — eliminates half the possible causes immediately.

If the raw value is correct and the processed value is wrong, I'd look at the firmware path: buffer reuse, interrupt/preemption around the read, fixed-point scaling overflow, or a shared variable without proper atomic access. If the raw value is already wrong, I'd look at the analog and timing side: sampling during a switching event, insufficient settling after a channel change, reference drift, or noise coupled from a subsystem that happens to be active at that moment. The fact that it's plausible rather than wild argues against a gross hardware fault and toward something marginal — a timing window, a settling time, a ground reference that shifts slightly.

I'd also try to correlate the bad samples with device state: what else was happening at that timestamp? Radio activity, motor or actuator events, charging, temperature. If I can find a correlation, I can try to reproduce it deliberately by forcing that condition. And I'd review the offline data for patterns — periodicity, clustering, correlation with sample rate or channel — because the pattern often names the mechanism. Throughout, I'd keep the investigation honest about the difference between "I found a plausible cause" and "I can reproduce it on demand," because only the latter lets you verify a fix.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the instrumentation so it doesn't itself perturb the timing you're trying to observe? If the corruption turns out to be a single-bit flip in RAM, what would that point you toward?

## Q4: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The first move is to reframe the disagreement: both teams are usually right about their own domain and wrong about the boundary, so the productive question isn't "whose fault is it" but "what single measurement would distinguish the two hypotheses?" I'd get the teams in a room and have each side state, precisely, what evidence would prove their case and what evidence would disprove it. That converts a positional argument into a set of testable predictions, and it usually reveals that the two hypotheses aren't actually mutually exclusive — a marginal timing margin can be jointly caused by a slow clock edge and firmware that doesn't allow for it.

Then I'd design a test that captures the bus or interface at the moment of failure, with instrumentation both sides trust: a logic analyzer or scope capture that shows the physical signals and the firmware's view simultaneously, ideally with a GPIO marker the firmware toggles at the point it starts and stops waiting. That marker is the Rosetta Stone — it lets you align the firmware's timeline with the electrical timeline and see whether the firmware gave up too early, the hardware was late, or the signal was marginal. I'd also want the failure reproduced under controlled conditions, with temperature or voltage margining if the failure rate is temperature-dependent, so the capture is repeatable rather than a one-off.

On the process side, I'd make it explicit that the goal is a shared root cause, not a verdict, and I'd assign the investigation to a small joint team rather than letting each group investigate in parallel and report back defensively. I'd set a decision point: if the evidence after a defined effort still doesn't separate the hypotheses, we change the design to add margin on both sides rather than continue litigating. That's not a cop-out — in an intermittent failure, adding margin where the evidence is ambiguous is often the correct engineering answer, provided you document the reasoning and verify the fix against the original symptom.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the joint test is inconclusive because the failure won't reproduce in the lab? What would you document so the corrective action is traceable back to the evidence rather than to whoever argued loudest?

## Q5: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** A consistent offset that appears only in the assembled, installed configuration is a strong signal that the change is mechanical or environmental rather than electrical in the conventional sense — something about the assembly is altering a physical condition the front-end depends on. I'd start by enumerating what changes between the two states: cable routing and length, grounding and shielding connections made or broken by the enclosure, mechanical stress on the PCB or on the sensor, thermal environment, and the proximity of the front-end to other conductors or magnetic materials. The fact that it's consistent and repeatable is helpful — it means I can measure it, and I can bisect the assembly steps to find which one introduces it.

The most efficient approach is a build-up test: take a unit that exhibits the offset, and progressively disassemble it — remove the enclosure, then the mounting, then restore the bench harness — measuring the offset at each step. The step where the offset disappears names the contributing factor. In parallel I'd check the obvious candidates: whether the enclosure is creating a ground loop or a shield connection that shifts the reference, whether mounting hardware is stressing the board and changing a solder joint or a connector's contact resistance, and whether the installed position changes the thermal profile enough to shift a component's offset voltage. A small consistent offset in an instrumentation amplifier front-end is very often a reference or bias issue, and a changed ground reference from the enclosure is a classic cause.

I'd also verify the offset is truly in the front-end and not in the digitization or the reference: measure the actual analog voltage at the amplifier output in both configurations, not just the firmware's reported value. If the analog output is offset, the cause is upstream; if it's not, the cause is in the ADC reference or the digital path. And I'd want to know whether the offset scales with the input signal (a gain error) or is constant (an offset error), because that distinguishes a reference problem from a gain-setting component being affected. The discipline is the same as anywhere: change one assembly variable at a time, measure at the same point each time, and don't accept "it went away" without knowing which step made it go away.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset turns out to be caused by mechanical stress on a solder joint, how would you confirm that non-destructively? How would you decide whether the right fix is a design change or a manufacturing/assembly change?
