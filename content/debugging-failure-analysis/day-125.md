# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 125

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally enters a hard fault handler, and the stack trace consistently points to a memory copy operation — but the source and destination addresses are always valid, and the memory regions being copied are within their allocated bounds?

**Answer:** When the faulting instruction is a memory copy and the addresses and bounds all check out, the fault is almost never about the copy itself — it's about the *state* the copy runs in. I'd treat the memcpy as a symptom and widen the lens in three directions.

First, alignment and access width. A copy routine that uses word- or double-word accesses on a buffer that is only byte-aligned can fault on some cores even when the address range is legal. I'd check the actual fault status register bits (unaligned access, bus fault vs. memory management fault) rather than trusting the high-level stack trace, and confirm the compiler's memcpy implementation and whether it assumes alignment.

Second, the source of the pointer. Valid-looking addresses can be *stale* — a buffer that was freed, a stack frame that has already returned, or a pointer into a region that has been remapped or whose clock/power was gated. I'd look at whether the copy is running on a stack that has overflowed (the destination may be "valid" but actually overwriting the stack guard or another frame), and whether the fault is really a stack overflow masquerading as a memcpy fault. Checking the stack high-water mark and enabling stack canaries or an MPU region on the stack is a good way to confirm.

Third, concurrency. If the copy runs in an ISR or a second task while another context is mutating the buffer or the length, the length can change between the bounds check and the copy — a classic TOCTOU. I'd look for shared buffers without proper critical sections, and for DMA that is still in flight touching the same region.

The systematic approach: reproduce under a debugger with the fault registers captured, single-step the copy, and correlate the faulting address against the linker map and the task/ISR context. Then instrument — log the pointer values, the length, and the calling context at the moment of the copy — so the next occurrence is self-documenting rather than requiring a lucky reproduction.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine stack overflow from a stale-pointer fault when both present as a memcpy fault? What would you change in the build (MPU, canaries, fault handlers) to make the next occurrence diagnosable without a debugger attached?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and loop-stability problem, so I'd separate "the regulator can't respond fast enough" from "the ripple is coupling in from somewhere else."

First, characterize the ripple properly. Where am I probing? Ripple must be measured at the decoupling capacitors with a short ground lead (a spring-tip probe, not the long ground clip), because a long ground lead picks up radiated switching noise and inflates the reading. I'd capture the ripple with the subsystem switching and confirm its frequency content — is it at the regulator's switching frequency, at the subsystem's switching/chopping frequency, or a beat between them? That single observation splits the diagnosis.

If the ripple is at the regulator's own switching frequency and grows with load steps, I'd look at loop response: output capacitance (value, ESR, and whether it's within the regulator's stable range), the feedforward/compensation network, and whether the transient dips below the point where the error amplifier saturates. A load-transient step test with a known current step tells me the actual transient response versus the datasheet.

If the ripple is at the subsystem's frequency, it's coupling — either conducted through a shared impedance (common ground return, shared input rail) or radiated. I'd check the return path: is the high-current subsystem's ground return sharing a trace or plane with the sensitive rail's reference? Adding local bulk capacitance at the load and tightening the return path often resolves conducted coupling. For radiated, a near-field probe sweep over the board localizes the coupling path.

Throughout, one change at a time: add bulk cap at the load, re-measure; adjust compensation, re-measure. I'd also verify the ripple isn't an artifact of the probe setup before chasing a design change.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you tell a loop-instability oscillation from a load-transient droop on a scope capture? If adding bulk capacitance at the load fixes it, what does that tell you about the root cause versus just masking it?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's battery charging circuit draws excessive current from the wall adapter only when the device is connected to a specific model of USB charger, but charges normally with the charger that ships with the device?

**Answer:** The device behaving differently across chargers points at the *interface negotiation and source characteristics*, not the charging circuit itself — so I'd resist the urge to re-spin the charger IC and instead characterize what's different about that charger.

First, what does that charger present? USB chargers differ in their advertised current capability (DCP vs. CDP vs. SDP signaling on D+/D-), their actual current-limit behavior, and their output voltage regulation and cable drop. I'd measure the adapter's output voltage and current at the device's input under both chargers, and check what the device's USB interface detects — if the device mis-detects the charger type and requests more current than the source can supply, the source may fold back its voltage, and the charger IC then draws more current trying to maintain charge current. That's a plausible mechanism for "excessive current from the adapter."

Second, inrush and startup. Some adapters have slow soft-start or limited bulk capacitance; the device's input capacitance plus the charger's startup can cause a current spike that trips the adapter's protection or causes it to hiccup. I'd scope the input current at plug-in on both chargers.

Third, the charger IC's input-current-limit (ILIM) and dynamic power-path behavior. If the device sets its input current limit based on a detected adapter capability, a mis-detection directly causes over-draw. I'd verify the ILIM resistor/programming and the detection logic against the USB battery-charging spec.

The corrective direction depends on root cause: fix the detection/negotiation so the device never requests more than the source advertises, add input current limiting that is independent of detection, or add input bulk/soft-start. But the investigation has to establish *which* mechanism before touching the design.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the device's charger-type detection is correct without a USB protocol analyzer? If the adapter is folding back its voltage, how would that show up on a scope at the device input?

## Q4: How would you approach a failure investigation where a medical device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The bench-to-installed delta is the whole story here, so I'd enumerate what changes between those two states and test each variable in isolation. The candidates are: mechanical (enclosure, mounting, cable routing, connector mating), thermal (self-heating in the closed enclosure), electrical (grounding/reference changes, shield connections, added cable capacitance/inductance), and environmental (proximity to other equipment, the mounting surface).

First, I'd reproduce the offset with the unit assembled but *not* installed, then installed but with the enclosure open, to separate "enclosure" from "mounting position." That bisection usually narrows it fast.

A consistent offset (not noise, not drift) most often points to a reference or bias change: the analog ground reference shifting because the enclosure or mounting introduces a new ground path, or the sensor's excitation/reference being loaded differently once the real cable and connector are in the loop. I'd measure the reference voltage and the front-end's input bias at the ADC pins in both states — if the reference moves, the offset is a reference problem, not a gain or sensor problem.

If it's thermal, the offset would track warm-up; a *consistent* offset that appears immediately on assembly points more to a DC path change. I'd also check for mechanical stress on the sensor or connector — a connector that mates slightly differently in the enclosure can change contact resistance in a bridge circuit.

The discipline is: change one variable at a time (open/closed, mounted/unmounted, real cable/harness), measure at the ADC input each time, and only then form a hypothesis. Guessing at the fix before localizing the variable wastes cycles.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell a ground-reference shift from a sensor bridge imbalance when both produce a fixed offset? What would you measure first if the offset only appears after the unit has been installed for several minutes?

## Q5: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When the team is split, the trap is to argue about which hypothesis is *more likely* — that's a debate, not an investigation. I'd reframe it as: what is the cheapest, fastest test that would *distinguish* between the two, and can we run both in parallel?

Concretely, I'd write down each hypothesis with its predicted observable: "If cause A is true, then under condition X we should see Y; if cause B is true, we should see Z." That converts opinion into a falsifiable test. Often one hypothesis predicts something the other doesn't, and a single experiment settles it. If both remain viable, I'd assign an owner to each with a bounded time-box and a defined decision criterion, so the split doesn't stall the team — parallel tracks with a scheduled convergence point.

I'd also make sure the split isn't actually a communication problem: sometimes two teams "disagree" because they're looking at different evidence sets or different failure instances. Putting all the data on one wall and agreeing on what is *established* versus *assumed* frequently collapses the disagreement.

On the human side, I'd be explicit that the goal is not to pick a winner but to converge on the true cause, and that a hypothesis being wrong is a normal, useful outcome — that keeps the senior engineer who's attached to one theory from feeling personally overruled. I'd set a decision point: if the distinguishing test is inconclusive by a certain date, we escalate to a more invasive test (destructive analysis, fault injection) rather than continuing to accumulate circumstantial evidence.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the distinguishing test is expensive or destructive and the schedule won't allow it? How do you keep a team member who "lost" the hypothesis debate engaged and productive afterward?
