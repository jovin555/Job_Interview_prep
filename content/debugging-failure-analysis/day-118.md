# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 118

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally fails to complete a flash write operation, and the failure rate increases as the device's internal temperature rises — but the flash device is rated well above the operating temperature range?

**Answer:** The datasheet rating is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, so I'd treat this as a system-level margin problem rather than assuming the flash part is defective. First I'd reproduce it deterministically: put the unit in a thermal chamber, sweep temperature in steps, and log write success/failure with timestamps and the measured die/board temperature at each attempt. That converts a vague "increases with temperature" into a curve I can reason about.

Then I'd separate the three things that actually change with temperature: the flash's own timing margins (program/erase time lengthens as the cell wears and as temperature moves away from the characterized point), the supply rail feeding it (LDO or regulator dropout and transient response degrade hot), and the bus timing between the MCU and the flash (clock and data setup/hold margins shrink as driver strength and trace impedance shift). I'd scope the supply rail and the SPI/QSPI lines at the flash pins during a write at the failing temperature — probing at the pin, not at the regulator output, because a marginal decoupling cap or a long return path shows up there and not at the source.

A common finding is that the write is issued while the rail is sagging during a concurrent high-current event, or that the firmware doesn't honor the flash's busy/ready status long enough at temperature, so it starts the next operation before the internal state machine is ready. I'd also check whether the write is being done inside an interrupt context where it can be preempted mid-sequence. The fix is usually margin restoration — better local decoupling, a longer or status-polled wait, reduced concurrent load during writes, or a lower write clock — rather than swapping the part. And I'd verify the fix by re-running the temperature sweep with margin beyond the spec limits, not just to the limit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a flash-endurance/wear effect from a pure temperature effect if both are present? What would you log in firmware to make this reproducible in the field rather than only in the chamber?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and loop-stability problem, so I'd start by characterizing it properly rather than guessing at components. I'd measure the rail at the load's decoupling capacitors with a short-ground-tip probe or a coax pigtail — a long ground lead picks up loop inductance and shows ripple that isn't really there. I'd capture the transient with the scope triggered on the subsystem's enable or switching node, so I can see the dip and recovery in the time domain and correlate it with the exact moment the load steps.

Then I'd separate the causes: is the ripple at the regulator's switching frequency (a loop stability or output-cap ESR issue), at the load's own switching frequency (coupling through the shared impedance of the rail), or a transient response that simply can't slew fast enough for the step (insufficient bulk capacitance or too much ESR/ESL between the regulator and the load)? I'd look at the regulator's phase margin indirectly — a transient that rings or overshoots on recovery suggests marginal stability, which often means the output cap is outside the regulator's recommended ESR window or the feedforward cap is wrong.

I'd also check the return path: if the high-current subsystem's ground return shares impedance with the sensitive rail's reference, the "ripple" is really ground bounce. Adding a local bulk cap right at the load, tightening the return, or adding a small series ferrite with local decoupling often fixes it — but I'd confirm with measurement before changing the BOM, and re-measure at the load pins after each change, one change at a time.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell a loop-stability problem from a decoupling problem using only scope captures? What's your approach if the ripple is acceptable at the regulator but excessive at the load?

## Q3: A medical device passes all functional and safety tests at design verification, but during a 30-unit pilot production run, three units fail a 72-hour burn-in test with the same symptom: the device stops communicating after roughly 48 hours and requires a power cycle to recover. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Three out of thirty with an identical symptom and a consistent time-to-failure is a strong signal — it's not random, and the fact that it appears only in burn-in and only after ~48 hours points to something that accumulates: thermal soak, a counter or buffer that fills, a leak, or a marginal timing that only manifests after sustained operation. I'd resist the urge to declare it a "bad batch" and instead treat the pilot as an accelerated reliability test that's doing its job.

First I'd preserve the failing units in their failed state — don't power-cycle them before capturing whatever state they hold. Pull logs, check whether the communication stack is hung, the MCU is in a fault handler, or the peripheral has locked up. If there's a debug port, attach without resetting and read the state. Then I'd try to reproduce on the bench with a soak test that mirrors the burn-in profile, and instrument it: current draw over time, die temperature, heap/stack high-water marks, and a watchdog or heartbeat counter that survives the hang.

The "requires a power cycle" detail is important — it suggests a latched condition, not a crash that would reset on its own. That could be a peripheral that's entered an error state the firmware doesn't recover from, a communication bus that's hung with no recovery routine, or a thermal fold-back that never releases. I'd also compare the three failing units against the passing ones for any build difference — component lot, assembly date, firmware version — because a pilot run is exactly where process variation shows up. The corrective action has to be verified by re-running the full burn-in on a larger sample, and I'd want the fix to include a recovery path so a single hang doesn't require a user to power-cycle a medical device.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to root-cause this before shipping or ship with a recovery mechanism and investigate in parallel? What would you add to the burn-in test to catch this earlier next time?

## Q4: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The deadlock usually isn't a technical disagreement — it's that each team is testing its own layer in isolation and neither has a shared, observable definition of the failure. So my first move is to reframe the problem: get both teams to agree on a single reproducible failure signature and a single measurement that captures it, before anyone argues about cause. If we can't reproduce it on demand, that becomes the first joint task — build a stress harness that maximizes the failure rate, and agree on what "failed" looks like at the wire level.

Then I'd instrument the boundary itself — the physical bus between the two domains — with a logic analyzer or scope capture that both teams trust, triggered on the failure. That gives a neutral record: was the transaction issued, was the response late, was the line marginal, was the clock clean? Once you have the wire-level truth, the argument usually collapses because the evidence points to one side of the boundary. I'd assign each team a falsifiable hypothesis and a specific test that could disprove it, rather than letting them defend positions.

I'd also set up a short cadence — daily or every-other-day — with a shared log of what was tested and what was ruled out, so progress is visible and nobody re-litigates settled points. If the evidence genuinely points to an interaction (marginal timing that only fails under a particular firmware access pattern), I'd frame the fix as a joint one and make sure both teams own part of the verification. The goal is to make the investigation data-driven and shared, not to adjudicate who's right.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the wire-level capture is ambiguous and both hypotheses remain plausible? How do you keep the teams collaborative rather than defensive when one side's hypothesis is disproven?

## Q5: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** A consistent offset that appears only in the assembled, installed state is almost always a mechanical or environmental coupling effect rather than an electronics defect — the electronics didn't change, so something about the physical configuration did. I'd start by reproducing it and then bisecting the configuration: bench with harness, board in enclosure but not installed, enclosure installed but not fastened, fully installed. That isolates whether it's the enclosure itself (shielding, grounding, a cable routed differently), the mounting (mechanical stress on the board or connector, a ground path through the chassis), or the installation environment (a nearby field, a different reference potential).

A small consistent offset often traces to a ground reference shift — the analog front-end's reference is tied to a point that, once the board is fastened to a chassis or a cable shield is connected, sits at a slightly different potential than on the bench. I'd measure the reference and the signal return at the ADC pins in each configuration, and check whether the offset tracks the chassis connection. Mechanical stress on a connector or a flexing board can also shift a solder joint or a sensor's mounting, which shows up as a DC offset.

I'd also check for thermal effects — an assembled unit runs warmer, and a small offset that's actually temperature-driven can look like an assembly effect if you don't control for it. Once I've isolated the configuration variable, the fix is usually a grounding/reference change, a strain relief, or a shielding change, and I'd verify by measuring the offset across multiple units in the final installed configuration, not just the one that failed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a ground-reference shift from a genuine sensor offset in this scenario? What would you change in the design review process to catch this class of issue before assembly?
