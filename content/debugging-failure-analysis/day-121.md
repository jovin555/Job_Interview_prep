# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 121

## Q1: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's firmware occasionally fails to complete a flash write operation, and the failure rate increases as the device's internal temperature rises — but the flash device is rated well above the operating temperature range?

**Answer:** The first move is to separate "the flash part is out of spec" from "the conditions around the flash part are out of spec." A part rated to 85°C or 105°C ambient can still fail writes if the local conditions at the die — supply voltage, clock stability, or the write timing itself — degrade before the ambient limit is reached. So I'd instrument rather than assume: log the actual supply rail at the flash device during writes across the temperature ramp, capture the write-enable and clock lines with a scope, and correlate any failed write with the state of those signals at the moment of failure. Temperature-dependent write failures often trace to a marginal decoupling or bulk capacitance issue where ESR rises with temperature, a supply that sags during the write current burst, or a timing margin that shrinks as the internal oscillator drifts. I'd also check whether the failure is truly a write failure or a verify failure — the firmware's error path matters here, because a write that succeeds but fails readback points at a different root cause than a write that times out. Once I have the failing condition reproduced on a thermal chamber with logging, I'd bisect: hold temperature constant and vary supply, then hold supply constant and vary temperature, to see which variable actually drives the failure rate. The datasheet rating is a starting point for the part, not a guarantee about the system around it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine flash cell wear-out effect from a board-level thermal issue if the units have different write-cycle histories? What would you change in the firmware's write/verify sequence to make the failure more diagnosable in the field?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** This is a load-transient and loop-response problem, so I'd start by characterizing the disturbance rather than the symptom. I'd probe the rail directly at the decoupling capacitors with a short-ground-tip probe — not at the regulator output pin, and not with a long ground lead, because both will lie to you about high-frequency content. I'd capture the switching edge of the high-current subsystem and look at the rail's response: how far it deviates, how fast it recovers, and whether the ripple is at the subsystem's switching frequency, at the regulator's own switching frequency, or at some beat between them. That distinction tells me whether I'm looking at insufficient bulk capacitance, a control-loop bandwidth that's too slow to respond to the transient, or a layout issue where the high-current return path shares impedance with the sensitive rail. I'd also check whether the ripple appears on the input side of the regulator as well as the output — if the disturbance is coupling through the input, the fix is different from an output-side decoupling problem. From there it's one change at a time: add bulk capacitance locally at the load, then check loop compensation, then examine the return path and grounding. I'd verify each change with the same measurement setup so I'm comparing like with like, and I'd confirm the fix holds across the full load range, not just the one operating point that reproduced the problem.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a decoupling problem and a regulator loop-stability problem from the scope capture alone? What's your approach if adding bulk capacitance reduces the ripple amplitude but doesn't eliminate a fast transient droop?

## Q3: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate on the bench with a test harness, but shows a small, consistent offset only when the enclosure is fully assembled and the unit is installed in its final mounting position?

**Answer:** The bench-to-assembled delta is the whole investigation, so I'd treat the enclosure and mounting as variables to be isolated rather than as background. First I'd reproduce the offset with the unit assembled but on the bench, then progressively remove variables: enclosure open vs. closed, unit in final mounting orientation vs. flat, cable routing in its production position vs. loose. The offset being small and consistent is a useful clue — it suggests a systematic effect like a ground reference shift, a thermal gradient from a nearby component, or mechanical stress on a sensor or connector, rather than a random noise problem. I'd measure the analog front-end's reference and ground at the point of conversion with the unit in each configuration, because a consistent offset that appears only when assembled often traces to a ground return path that changes when the chassis or mounting hardware is connected. I'd also check whether the offset correlates with warm-up time, since a closed enclosure changes the thermal environment. If the offset tracks a specific assembly step, I'd narrow to that step; if it tracks mounting orientation, I'd look at mechanical stress or gravity-dependent connector seating. The goal is to find the one variable that flips the behavior, then confirm it by reversing it.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the offset turns out to be a ground-reference shift caused by the chassis connection, how would you correct it without compromising the safety grounding required for a medical device? How would you design a test that proves the fix holds across units, not just the one you debugged?

## Q4: You're leading a cross-functional investigation where the firmware and hardware teams each believe the other side owns an intermittent communication failure, and neither has conclusive evidence. How would you structure the investigation to break the deadlock?

**Answer:** The deadlock usually comes from each team testing in isolation and finding their own side clean, so the first thing I'd do is stop the parallel finger-pointing and define a single shared experiment that neither team can run alone. I'd frame it as a falsifiable question — for example, "at the moment of failure, is the bus electrically valid?" — and design a measurement that captures both domains simultaneously: a logic analyzer or scope on the bus lines, time-correlated with firmware state markers toggled on a spare GPIO at known points in the driver code. That gives one dataset that shows whether the hardware was signaling correctly when the firmware decided it had failed, or whether the firmware was in the wrong state when the hardware was fine. I'd also make the failure reproducible on demand if at all possible, because an intermittent that only shows up in the field can't settle an argument. If it can't be reproduced, I'd add instrumentation to the next field return or to a stressed unit and let the data decide. The key leadership move is to make the investigation about the evidence rather than about whose component is at fault, and to get both teams to agree in advance on what result would vindicate each hypothesis — so when the data comes in, it's already accepted as decisive.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the shared experiment shows the failure is genuinely a timing interaction that spans both domains, so neither team is solely responsible? How do you keep the teams collaborating once the root cause is assigned to one side?

## Q5: A junior engineer on your team has been debugging an intermittent failure for several days without progress — they've been testing components in isolation and everything passes, but the system-level failure persists. They're frustrated and the schedule is tight. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** The pattern here — components pass in isolation, system fails — usually means the engineer is testing the wrong thing, not that they're not working hard. So my first step is to reframe the problem with them rather than take it over: ask what the failure looks like at the system level, what conditions reproduce it, and what they've already ruled out. Often the gap is that isolation testing removes the very interaction that causes the failure, so I'd steer toward reproducing the failure in the full system first, then bisecting by removing one subsystem or condition at a time while keeping the rest intact. I'd also check whether they have a reliable reproduction at all — if the failure is intermittent and they can't trigger it on demand, the priority is instrumentation and stress conditions, not more component testing. On the human side, I'd normalize the frustration, because days on an intermittent is a normal part of this work, and I'd make sure they're not alone in it — pairing on the next experiment or reviewing their test log with them. With schedule pressure, I'd help them timebox: agree on what a decisive experiment looks like, run it, and let the result redirect the effort rather than letting them grind on the same approach. The goal is to get them to a better method, not to rescue them.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when to step in and lead the debugging yourself versus continuing to coach? What would you put in place so the next intermittent failure on the team doesn't consume several days before someone asks for help?
