# debugging-failure-analysis — Day 102

## Q1: How would you approach debugging a device that passes every functional test on the bench but fails only when powered from its internal battery, at the same nominal voltage as the bench supply?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is stop treating "same nominal voltage" as "same electrical environment," because it almost never is. A bench supply is a low-impedance, well-regulated source with a short, thick cable; a battery pack is a higher-impedance source with protection circuitry, a fuel gauge, and a connector in the path. So I'd start by characterizing the difference rather than the symptom.

Concretely: I'd measure the rail voltage at the load — not at the supply terminals — under both conditions, with a scope and a short ground lead, looking at DC level, transient droop under load steps, and ripple. I'd compare the battery path's series resistance and any protection FET or fuse drop against the bench path. Then I'd look at what else changes when the unit runs on battery: power-path switching, charger IC state, any load switches or regulators that are bypassed or enabled differently, and firmware power-management modes that may only engage on battery. A very common class of bug here is a brownout or reset event during a load transient that the bench supply's bulk capacitance masks, or a GPIO/regulator enable that's driven differently depending on source.

I'd also check whether the failure is really "on battery" or "not on bench supply" — for example, whether the bench setup is back-powering a node through a protection diode or providing a ground path the battery configuration doesn't. Divide and conquer: reproduce on battery, then substitute a bench supply *through the battery connector* with realistic series resistance and cable length. If it still passes, the difference is in the battery pack or its management; if it fails, the difference is source impedance and transient response. That single experiment usually splits the problem in half.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you emulate a battery's source impedance and transient behavior on the bench without the actual pack? What would you look for in the firmware's power-management state machine that could differ between the two sources?

## Q2: You're debugging a power rail that shows excessive ripple only when a high-current subsystem is switching, but the ripple is within specification when that subsystem is idle. How would you approach this?

**Answer:** Ripple that appears only under a switching load points at the interaction between the load's current transients and the power delivery network, not at the regulator's steady-state regulation. I'd approach it in layers: source, path, and load.

First, measure properly. Ripple measurements are easy to get wrong — I'd use a short ground spring or a coaxial probe tip right at the decoupling capacitors, not a long ground clip, and I'd confirm the probe isn't picking up radiated switching noise. I'd capture the ripple synchronized to the load's switching event so I can see whether it's a transient droop that recovers, a sustained oscillation, or high-frequency ringing.

Then I'd separate the mechanisms. A droop that tracks the load step and recovers is a transient-response/loop-bandwidth or bulk-capacitance issue. A sustained oscillation suggests control-loop instability or insufficient phase margin at that load condition. High-frequency ringing at the edges suggests parasitic inductance in the path — layout, via count, capacitor placement — or a resonance between the bulk and ceramic capacitors. I'd check the regulator's load-transient response in isolation with an electronic load stepping at the same di/dt, which tells me whether the regulator alone can handle the step.

On the path side, I'd look at the impedance between the regulator output and the load: trace width, vias, connector resistance, and whether the high-current subsystem shares a return path with sensitive circuitry. On the load side, I'd check whether the subsystem's own input capacitance is adequate and whether its switching edges are as fast as they need to be. The fix is usually a combination — more bulk or better-placed ceramics near the load, improved return path, or slowing the load's edges — but I'd want the measurement to tell me which mechanism dominates before changing anything.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a control-loop stability problem from a PDN impedance problem using measurements alone? What's your process for deciding whether to fix this in hardware or by sequencing/slowing the load in firmware?

## Q3: A device's firmware occasionally reports a sensor value that is plausible but wrong — not out of range, not flagged by any self-test, just incorrect — and it's only caught later when the data is reviewed offline. How would you approach this investigation?

**Answer:** This is the hardest class of bug because every layer's self-checks pass — the value is in range, so range checks don't catch it, and the error is only visible against ground truth. My approach is to first make the failure observable, then work backward from the data path.

The key move is to establish an independent reference at multiple points in the chain and log them together with timestamps. If I can capture the raw ADC output, the post-processing value, and the transmitted value in the same record, I can localize whether the corruption happens at conversion, in the processing math, in the buffer/memory, or in the transport. Without that, I'm guessing. I'd add temporary instrumentation — a debug stream or a ring buffer — that captures the raw samples around the event, ideally with a trigger condition based on disagreement between two independent paths.

Then I'd think about what "plausible but wrong" implies. It rules out gross saturation, open circuits, and out-of-range faults. It's consistent with: a stale sample being used (timing/race), a buffer being read while partially updated (concurrency), a scaling or calibration coefficient applied incorrectly under some condition, a single-bit error in memory or transport, or a sample taken during a transient that the analog front-end hadn't settled from. I'd look for correlation with operating state — temperature, supply mode, other subsystem activity, time since boot — because a plausible-but-wrong value that correlates with a state change is a strong clue.

I'd also check the review process itself: how is "wrong" being determined offline? If it's against a reference measurement, I need to understand the reference's own uncertainty before chasing a small discrepancy. And I'd want to reproduce it — fault injection, accelerated cycling, or replaying the logged conditions — because an intermittent bug that can't be reproduced can only be bounded, not fixed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the logging so it doesn't perturb the very timing you're trying to observe? If the corruption turns out to be a single-bit flip, how would you distinguish a memory issue from a transport issue from a cosmic-ray-style soft error?

## Q4: You're leading a failure investigation and you've reached a point where two plausible root causes remain, both supported by partial evidence, and the team is split on which to pursue. How would you handle this and drive the investigation to a conclusion?

**Answer:** When two hypotheses both have partial support, the trap is to let the team argue about which is more likely. I'd reframe the question from "which is right?" to "what evidence would discriminate between them?" — and then design the cheapest, fastest experiment that produces that discriminating evidence.

Practically, I'd write down both hypotheses explicitly, with the evidence for and against each, and identify the specific prediction where they diverge. If hypothesis A is a marginal timing violation and hypothesis B is a firmware race, they predict different behavior under temperature, under load, or under a controlled timing perturbation — so I'd design a test that stresses that difference. I'd also consider whether the two are actually mutually exclusive; in real systems, a marginal hardware condition and a firmware race often combine, and the "root cause" may be the interaction. I'd be open to that rather than forcing a single winner.

On the team side, I'd make the process fair and evidence-driven so nobody feels their hypothesis was dismissed on opinion. I'd assign each camp to define the test that would falsify their own hypothesis — people are more rigorous when they're trying to break their own theory. I'd set a time-box and a decision criterion up front: "if we see X, we pursue A; if Y, we pursue B; if neither, we widen the search." That keeps the investigation moving and prevents it from stalling on consensus.

Finally, I'd document the reasoning and the decision, because in a regulated medical context the traceability from evidence to conclusion matters as much as the conclusion itself. If the discriminating test is inconclusive, I'd rather implement a conservative mitigation that covers both mechanisms while continuing the investigation than ship a fix based on a coin flip.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test is expensive or takes weeks, and the schedule won't allow it? How do you keep the losing camp engaged and productive after the decision?

## Q5: How would you approach a failure investigation where a device's analog front-end is accurate at room temperature but shows a growing offset error as the device warms up during continuous operation — and the offset returns to zero after the device cools down?

**Answer:** A reversible, temperature-correlated offset is a strong signal that I'm looking at a thermal effect in the analog signal chain, and the reversibility tells me it's not permanent damage — it's a parameter shift. I'd approach it by first mapping the error against temperature rather than against time, because "warms up during operation" is really a temperature curve, and I want to know whether the offset tracks junction temperature, board temperature, or a specific component's temperature.

The measurement plan: instrument the unit with thermocouples or a thermal camera to find the hot spots, and log the offset alongside temperature at several points — the sensor, the instrumentation amplifier, the ADC reference, and the board near the power components. If the offset correlates tightly with one component's temperature, that narrows the suspect list fast. I'd also check the obvious thermal mechanisms: input offset voltage drift of the amplifier, gain drift of the front-end resistors, reference voltage drift, thermocouple effects at solder joints or connectors where dissimilar metals meet, and mechanical stress on the sensor from CTE mismatch as the board heats.

A key discriminator is whether the offset is in the analog domain or introduced digitally. If I can measure the amplifier output directly and compare it to the ADC's digital output, I can tell whether the drift is upstream or in the conversion/reference. I'd also test with the sensor replaced by a fixed resistor or a stable reference source — if the offset still appears, it's in the electronics; if it disappears, it's in the sensor or its interface.

Then I'd verify against datasheets: is the observed drift within the components' specified temperature coefficients, or is it larger? If it's within spec but still unacceptable, the issue is a design margin problem — the error budget didn't account for the thermal sum. If it's outside spec, I'd suspect a specific component or a layout issue like a thermal gradient across a matched pair. The corrective action differs completely between those two cases, so I'd want that answer before proposing a fix.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine component drift from a thermocouple effect at a connector? If the drift is within every component's datasheet but still fails the system requirement, how would you approach the error budget and the fix?

**Possible follow-ups (Q5 continued):** What role would a thermal chamber play versus self-heating during normal operation, and how would you separate the two?
