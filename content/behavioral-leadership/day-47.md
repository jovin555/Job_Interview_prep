# behavioral-leadership — Day 47

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a battery management system fault or a charging circuit problem, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd start by framing this as a structured root-cause investigation rather than a debate between two hypotheses. The first step would be to assemble a small cross-functional team — hardware, firmware, and possibly quality — and clearly define the problem statement with objective evidence from the field: what exactly is failing, under what conditions, and with what frequency. I'd then use a fishbone diagram to map out all potential contributing factors across categories like power source, charging environment, battery state, firmware behavior, and mechanical stress. This prevents the team from anchoring too early on either the BMS or the charging circuit.

Next, I'd design a data-collection plan to discriminate between the two hypotheses. This might include reviewing field return data, examining charge/discharge logs if the device records them, and setting up controlled lab reproductions — for example, testing with known-good batteries and known-good chargers in combination with suspect units. The key is to identify measurements that would behave differently depending on which subsystem is at fault. For instance, if the issue is in the charging circuit, you might see abnormal voltage or current profiles during charging; if it's the BMS, you might see issues during discharge or after specific state-of-charge thresholds.

I'd also apply the 5 Whys technique once we have a reproducible failure to drill down to the actual root cause rather than stopping at the first obvious symptom. Throughout the investigation, I'd maintain clear documentation of what was tested, what was ruled out, and why — this is critical in a medical device context because the investigation itself may become part of the corrective action record. Once the root cause is confirmed, I'd separate immediate containment actions from long-term corrective actions, and I'd insist on verification that the fix actually resolves the issue under the original failure conditions before closing the investigation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the two subsystems are owned by different teams that are each convinced the other is at fault? What level of evidence would you require before committing to a corrective action that affects fielded devices?

## Q2: How would you approach building a culture where engineers feel comfortable raising concerns about a design decision early, even when the concern is based on a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated technical argument?

**Answer:** I'd approach this by addressing both the psychological safety aspect and the technical process aspect. On the psychological side, I'd model the behavior myself — openly sharing my own uncertainties and half-formed concerns in design discussions, and explicitly thanking people when they raise issues, even if the issue turns out to be unfounded. I'd also make it clear through my reactions that raising a concern early is valued more than being right, and I'd avoid any pattern of dismissing questions or making people feel like they need to have a complete argument before speaking.

On the process side, I'd introduce a structured way to capture and evaluate early concerns. For example, a design review could include a dedicated "concerns and open questions" section where items are logged without requiring the person raising them to have a proposed solution. Each concern gets assigned an owner and a follow-up action — even if that action is just "investigate and report back next week." This transforms a vague feeling into a tracked item, which signals that the organization takes these inputs seriously. I'd also encourage engineers to validate their gut feelings through quick experiments or calculations — often a half-day of investigation can turn a vague unease into a concrete technical argument.

Finally, I'd work to ensure that raising concerns early is never penalized, even when it leads to rework or schedule impact. If someone raises an issue that causes a design change late in the process, I'd frame that as a success — catching it before release — rather than as a disruption. Over time, this reinforces the message that early concerns are welcomed and acted upon.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where an engineer repeatedly raises concerns that turn out to be unfounded, and the team starts to tune them out? How would you distinguish between a healthy culture of questioning and one where decisions are never finalized because someone always has a doubt?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a risk-balancing problem that needs a test strategy capable of answering the specific question: does the wake-up transition from low-power sleep mode introduce artifacts or instability in the analog sensor readings, and if so, under what conditions? I'd start by bringing both teams together to define exactly what the firmware change involves — what peripherals are powered down, what clock sources are used during sleep, what the wake-up sequence looks like, and how long it takes for the system to become fully operational.

From there, I'd design a test matrix that covers the parameter space of concern. This would include testing across the full range of battery voltages, since the behavior of analog circuits and voltage references can change as the battery depletes. I'd also test at different temperatures, as both the sensor and the power management circuitry may behave differently. The key measurements would be the sensor output during and immediately after wake-up, looking for settling time, overshoot, offset shifts, or noise that could affect measurement accuracy.

I'd also want to characterize the power supply rails during the transition — a scope capture of the supply voltage and current during wake-up can reveal droop or ringing that might couple into the analog front end. If the hardware team's concern is valid, we might see supply transients that occur at the same time as sensor artifacts. I'd suggest adding test points or using an evaluation board to make these measurements accessible.

The test strategy should also include a comparison baseline — measuring sensor performance without the sleep mode enabled, so we can quantify what level of disturbance is actually introduced. This gives us objective data to decide whether the sleep mode is acceptable as-is, needs hardware changes like additional decoupling or a cleaner power switch, or needs firmware changes like a longer settling delay before taking measurements. I'd also recommend running the device through many sleep-wake cycles to catch intermittent issues that might not appear in a single transition.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide what settling time or noise level is acceptable for the sensor readings? How would you handle it if the test results show that the sleep mode causes marginal but measurable degradation that doesn't clearly pass or fail the requirements?

## Q4: How would you approach handling a situation where a regulatory audit is scheduled in two weeks, and you discover that several key design verification test reports are missing signatures or have incomplete data that cannot be re-run in time?

**Answer:** I'd start by immediately assessing the full scope of the problem — which reports are affected, what exactly is missing, and whether the gaps are administrative (like missing signatures) or substantive (like incomplete data that would affect the validity of the test results). This triage is critical because the response strategy differs significantly. Administrative gaps might be resolvable by tracking down the original signatories or following the documented process for late signatures. Substantive gaps are more serious and need a different approach.

For administrative issues, I'd work with the quality team to understand what the documented process allows. Some organizations have provisions for late signatures with appropriate justification, or for a designated alternate to review and sign if the original tester is unavailable. I'd gather whatever supporting evidence exists — email approvals, meeting notes, test data logs — to demonstrate that the work was actually done and reviewed, even if the formal signature wasn't captured at the time.

For substantive data gaps, I'd be honest about what can and cannot be resolved in two weeks. I would not attempt to fabricate or backdate data — that's both unethical and a serious regulatory violation. Instead, I'd document the gap clearly, assess whether the missing data affects the overall conclusion of the test report, and determine if there's any existing data from related tests that could provide supporting evidence. If the gap is significant, I'd prepare a formal statement for the auditor explaining the situation, what was done to address it, and the timeline for completing the missing work. In some cases, it may be appropriate to request a partial audit or to defer the affected verification activity to a follow-up audit.

I'd also use this as a learning opportunity — after the immediate crisis is managed, I'd review how the process failed to catch these gaps earlier and implement checks to prevent recurrence, such as a pre-audit readiness review that verifies documentation completeness before the audit date.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you communicate this issue to the auditor versus to your management — would your approach differ? What would you do if you discovered that a test report had been signed by someone who wasn't actually present during the testing?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical review of a proposed architecture for a new medical device where the system architect has chosen a modular design with multiple microcontrollers communicating over CAN-FD, but several senior engineers believe a single high-performance processor would be simpler and more reliable?

**Answer:** I'd approach this by structuring the review around objective evaluation criteria rather than allowing it to become a debate between architectural philosophies. The first step would be to define what the system actually needs — processing requirements, real-time constraints, safety requirements, power budget, physical size constraints, and the expected lifespan of the product. Both architectures should be evaluated against these requirements, not against abstract notions of simplicity or modularity.

I'd ask the architect to walk through the modular design in detail, explaining the rationale for the partition — what each microcontroller handles, how they communicate, what happens if one fails, and how the CAN-FD bus handles timing and fault tolerance. I'd also ask the proponents of the single-processor approach to articulate their concerns specifically: is it about reliability, complexity of inter-processor communication, debugging difficulty, or something else? Often, these concerns are legitimate but can be addressed within the modular architecture — for example, by defining clear interface contracts or adding diagnostic capabilities.

I'd also want to bring in relevant data points from similar systems. In a medical device context, there are regulatory considerations — a modular architecture might make it easier to isolate safety-critical functions, but it also introduces new failure modes like communication loss between processors. A single processor might simplify the safety case but could create challenges with mixed-criticality software. I'd ask the team to consider how each architecture would be tested and verified, since that's often where the real cost and risk lie.

If the disagreement persists after a thorough technical discussion, I'd suggest a structured decision-making approach — perhaps scoring both architectures against weighted criteria, or building a small proof-of-concept to test the riskiest assumptions of each approach. The goal is to reach a decision based on evidence and analysis rather than conviction. I'd also ensure that whichever architecture is chosen, the decision and its rationale are documented clearly, including the alternatives considered and why they were rejected — this is important for the design history file and for future engineers who will question why the team chose this path.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the architect is senior and well-respected, and the engineers raising concerns are more junior — would that change your approach? What criteria would you use to decide when to escalate the decision to a higher level rather than resolving it within the team?
