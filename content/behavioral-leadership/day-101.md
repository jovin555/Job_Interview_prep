# behavioral-leadership — Day 101

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** I would separate the two problems — the missing justification and the milestone gate — because they have different remedies and different owners. On the missing calculations: a safety-critical decision without documented rationale is not reviewable, regardless of how sound the design itself may be. I would open the meeting by acknowledging the package is late and incomplete, then ask the presenter to walk the reviewers through the decision verbally, capturing the reasoning live in the meeting minutes. That gives the reviewers enough to assess the logic in the room, and it produces a record that can be formalized afterward. I would explicitly assign an action item with an owner and a date to write the calculation up and route it for sign-off, and I would not mark the review closed until that action is verified — the milestone gate can be passed conditionally, but the justification cannot be waived. The key discipline is not letting the urgency of the gate convert an undocumented safety decision into an accepted one by default. If the verbal walkthrough reveals the reasoning is actually weak, that's a finding in its own right and the gate decision changes.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the presenter pushes back that the calculation is "obvious" and doesn't need writing down? What would you do if the milestone owner pressures you to close the review without the action item being completed?

## Q2: How would you approach building a risk management culture on a medical device team that currently treats ISO 14971 documentation as a checkbox exercise done after the design is complete?

**Answer:** The root cause of checkbox risk management is almost always sequencing: if the risk file is produced after the design is frozen, it can only describe what was built, not influence it. So I would attack the sequencing first. I would introduce risk review as an input to design reviews rather than an output of them — before a design decision is approved, the team asks what could go wrong, how severe it would be, and what detects or mitigates it. Practically, that means a short hazard brainstorm at the concept and architecture stages, with entries carried forward and updated as the design matures, so the file grows alongside the design instead of being reconstructed at the end. Second, I would make the connection between risk controls and verification explicit: every mitigation should trace to a test that demonstrates it works, which turns the risk file from paperwork into a test-planning tool the team actually wants. Third, I would model the behavior myself — in reviews, asking "what's the failure mode here and how would we catch it?" as a normal engineering question, not a compliance ritual. Culture changes when the tool starts saving the team from rework, not when they're told it matters.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a team that agrees in principle but reverts to end-of-project risk files under schedule pressure? What's a lightweight way to start this on a project that's already mid-flight?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The firmware team doesn't need the analog design rationale; they need to know what the signal actually looks like at the boundary and what the consequences are for their algorithms. So I would translate the constraint into three things: the effective number of bits or noise floor they can realistically expect at the sampling point, the conditions under which that degrades (temperature, supply ripple, nearby switching, cable length), and the design margin available — meaning how much of the error budget the analog front end has already consumed versus what's left for their filtering and calibration. That framing lets them make real decisions: whether their filter needs more settling time, whether they should oversample, whether a particular feature is feasible at all. I would back it with measured data rather than datasheet numbers where possible — a characterization run on a real board showing noise under representative conditions is worth more than a spec table. And I would keep the detail available but layered: a one-page summary of the constraint and its implications, with the underlying measurements and schematics referenced for anyone who wants to dig in. The failure mode to avoid is handing over a schematic and saying "deal with it," which pushes the analog problem into firmware without giving them the information to solve it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filtering approach would mask a real sensor fault rather than reject noise? What would you do if the measured noise on the board is worse than the design predicted?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** My default is to resolve it within the team, because escalation that could have been avoided erodes the team's ownership and teaches people to route around each other. But there are conditions that make escalation the right call rather than a failure. First, if the disagreement has stopped being technical and become personal — if the two engineers are no longer evaluating each other's arguments on merit — a neutral party above both of them is needed, and that's a legitimate escalation. Second, if the decision has consequences outside the team's authority — it affects budget, schedule commitments, regulatory strategy, or another team's work — then the person who owns those consequences needs to be in the decision, not informed after it. Third, if the team has genuinely exhausted the technical avenues — data has been gathered, a prototype or test has been run, and the disagreement persists — then the decision needs to be made at a level with the authority to accept the residual risk, and that should be documented with the rationale for why the losing option was set aside. Before escalating, I would make sure both engineers agree on what the disagreement actually is and what evidence would settle it, because a surprising number of escalations dissolve once the question is stated precisely.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if one of the engineers escalates over your head before you've had a chance to resolve it? What would you document after an escalated decision so the team doesn't relitigate it?

## Q5: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** Deference in the room is usually not a confidence problem — it's a practice problem. Someone who has always executed against decisions others made has never built the muscle of defending a choice under scrutiny, so I would give them structured, low-stakes reps before the high-stakes ones. Concretely: assign them ownership of a bounded design decision with a clear scope — a subsystem, a component selection, a test approach — where they are the one who presents the trade-offs and makes the call, and where I deliberately do not weigh in first. The order of speaking in a review matters enormously here; if the senior person speaks first, the decision is effectively made, so I would arrange for them to present before anyone senior comments. I would also coach the specific skill of stating a position with its rationale and its known weaknesses — "I chose X because of A and B, and I accept the cost of C" — because that's the sentence they've never had to say. And I would debrief afterward, asking them to assess their own decision rather than telling them what I thought, so the judgment develops in them rather than being borrowed from me. Over time the goal is that they stop checking whether the senior person agrees and start checking whether the evidence supports them.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a decision they own turns out badly — how do you debrief without making them more risk-averse? What would you do if a senior engineer keeps overriding their decisions in reviews despite the arrangement?
