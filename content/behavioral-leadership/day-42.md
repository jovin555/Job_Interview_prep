# behavioral-leadership — Day 42

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a battery management system fault or a charging circuit problem, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd start by establishing a structured investigation framework rather than letting the team gravitate toward whichever cause seems more likely. First, I'd assemble a small cross-functional team — hardware, firmware, and test — and clearly define the problem statement with all available data: field reports, device logs, failure rates, and any environmental conditions. I'd then use a fishbone diagram to map all plausible causal categories, ensuring we don't prematurely narrow the investigation. For the two leading hypotheses, I'd design a set of discriminating tests that can isolate each path — for example, bench-level characterization of the charging circuit under various input conditions, and independent monitoring of the battery management system's state-of-charge estimation and protection thresholds. I'd also look for any data that's already available from returned units, such as charge cycle counts, voltage/current logs, or fault registers. The key is to avoid fixing either cause until the evidence clearly points one way. I'd document every finding, even negative ones, because in a medical device context that documentation becomes part of the corrective action record. Once the root cause is confirmed, I'd apply the appropriate corrective action — whether that's a hardware revision to the charging circuit or a firmware update to the battery management algorithm — and then verify the fix under conditions that reproduce the original failure.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the two hypotheses require different teams (hardware vs. firmware) and those teams are already pointing fingers at each other?
- What would you do if the field data is insufficient to discriminate between the two causes, and you need to make a decision about whether to recall or continue shipping the device?

## Q2: How would you approach building a technical leadership roadmap for a team that is transitioning from a single-product medical device focus to a multi-project portfolio, where engineers are used to deep specialization rather than cross-project flexibility?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a structured transition that respects the team's existing strengths while deliberately building new capabilities. First, I'd assess the current skill matrix — who has deep expertise in which areas, and where there are adjacent skills that could be cross-trained. I'd then map the upcoming project portfolio to identify which skills will be needed across multiple projects, and where there are gaps. Rather than forcing everyone to become generalists overnight, I'd create a phased plan: start by pairing specialists from different projects on shared design reviews, so they learn each other's domains through exposure. I'd also establish reusable design guidelines and modular reference designs that reduce the need for every engineer to reinvent solutions for common blocks like power management, sensor interfaces, or communication stacks. For the cultural shift, I'd introduce a lightweight knowledge-sharing cadence — technical lunch-and-learns, design review cross-participation, and a shared component library with documented rationale. I'd also be explicit about career development: some engineers will thrive on cross-project flexibility, while others may prefer to remain deep specialists. The roadmap should accommodate both paths rather than forcing a single model. Finally, I'd set clear expectations that while specialization is valued, the team needs enough redundancy that no single person becomes a bottleneck across multiple projects.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a senior engineer who resists the transition and wants to remain a deep specialist in one area?
- What metrics would you use to track whether the transition is succeeding?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a system-level test strategy that addresses both the battery life benefit and the sensor stability risk, rather than letting the two teams argue about which concern takes priority. First, I'd define the acceptance criteria for both dimensions: what battery life improvement is required, and what sensor accuracy or settling time is acceptable after wake-up. I'd then design a test matrix that covers the full wake-up transition — not just steady-state operation — because that's where the risk lies. This would include: repeated wake/sleep cycles at various intervals, temperature extremes, and different battery voltage levels, since low battery can affect both the power rail behavior and the sensor reference. I'd also instrument the device to capture both the analog sensor output and the power rail during the transition, so we can correlate any instability with its cause. For the firmware side, I'd test different wake-up sequences — for example, allowing the analog front-end to settle before taking a measurement, versus taking an immediate reading. I'd also consider adding a hardware test point or debug mode that allows the test team to trigger wake-up events externally and capture synchronized data. The key is to make the test strategy explicit about what "good" looks like for both battery life and sensor accuracy, and to design tests that can discriminate between a firmware timing issue and a hardware settling issue.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the test results show that the sleep mode achieves the battery life target but causes sensor instability that can't be fully resolved in firmware?
- What would you do if the two teams disagree on what settling time is actually acceptable for the clinical use case?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a technical review of a proposed architecture for a new medical device where the system architect has chosen a modular design with multiple microcontrollers communicating over CAN-FD, but several senior engineers believe a single high-performance processor would be simpler and more reliable?

**Answer:** I'd structure the review to focus on objective criteria rather than personal preference. First, I'd define the key architectural requirements that matter for this device: reliability, determinism, power consumption, thermal constraints, development complexity, and long-term maintainability. I'd then ask both camps to map their proposed architecture against these criteria, with specific attention to failure modes — for example, what happens if one microcontroller in the modular design fails, versus what happens if the single processor has a fault. I'd also push for data: if the modular approach is chosen, what's the CAN-FD bus loading under worst-case traffic? What's the latency budget for critical messages? If the single-processor approach is chosen, what's the interrupt latency and how does it handle mixed criticality tasks? I'd also consider the regulatory angle — a modular design with clear separation between safety-critical and non-critical functions can sometimes simplify safety case arguments, while a single processor requires careful partitioning in software. Rather than trying to force consensus in the meeting, I'd assign owners to investigate specific open questions and bring back data. If the disagreement persists after the data is gathered, I'd make a decision based on the evidence and document the rationale, including the trade-offs that were considered. In a medical device context, the decision needs to be defensible in the design history file, so the documentation of why one approach was chosen over the other is as important as the choice itself.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the architect is strongly attached to their design and resists the data-driven review?
- What specific failure mode analysis would you want to see before making a final decision?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers feel comfortable raising concerns about a design decision early, even when the decision was made by a senior person or a consensus of the team, and even when the concern is based on a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated technical argument?

**Answer:** I'd focus on creating psychological safety through both structure and behavior. Structurally, I'd establish a formal mechanism for raising concerns — for example, a "technical concern log" that anyone can add to, with a clear process for how concerns get triaged and addressed. This removes the burden of having to challenge someone directly in a meeting. I'd also make it explicit in design reviews that the goal is to find problems, not to defend decisions, and I'd model this by being the first to raise concerns about my own designs. Behaviorally, I'd respond to gut-feeling concerns by taking them seriously — asking the person to articulate what's bothering them, even if it's vague, and then working together to turn it into a testable hypothesis. I'd never dismiss a concern for lack of evidence, because in medical device development, a gut feeling often reflects subconscious pattern recognition from past experience. I'd also make it safe to be wrong: if someone raises a concern that turns out to be unfounded, I'd thank them publicly for raising it and note that the cost of investigating was low compared to the cost of missing a real issue. Over time, this builds a norm where raising concerns early is seen as responsible engineering, not as challenging authority. I'd also watch for patterns — if the same person's gut feelings consistently point to real issues, I'd encourage them to develop those intuitions into more structured heuristics that the whole team can learn from.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where a junior engineer raises a concern that is technically incorrect, and a senior engineer responds dismissively?
- What would you do if you notice that only certain team members feel comfortable raising concerns, while others stay silent?
