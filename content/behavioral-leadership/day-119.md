# behavioral-leadership — Day 119

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when a proposed design change would improve performance but requires re-qualifying a component already approved in the design history file, and the schedule has no slack?

**Answer:** The first move is to separate the technical merit of the change from the process and schedule consequences, because those are two different decisions and conflating them tends to produce bad outcomes in both directions. On the technical side, I'd want the case for the change made in writing: what performance problem it solves, how the current approved component falls short, and what evidence supports the improvement — bench data, simulation, or field data. "It's better" is not a justification; "it improves this parameter by this much, which matters because of this requirement or risk" is. I'd also want an honest assessment of what re-qualification actually entails: which verification tests are affected, whether any regulatory submission is impacted, and how much of the existing qualification evidence can be reused versus must be regenerated.

Then I'd frame the decision explicitly as a trade-off between three things — the performance benefit, the re-qualification cost and schedule risk, and the risk of *not* changing. That last one is often skipped. If the current component is adequate and the change is a nice-to-have, the disciplined answer may well be to defer it to the next revision and capture it in the design backlog with the rationale documented. If the change addresses a real risk or a requirement that's at risk of not being met, then the re-qualification is not optional and the schedule conversation becomes about how to absorb it, not whether to.

Where there's genuinely no slack, I'd bring the trade-off to the people who own the schedule and the regulatory risk together — not decide it unilaterally in engineering. My job as lead is to make the options and their consequences legible: change now and slip, change now and compress verification (with the risk that carries), or defer with a documented rationale. I'd state a recommendation with reasoning, but the schedule-vs-performance call is a business decision that needs the people accountable for launch and compliance in the room. Whatever is decided, I'd make sure the decision and its rationale land in the design record, because "we considered this and deferred it for these reasons" is exactly the kind of thing an auditor or a future engineer needs to see.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the performance shortfall turns out to be a genuine safety concern rather than a nice-to-have — does that change who decides? What would you do if the schedule owners overrule your recommendation to defer, and the change then causes a verification failure late in the project?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team has quietly stopped escalating problems early — not because anyone decided to, but because past experience taught them that raising an issue early gets the messenger blamed or handed extra work?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a systems problem first, not a motivation problem. If a team has learned that early escalation is punished, that's a rational adaptation to a real incentive, and telling people to "speak up more" without changing the incentive won't work. The first thing I'd do is try to understand the actual pattern — was there a specific incident where someone raised a concern and got blamed, or got buried in remediation work while the original owner walked away? Naming that pattern honestly, without relitigating the incident, is usually what unlocks the conversation. People need to hear that the behavior they observed was a real signal and that I'm trying to change the system that produced it, not lecture them about attitude.

Then I'd look at the mechanics of escalation. A lot of "don't escalate" cultures come from escalation being high-friction and high-risk: there's no clear path, no neutral way to raise a concern, and no separation between "flagging a risk" and "volunteering to own the fix." I'd try to make raising a concern low-cost and blameless — a standing channel or a short risk log where anyone can log a concern with a severity, and where logging it doesn't automatically assign the work to the logger. I'd also try to visibly model the behavior from the top: when I escalate something and it turns out to be a false alarm, I want that treated as a good outcome, not a waste of time, and I'd say so publicly. The asymmetry that kills these cultures is that false alarms get punished while missed problems get forgiven; reversing that is the whole game.

Finally, I'd watch for the test case. The real proof isn't a policy document, it's what happens the first time someone junior escalates something and it turns out to be a genuine problem — or doesn't. How that gets handled, and how visibly, sets the norm far more than anything I say. I'd want to be deliberate about making the first few escalations go well and be seen to go well.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the blame culture originates above you — your own manager is the one who reacts badly to early escalation? How do you distinguish a team that's genuinely afraid to escalate from one that's simply comfortable and not looking hard enough for problems?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — something outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team with more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?

**Answer:** I'd start by being honest about *why* I'm tempted to take it. If the real reason is that I want the visibility or the learning, that's legitimate but it needs to be weighed against what's best for the outcome and for the team. If the real reason is anxiety about being accountable for work I don't personally control, that's a signal to look harder at the delegation option rather than a reason to grab the work. Accountability doesn't require doing the work yourself; it requires setting up the conditions for it to succeed and staying close enough to catch problems early.

The decision factors I'd weigh: who has the best chance of a good outcome, what the cost of a miss is, and what each option does to the team's capability over time. If someone on the team genuinely has more relevant depth, giving them the assignment with me in a supporting and reviewing role is usually the better call — it plays to their strength, it develops them, and it keeps me in the accountability loop without pretending to expertise I don't have. The high-visibility, high-stakes nature actually argues *for* putting the deepest expertise on it, not for me personally owning it. Where I'd lean toward taking it myself is if the assignment is a genuine gap nobody on the team can cover, or if it's a stretch that's specifically valuable for my own growth and the risk is tolerable — and even then I'd want a named reviewer or mentor who does have the depth.

Either way, I'd be explicit about the arrangement: who owns what, what my review cadence is, where the decision rights sit, and how I'll represent the work upward. The failure mode I'd most want to avoid is a half-delegation where someone junior is nominally responsible but I'm hovering and second-guessing every call — that gives them the accountability without the authority, which is the worst of both. If I delegate, I delegate the decisions too, with clear escalation triggers.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the person you delegated to is clearly struggling and the deadline is close — at what point do you step in, and how do you do that without undermining them? How do you make sure the visibility of the outcome accrues to the person who did the work rather than to you?

## Q4: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across hardware, firmware, and verification teams whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?

**Answer:** The divergence usually isn't anyone being sloppy — it's that each discipline has a locally reasonable definition of done that was never reconciled at the interfaces. Hardware thinks "done" means the board is built and brings up; firmware thinks "done" means the feature works on the bench; verification thinks "done" means the requirement has a passing test with evidence in the record. Each is correct within its own frame, and the gaps live exactly at the handoffs: a board that "brings up" but isn't characterized across temperature, a feature that "works" but hasn't been exercised at the corners verification will test, a test that passes but whose evidence isn't in the form the design record needs.

So I'd attack it at the interfaces rather than trying to impose one universal definition. Concretely, I'd want an explicit entry/exit criteria table per phase and per handoff — what hardware must demonstrate before firmware treats the platform as stable, what firmware must demonstrate before verification starts writing formal test procedures against it, what evidence verification must produce for a requirement to count as verified. The key is that these criteria are agreed jointly and written down, not assumed. I'd anchor them to the requirements and the risk file rather than to habit, because that's the common reference all three teams already share.

I'd also want to make the divergence visible early rather than late. A short joint readiness review at each handoff — even a lightweight one — where each team states what it believes is done and what it's assuming the other team has done, tends to surface the mismatches while they're still cheap. And I'd watch for the specific failure mode where "done" quietly means "done enough to hand off" — that's where teams start throwing work over the wall and the receiving team discovers the gap. Making the receiving team's acceptance criteria part of the definition, rather than an afterthought, is what closes that.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if verification insists on a stricter definition of done than hardware and firmware are willing to commit to, and the schedule can't absorb the extra rigor? What would you do if you discover the mismatch only after a handoff has already happened and work has been built on a shaky foundation?

## Q5: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but consistently becomes defensive when their work is questioned, and the review needs to be genuinely critical to be useful?

**Answer:** I'd separate two things that tend to get fused: the *content* of the critique and the *way* it's delivered and received. The presenter's defensiveness is real and needs addressing, but the review still has to do its job, so I wouldn't let the interpersonal issue become a reason to soften the technical scrutiny — that just trades a short-term awkwardness for a long-term risk. The approach is to change the conditions of the review so that critique is structurally normal rather than personally directed.

A few concrete levers. First, shift the framing from "is this design good?" to "what are the failure modes and how does the design address them?" — a question that invites the presenter to critique their own work alongside everyone else, rather than defend it. Second, get the review package out early and ask for written comments before the meeting, so the discussion isn't a live ambush and the presenter has had a chance to process the questions in private. Third, as the chair, model the behavior: ask questions rather than pronounce judgments, and when someone raises a concern, route it to "what would we need to see to resolve this?" rather than "this is wrong." That keeps the energy on the problem.

I'd also address the pattern with the presenter directly and privately, but framed around the review's purpose rather than their personality. Something like: the reviews need to be genuinely critical to catch problems before they're expensive, and I've noticed the sessions get tense when questions come up — I want to make sure the format works for them and that the critique lands as being about the design, not about them. Often the defensiveness is coming from a real fear — that being questioned in front of peers or seniors reflects badly on them — and naming that and separating "the design has open questions" from "you did bad work" defuses a lot of it. If the pattern persists despite that, it becomes a performance conversation, because a senior engineer who can't have their work scrutinized is a real liability on safety-critical work.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the defensiveness is driving junior engineers to stop asking questions in the review entirely? What would you do if the presenter's manager is in the room and the defensiveness is partly performative for their benefit?
