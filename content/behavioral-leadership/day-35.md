# behavioral-leadership — Day 35

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a sensor hardware failure or a firmware filtering algorithm error, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd start by framing the investigation around evidence rather than hypothesis ownership. The first step is to establish a clear containment action — if patient safety is potentially affected, that means working with the clinical team and regulatory affairs to define immediate risk mitigation while the investigation proceeds. Then I'd structure the investigation using a formal root-cause methodology like 8D or a fishbone diagram, but crucially, I'd separate the two candidate causes into parallel workstreams rather than letting the team argue about which is more likely. For the sensor hardware path, I'd want to pull failed units from the field and do bench-level characterization — checking supply voltages, reference stability, and whether the sensor output is within spec under the reported conditions. For the firmware path, I'd review the filtering algorithm's behavior at the boundaries of its input range, including what happens with out-of-range or transient values, and I'd look at the actual code path with fresh eyes rather than assuming it was reviewed correctly. The key is to gather data that discriminates between the two hypotheses — for example, if the reported symptom correlates with temperature or mechanical handling, that points toward hardware; if it correlates with specific input patterns or timing, that points toward firmware. I'd also look at the device's own diagnostic data if it logs any, since that can tell you whether the sensor was reading plausibly before the failure. Once the data points clearly in one direction, I'd verify the root cause by reproducing it under controlled conditions before committing to a corrective action. If the data is genuinely ambiguous, I'd design an experiment that forces the two hypotheses to make different predictions, then run that experiment. Throughout, I'd keep the team focused on the process rather than on defending positions, and I'd document every step so the investigation itself becomes part of the design history file.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the field data is insufficient to discriminate between the two hypotheses, and pulling more units would take weeks? What criteria would you use to decide when to escalate the investigation to a formal CAPA versus resolving it internally?

## Q2: How would you approach building a risk management culture on a medical device team that currently treats ISO 14971 documentation as a checkbox exercise done after the design is complete?

**Answer:** The core issue is that risk management has become a documentation activity rather than an engineering activity. I'd start by reframing how the team talks about risk — not as a deliverable for auditors, but as a design input that shapes decisions from the very beginning. Concretely, I'd introduce risk review as a standing agenda item at design reviews, not as a separate meeting. At each design review, we'd ask: what can go wrong with this specific circuit, this specific firmware state machine, this specific mechanical interface? What's the severity, what's the probability, and what are we doing to mitigate it? This forces risk thinking into the natural engineering workflow. I'd also change how risk documentation is created — instead of one person writing a risk management file at the end, each engineer would be responsible for documenting risks associated with their own design elements as they go. That means the risk file becomes a living document that grows with the design. To make this stick, I'd pair it with a practical tool like a simple risk register template that engineers can fill out in minutes, rather than a heavyweight document that feels like paperwork. I'd also use real examples from the team's own projects — for instance, if a previous design had a decoupling issue that caused noise problems, that becomes a risk entry in the new project's register, with a mitigation that's actually implemented and verified. Over time, the goal is that risk management becomes so integrated that engineers naturally think in terms of "what's the failure mode here?" without needing a process reminder. The documentation then becomes a byproduct of good engineering rather than an obligation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a senior engineer resists this approach, arguing that it slows down design work? How would you measure whether the risk culture is actually improving, beyond just checking that documents are being filled out?

## Q3: How would you approach leading a design review for a medical device PCB when you discover, mid-review, that a critical safety-related trace width calculation was never documented, and the original designer is no longer with the company?

**Answer:** The immediate priority is to establish whether the design is actually safe, not just whether the documentation exists. I'd pause the review at that specific item and treat it as a potential safety gap rather than a documentation gap. The first step is to determine what the trace in question carries — if it's a power trace, a ground return, or a signal that could affect patient safety if it fails, the calculation matters. I'd reconstruct the calculation from first principles using the known current requirements, the copper weight, the allowable temperature rise, and the applicable standard (for medical devices, that would include the relevant IEC 60601 requirements for creepage and clearance, as well as IPC-2221 for current-carrying capacity). I'd also check the actual layout to see what the trace width is, and whether there are any thermal reliefs, neck-downs, or vias that effectively reduce the cross-section. If the reconstructed calculation shows the trace is adequate, I'd document the calculation retroactively with a clear note about the reconstruction and have it independently verified by another engineer. If it shows the trace is marginal or inadequate, that's a design issue that needs to be addressed — either by widening the trace in a layout revision, or by documenting a risk assessment if the design is already in production. I'd also use this as a trigger to review whether other critical calculations were documented — trace widths for other safety-related paths, fuse ratings, thermal calculations, and so on. The broader lesson is that the design history file should have captured this, and I'd flag it as a process gap to address in the next design phase review. I would not simply accept the design based on the assumption that the original designer must have done the calculation correctly.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation if the reconstructed calculation shows the trace is inadequate, but the device is already in production and a recall would be extremely costly? What would you do if the trace is part of a firmware-controlled safety function, and the firmware team argues that the trace width doesn't matter because the firmware will shut down before the trace is stressed?

## Q4: How would you approach managing a situation where two senior engineers on your team have a fundamental disagreement about whether to use a hardware-based watchdog timer or a software watchdog with a separate supervisory IC for a life-support medical device, and the decision is blocking the project timeline?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating the technical decision from the interpersonal dynamic. The blocking issue is that both engineers are likely anchored on their preferred approach and are arguing from general principles rather than from the specific requirements of this device. I'd call a focused technical meeting with just the two of them and myself, and I'd frame the discussion around the system requirements rather than the solutions. What are the failure modes we're protecting against? What's the worst-case time to detect a fault and take corrective action? What's the failure rate of each approach — a hardware watchdog can fail if its clock source fails, a software watchdog can fail if the firmware is stuck in a loop that still services the watchdog. I'd ask each engineer to write down the specific failure scenarios they're concerned about, and then we'd evaluate both approaches against those scenarios. If the disagreement persists, I'd propose building a small prototype or doing a failure-mode analysis to generate data rather than opinions. For a life-support device, I'd also bring in the risk management perspective — what does the ISO 14971 analysis say about the severity and probability of watchdog failure? That's not a popularity contest; it's a structured way to evaluate the options. If we still can't reach consensus, I'd escalate to a higher-level technical authority or a peer review panel, but only after we've exhausted the data-driven path. The key is to make sure the decision is documented with the rationale, the alternatives considered, and the reasons for the choice — regardless of which approach wins. That documentation is what matters for the design history file and for regulatory review. I'd also set a firm deadline for the decision so that the project timeline is protected, and I'd make it clear that once the decision is made, the team needs to commit to it rather than continuing to debate it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where one of the engineers refuses to accept the decision after it's been made and continues to raise concerns in meetings? What if the disagreement is not just technical but also reflects a deeper conflict between the two engineers that predates this project?

## Q5: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each have different estimates for how long their work will take, and there's no historical data from similar projects?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that the estimates are uncertain and that the goal is to build a timeline that is realistic rather than optimistic. The first step is to break down each team's work into smaller tasks and ask them to estimate each task individually, rather than giving a single high-level number. This forces them to think through the details and often reveals hidden dependencies or assumptions. I'd also ask each team to identify their top three risks or unknowns — the things that could cause their estimate to be wrong — and to give me a range rather than a point estimate. For example, the hardware team might say the PCB layout will take four to six weeks, but the risk is that the sensor interface needs a redesign if the noise performance isn't met. The firmware team might say the driver development will take three to five weeks, but the risk is that the sensor's register map is poorly documented. The regulatory team might say the submission preparation will take eight weeks, but the risk is that the test lab has a backlog. Once I have these ranges, I'd build the timeline using the pessimistic end of the ranges for critical path items, and I'd identify where there's slack. I'd also look for opportunities to parallelize work — for example, the regulatory team can start drafting the submission outline while the hardware team is still doing layout, and the firmware team can start developing against a simulator or an early prototype while the hardware is being fabricated. I'd then present the timeline to the stakeholders with explicit confidence levels — "this is the date we're confident about, this is the date if everything goes wrong" — and I'd ask them to make decisions about scope or resources based on that. The key is to avoid the trap of averaging the estimates or picking the most optimistic one, because that just sets the team up for failure. I'd also build in regular checkpoints where we revisit the estimates as more information becomes available, so the timeline is a living document rather than a one-time commitment.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a senior executive pressures you to commit to a single date rather than a range, and you believe the range is the honest answer? How would you decide which tasks are on the critical path versus which ones have slack, when the dependencies between teams are not fully understood?
