# behavioral-leadership — Day 122

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when a proposed design change would improve performance but requires re-qualifying a component already approved in the design history file, and the schedule has no slack?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating the decision into two independent questions: is the change technically worth making, and is it worth making *now*. On the technical side, I'd want the performance gain quantified against the requirement it serves — if the current approved component already meets the spec with margin, "improves performance" may be an optimization rather than a need, and the bar for re-qualification should be correspondingly higher. If the gain is genuinely required to meet a safety or efficacy requirement, that changes the calculus entirely. On the schedule side, I'd map out what re-qualification actually entails — bench verification, any regulatory testing impact, documentation updates, and the review cycles — rather than treating it as a single opaque block, because the real cost is often smaller or larger than the team's gut estimate. Then I'd frame the decision explicitly as a trade-off for the accountable stakeholders: here is the performance benefit, here is the re-qualification cost and schedule risk, here is the option of deferring to the next revision. The key leadership move is not to make the call unilaterally in either direction, but to make sure the people who own the schedule and regulatory risk are deciding with the same information I have. If the decision is to defer, I'd document the rationale and the performance gap so it's a conscious, traceable choice rather than an omission — that protects the team later if the gap becomes a field issue.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the performance gain is real but the requirement it addresses is a "nice to have" rather than a regulated one? What would you do if the stakeholders split — engineering wants the change, regulatory and product want to hold the schedule?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team has quietly stopped escalating problems early — not because anyone decided to, but because past experience taught them that raising an issue early gets the messenger blamed or handed extra work?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a systems problem first, not a motivation problem. If a team has learned that early escalation is punished, that's a rational adaptation to a real incentive, and telling people to "speak up more" won't undo it. The first thing I'd do is look at the actual history: what happened the last few times someone raised a concern early? Was the person who flagged it assigned the cleanup? Was the escalation treated as an admission of failure in a status meeting? Once I understand the pattern, I'd work on changing the response, not just the request. Concretely, that means separating "raising a risk" from "owning the fix" — the person who surfaces an issue shouldn't automatically inherit it — and making sure early warnings are visibly credited rather than absorbed silently. I'd also model the behavior myself by escalating something real and low-stakes early and showing what happens: it gets triaged, not blamed. Over time I'd want a lightweight, low-ceremony channel for flagging concerns — something that doesn't feel like filing a formal report — because the friction of escalation is often what kills it. And I'd watch for the tell that it's working: issues arriving earlier and smaller, not later and bigger. If nothing changes after a few months, that's a signal the incentive is coming from above my level and needs to be raised there.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a team that's afraid to escalate and a team that's simply confident it can handle things locally? What would you do if the blame response is coming from your own manager?

## Q3: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across hardware, firmware, and verification teams whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?

**Answer:** The divergence usually isn't a disagreement about words — it's that each discipline has a different implicit exit criterion, and nobody wrote them down until a late-stage gate forced the comparison. Hardware may consider a board "done" when it's fabricated and brings up; firmware when the feature works on the bench; verification when the test procedure passes. Those are all reasonable locally and incompatible globally. My approach would be to make "done" explicit and layered early: define it at the deliverable level (what artifact, what evidence, what review sign-off) and at the milestone level (what must be true across all three disciplines for the milestone to close). I'd want each discipline to state its own exit criteria in writing and then reconcile them in a joint session, because the gaps usually surface immediately once they're side by side — for example, hardware thinks bring-up is done when the rails are stable, verification thinks it's done when the test fixture can drive the board across its operating range. I'd also build in a lightweight cross-discipline checkpoint at each milestone rather than only at the end, so drift is caught while it's cheap. The cultural piece matters too: "done" should mean "the next team can start reliably," not "my part is finished," and that framing has to come from leadership consistently, not just be written in a document.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if one discipline refuses to commit to written exit criteria because it feels the criteria will be used against them? Where would you put the cross-discipline checkpoints so they don't become bureaucratic overhead?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — something outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team with more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?

**Answer:** I'd start by being honest about *why* I'm tempted to take it. If the real reason is that I want the visibility or the learning, that's legitimate but it shouldn't be disguised as a delivery decision. If the real reason is that I don't trust the outcome without me in it, that's a different problem — a delegation and coverage problem — and taking the work myself would just postpone it. The decision criteria I'd use: who can actually deliver the best outcome, what the risk of each path is, and what the development cost of each path is. Delegating to someone with more relevant expertise but less seniority is often the right call on delivery grounds, provided I put real support around it — a clear scope, a review cadence, and an explicit statement that I'm accountable for the outcome, not them alone. Taking it myself is defensible when the assignment is genuinely a stretch I need for my own growth *and* the risk is manageable, or when the visibility means the accountability can't be shared down. What I'd avoid is the middle path where I nominally delegate but hover and redo the work — that gives the worst of both. Whatever I choose, I'd say the reasoning out loud to the team, so the decision reads as deliberate rather than as me grabbing the interesting work.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the support so delegation doesn't become abdication on a high-visibility item? If you take the stretch assignment and struggle, how do you keep the team's confidence intact?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a design review where the presenter is technically strong but has a habit of answering every question with a justification of why their original choice was correct, rather than actually engaging with the concern being raised?

**Answer:** The pattern usually means the presenter is hearing questions as challenges to their competence rather than as input to the design, and a review that keeps triggering that defensiveness stops producing useful critique — people either stop asking or start softening their questions, and the review becomes theater. My first move is to change the frame before the meeting: state up front that the purpose is to stress-test the design, that a question is not a verdict, and that the best outcome of a review is finding a problem while it's cheap. During the review, I'd intervene structurally rather than argue with the presenter's answers. If a concern gets deflected, I'd restate it as an open question and park it explicitly — "that's a question we haven't answered yet; let's capture it and come back" — so it can't be closed by a justification. I'd also separate the roles: whoever raises a concern isn't required to win the argument in the room, and the presenter isn't required to defend on the spot; both can commit to bringing data. After the review, I'd talk to the presenter privately, not about their manner but about the effect — that the review isn't surfacing the risks it should, and that this is a risk to them, not just to the process. If the behavior persists, I'd consider changing the format: written pre-review comments, or a reviewer other than me leading the critique, so the dynamic isn't tied to one relationship.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the presenter's defensiveness is justified — i.e., the questions really are low-quality or hostile? What would you do if other reviewers have started avoiding raising concerns in front of this person entirely?
