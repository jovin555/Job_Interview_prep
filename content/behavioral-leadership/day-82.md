# behavioral-leadership — Day 82

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct to cancel or wave it through are both wrong — one wastes the only slot, the other defeats the purpose of the gate. I'd split the review into two passes. In the meeting itself, I'd focus the room on what *can* be evaluated now: architecture, interfaces, component selection rationale, and the qualitative argument behind the safety-critical decision. For the missing calculations, I'd ask the presenter to walk through their reasoning verbally and have a second engineer independently sanity-check the numbers live — order-of-magnitude, worst-case corner, tolerance stack — which often surfaces whether the conclusion is plausible even without the full write-up. I'd explicitly record the gap as a formal action item with an owner and a date, and make clear that the milestone gate is *conditionally* passed pending that evidence, not unconditionally passed. The key is separating "the review happened" from "the evidence is complete" so the process isn't quietly bypassed. I'd also follow up privately with the presenter afterward — late packages usually signal a workload or prioritization problem, not indifference, and that's worth understanding before it becomes a pattern.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the verbal walkthrough suggests the calculation might actually be wrong, how do you handle that in the room without derailing the review?
- How would you prevent this situation from recurring without turning the review process into a bureaucratic hurdle?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures punish the urge to guess. The first move is to resist shipping a fix based on the most plausible hypothesis, because an unverified fix on an intermittent problem is indistinguishable from the problem going quiet on its own. I'd structure the investigation around three parallel tracks. First, containment: is there a way to bound the impact — a firmware guard, a derated operating mode, a monitoring counter — that reduces field risk without claiming to be the fix? Second, instrumentation: if you can't reproduce it, you make the system tell you when it happens. Add logging, counters, or a capture trigger around the suspect subsystem so the next occurrence yields data instead of anecdote. Third, hypothesis ranking: build a short list of candidate causes, and for each one ask what evidence would confirm or eliminate it, then design the cheapest experiment that discriminates between them. I'd be explicit with stakeholders that "we don't yet know the cause" is a legitimate status, and that the timeline depends on capturing an occurrence — which is a different kind of uncertainty than "we know the cause and are fixing it." Documenting the reasoning as you go matters here, because if the failure disappears you need to be able to say what you ruled out and why, not just that it stopped.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you decide when you've gathered enough evidence to declare the root cause found, versus continuing to investigate?
- What do you do if the failure never recurs after you add instrumentation — how do you close it out responsibly?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode on both sides is the same: the firmware team ends up designing around a mental model of the hardware that doesn't match reality. If I oversimplify to "the sensor is noisy," they'll add a filter and assume it's solved. If I hand them a full noise spectral density plot, they'll disengage. What works is translating the constraint into the *decision space* the firmware team actually operates in. Concretely: state the effective resolution they can rely on at the sample rates they'll use, the noise floor and its character (white vs. correlated vs. periodic — because that determines whether averaging helps or hurts), the settling behavior after a channel switch or a power-mode transition, and the timing budget around when a reading is valid. I'd frame it as "here's what you can assume, here's what you can't, and here's the measurement that backs each claim" — and point them at a bench setup where they can verify it themselves rather than taking my word for it. The goal is that they can make their own trade-off between sample rate, filtering, and latency without needing to re-derive the analog behavior. I'd also invite them to push back: if the constraint blocks a requirement, that's a system-level conversation, not a firmware workaround.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filtering approach would actually make the noise problem worse?
- What artifacts would you produce so this knowledge survives past the current project?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This pattern usually isn't a capability gap — it's a confidence and ownership gap, and it's often reinforced by the environment. If every time they propose something a senior person reframes it, they learn that deferring is the safe move. So the mentoring has to change both their behavior and the room's. I'd start by giving them a decision that is genuinely theirs to own — scoped small enough to be safe, but real, with a defined boundary where they don't need to seek approval. Before the decision, I'd work with them on how to frame it: what are the options, what's the trade-off, what's the recommendation and why. The point isn't to tell them the answer; it's to make sure they've done the reasoning themselves. In reviews, I'd deliberately direct questions to them first and hold the silence so they answer rather than a senior person jumping in. I'd also be explicit with the rest of the team about the intent, so they don't undercut it by reflex. Over time the goal is that they stop treating "most senior in the room" as the decision authority and start treating evidence and requirements as the authority. I'd check in regularly on how it feels from their side, because this kind of growth is uncomfortable and they need to know the discomfort is expected, not a sign they're failing.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if a senior engineer keeps overriding the junior engineer's decisions despite your intervention?
- How would you tell whether the mentoring is working, given that the outcome is a behavior change rather than a deliverable?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a technical disagreement turns personal, the technical merits stop being the actual blocker — the blocker is that neither side can concede without feeling they've lost. So the first job is to de-escalate the framing before trying to resolve the substance. I'd take the conversation out of the group setting and talk to each side individually, not to adjudicate but to understand what they're actually protecting: is it a real technical risk, a past bad experience, a concern about ownership, or something about how the decision reflects on them? Often there's a legitimate concern underneath the rhetoric that isn't being heard. Then I'd reframe the decision around criteria the team agrees on *before* evaluating the options — things like safety margin, regulatory burden, manufacturability, schedule risk, and long-term maintainability — and score both architectures against those criteria together, in the room. Making the criteria explicit and shared moves the argument from "my design vs. yours" to "both designs vs. the requirements." Where the disagreement is genuinely close, I'd propose a bounded prototype or a targeted test that produces evidence neither side can dismiss, and commit in advance to abiding by the result. If it's still deadlocked after that, I'd make the call myself, document the rationale and the dissenting view, and be clear that the decision is made so the team can move — while making sure the losing side knows their concerns were recorded, not discarded.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you keep the losing side engaged and productive after you make the call against their preference?
- What would make you decide that escalation to your manager is the right move rather than resolving it yourself?
