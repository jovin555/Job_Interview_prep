# behavioral-leadership — Day 117

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when a proposed design change would improve performance but requires re-qualifying a component that is already approved in the design history file, and the schedule has no slack?
**Answer:** I'd start by separating the decision into two questions: is the change necessary, and is now the right time. "Necessary" means it addresses a real requirement gap, a safety concern, or a known reliability risk — not an optimization that merely looks better on paper. If it's genuinely necessary, the re-qualification cost is not optional and the conversation shifts to how to absorb it. If it's an optimization, the default should be to defer it to the next revision and document the rationale, because re-opening an approved component late in a schedule with no slack introduces regulatory and verification risk that usually outweighs the performance gain.

If the change is necessary, I'd quantify the re-qualification work explicitly — what tests must be re-run, which ones can be waived with justification, what the regulatory submission impact is — rather than treating it as a vague "big cost." Then I'd bring that breakdown to the cross-functional group (quality, regulatory, product) and frame the decision as a trade-off between two concrete risks: shipping with the known limitation versus shipping later with the improvement. I'd make sure the decision and its rationale get recorded in the DHF regardless of outcome, so the choice is traceable later. The failure mode I'd guard against is letting the decision get made informally by whoever feels most strongly, which is exactly how undocumented design drift happens.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the performance improvement actually closes a gap that a competitor already addresses, and product management is pushing hard to include it? What would you do if regulatory told you the re-qualification would push the submission past a filing window?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is technically excellent but they consistently bypass the change-control process, making design changes directly and only mentioning them afterward?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a process-adherence problem, not a competence problem, and I'd be careful not to conflate the two — the person's technical output is good, and saying so first keeps the conversation from landing as a general criticism. The real issue is that unlogged changes break traceability: the DHF no longer reflects the design, verification may have been run against a different build, and if a failure occurs later, the investigation starts from a false baseline. That's a risk to the product and to the person, not just a bureaucratic annoyance.

My approach would be to first understand why they're bypassing the process. Common reasons are that the process feels slow relative to the pace of their work, that they don't see the value, or that they've never had the traceability failure explained concretely. If it's speed, I'd look at whether the change-control path can be made lighter for low-risk changes — a fast lane with a documented threshold — so compliance doesn't require heroics. If it's a values gap, I'd walk through a concrete scenario: a field failure traced to a change nobody can date or justify, and how much harder that makes the investigation and the regulatory response. Then I'd set a clear expectation that changes go through the process, and check in on it over the following weeks rather than assuming one conversation fixes it. If it persisted, that becomes a performance-management conversation, but I'd want the process itself to be defensible first.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer argued that the change-control process is genuinely too slow and cited specific examples? What would you do if you discovered that several of their undocumented changes had already shipped in a build that passed verification?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether to recommend a design freeze when one subsystem is still showing intermittent behavior that the team believes is a test-setup artifact rather than a real design issue?
**Answer:** "Test-setup artifact" is a hypothesis, not a conclusion, and the burden is on the team to demonstrate it rather than assert it. I'd want the claim made falsifiable: what specific mechanism in the setup would produce this signature, and what experiment would distinguish setup-induced behavior from design-induced behavior? A common approach is to reproduce the behavior on a second, independent setup — different fixture, different instrumentation, ideally a different operator — and see whether the signature follows the design or the bench. If it disappears on the second setup, that's meaningful evidence; if it persists, the artifact hypothesis is dead.

In parallel I'd look at whether the intermittent behavior is correlated with anything — temperature, supply ramp, specific firmware states, time since power-on — because even a weak correlation narrows the search space and tells you whether you're chasing noise or a real marginal condition. I'd also weigh the asymmetry of the two errors: freezing with a real latent defect is far more costly than delaying freeze to close out an artifact, especially in a regulated device where a field failure triggers a much larger investigation later. So my default bias would be to not freeze until the artifact hypothesis is either confirmed by evidence or the residual risk is explicitly accepted and documented with rationale. That documentation matters — if the team later turns out to be wrong, the record shows the decision was reasoned, not ignored.

**Possible follow-ups:** How long would you let the investigation run before escalating the schedule impact? What would you do if the team lead insisted the artifact explanation was obvious and pushed to freeze anyway?

## Q4: How would you approach communicating a technical risk to a non-technical executive audience when the risk is real but you can't yet quantify its probability or impact?
**Answer:** I'd resist the temptation to manufacture a false precision just to make the message land — a made-up probability number is worse than an honest range, because it invites the audience to treat the estimate as more solid than it is and then hold you to it. Instead I'd structure the message around three things: what we know, what we don't know, and what we're doing to close the gap. "We know the behavior exists under these conditions; we don't yet know how often it occurs in the field; we're running these specific experiments over the next two weeks to narrow it." That gives the executive something actionable — a decision point and a date — without pretending to certainty.

I'd also translate the risk into the terms the audience actually cares about: patient safety, regulatory exposure, launch date, and cost of a field correction versus cost of a delay. Those are the currencies executives reason in, and framing the risk in them makes the trade-off legible without requiring them to understand the technical mechanism. I'd be explicit about what decision I'm asking for, if any — sometimes the right ask is "no decision needed yet, just awareness," and saying so explicitly prevents the meeting from turning into an unproductive demand for a number that doesn't exist yet. Finally, I'd commit to a follow-up with the quantified picture once the experiments land, so the executive isn't left holding an open-ended worry.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the executive pressed you for a single probability figure anyway? What would you do if the follow-up experiments came back inconclusive and the risk remained unquantified?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a team through a situation where a design decision you strongly advocated turns out to be wrong, and the team needs to change course?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is say so plainly and early — naming the decision as wrong myself, before someone else has to. The instinct to soften it or wait for more data usually backfires, because the team can see the evidence too, and a leader who won't acknowledge a bad call loses more credibility than one who does. I'd separate the decision from the person: the decision was wrong given what we now know, and the reasoning that led to it may have been sound at the time, which is worth stating so the team doesn't conclude that reasoned decision-making itself is unsafe.

Then I'd focus the team on the recovery rather than the post-mortem of my judgment. What does the corrected path look like, what work is salvageable, what's the schedule and verification impact, and who owns each piece? I'd want the team's energy going into the new plan, not into relitigating the old one. Once the course correction is underway, I'd do a short retrospective on the decision itself — what signal we underweighted, whether there was a cheap test that would have caught it earlier — and feed that into how we make the next decision, so the failure produces a process improvement rather than just a story. The thing I'd watch for in myself is over-correcting into indecision afterward; the lesson should be better decision hygiene, not paralysis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a team member had raised the concern that turned out to be right, and you had overruled them? How would you prevent the team from losing confidence in your judgment going forward?
