# behavioral-leadership — Day 125

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when a proposed design change would improve performance but requires re-qualifying a component already approved in the design history file, and the schedule has no slack?

**Answer:** The first move is to separate the two questions that are usually tangled together: is the change worth making on its technical merits, and can the schedule absorb the re-qualification work. Those need to be answered independently before anyone decides anything.

On the technical side, I'd want the case for the change written down in the same terms the original component was justified in — what performance gap it closes, what risk it retires, what it costs in power, complexity, or new failure modes. A performance improvement that isn't tied to a requirement or a risk is a nice-to-have, and nice-to-haves don't get to jump the queue against a re-qualification cycle. If it does map to a real requirement or a real risk in the risk file, that changes the conversation entirely.

On the schedule side, I'd get a realistic estimate of the re-qualification effort from the people who would actually do it — not a wishful number. Re-qualifying an approved component typically means re-running the affected verification tests, updating the DHF, and possibly touching risk documentation. I'd want to know which tests are strictly required versus which could be covered by analysis or a delta-qualification argument, because that's often where the schedule can be recovered.

Then I'd frame the decision as a trade-off for the people who own the schedule and the regulatory posture: change now with a known re-qualification cost, defer to the next revision with the performance gap documented as a known limitation, or find an intermediate path. The key discipline is not to let "the schedule has no slack" silently become "the change is rejected" — that's a schedule decision masquerading as a technical one. I'd want the decision and its rationale recorded either way, so that if the performance gap causes problems later, there's a documented trail showing it was a conscious trade-off rather than an oversight.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the performance improvement is small but the engineer proposing it is very senior and clearly invested in it? What would make you decide the re-qualification is worth breaking the schedule for?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team has quietly stopped escalating problems early — not because anyone decided to, but because past experience taught them that raising an issue early gets the messenger blamed or handed extra work?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a systems problem first, not a motivation problem. If a team has learned that early escalation is punished, telling them "please escalate more" is asking them to act against their own evidence. The behavior won't change until the evidence changes.

The first thing I'd do is try to understand the actual pattern — was there a specific incident where someone raised a concern and got blamed, or got saddled with the cleanup? Often it's one or two memorable events that generalize into a norm. I'd want to hear those stories, because they tell me exactly what signal I need to reverse.

Then I'd work on making escalation visibly safe and visibly useful. That means when someone raises an early concern, the response has to be acknowledgment and a clear next step — not "who owns this now?" as the first question. I'd also try to separate the act of raising a concern from the act of owning the fix, at least initially, so that flagging a risk doesn't automatically mean you've volunteered to solve it alone. And when an early escalation turns out to prevent a problem, I'd make that visible — not as a trophy, but as a normal part of how the team works.

I'd also look at my own behavior and the behavior of senior people in the room. If the most senior voice responds to bad news with visible irritation, no amount of process will fix the culture. The team watches what happens to the person who speaks up, not what the process document says.

Finally, I'd want a low-friction channel for raising concerns that doesn't require someone to formally escalate in a meeting — a written note, a quick conversation, a standing risk item in a review. The lower the social cost of flagging something early, the more likely it happens before it's expensive.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the person who historically punished early escalation is your own manager? How do you tell the difference between a team that's afraid to escalate and a team that's genuinely comfortable with the current risk level?

## Q3: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across hardware, firmware, and verification teams whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?

**Answer:** The divergence usually isn't a disagreement about words — it's that each team has been optimizing against a different implicit finish line, and nobody noticed because the mismatch only bites at integration. So the fix has to start by surfacing those implicit definitions before they collide.

I'd start by asking each team, separately, to describe what "done" means for a specific deliverable — say, a subsystem or a build — in concrete, checkable terms. Not "the board works" but "the board passes these tests, these signals are characterized, this documentation exists, this review has happened." Writing those down side by side usually makes the gaps obvious immediately: hardware might consider a board done when it powers up and the rails are in spec, while firmware considers it done when it can run the full application, and verification considers it done only when the test evidence is captured and traceable. Those are three different finish lines.

From there I'd work toward a shared, layered definition — something like: functional done (it does the thing), verified done (there's evidence it does the thing under the specified conditions), and documented done (the evidence and rationale are captured where the DHF needs them). Each team owns its layer, but the project isn't "done" until all layers are satisfied for a given milestone. Making the layers explicit means nobody is surprised when verification asks for evidence that hardware thought was out of scope.

The other piece is timing. I'd want these definitions agreed at the start of a phase, not discovered at the end. A short joint session where hardware, firmware, and verification each state their exit criteria for the next milestone, and the group reconciles them, is cheap compared to the rework of finding the mismatch at integration. And I'd revisit it at each milestone, because definitions drift as the design evolves.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if verification insists on a stricter definition of done than the schedule can support? What would you do if the teams agree on the definition but one team consistently doesn't meet its layer of it?

## Q4: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but has a habit of answering every question with a justification of why their original choice was correct, rather than actually engaging with the concern being raised?

**Answer:** The pattern here is that the presenter is treating questions as attacks to be defended against rather than as inputs to be considered. That's usually not arrogance so much as a habit — often learned in environments where being questioned was a sign of weakness. The review can't do its job until that reframes.

I'd start by changing the shape of the questions, not the presenter. Instead of "why did you choose X?" — which invites a defense — I'd ask "what would have to be true for you to choose Y instead?" or "what's the failure mode you're most worried about with X?" Those questions are harder to answer with a justification of the original choice, because they ask the presenter to reason forward rather than defend backward. A strong technical person usually engages well with a genuinely interesting question; the problem is often that the questions feel like verdicts.

I'd also try to make the review's purpose explicit at the start: this is not an approval gate where you defend your work, it's a session where the team tries to find the weak points while there's still time to fix them. If the presenter believes the review is a pass/fail judgment, defensiveness is rational. If they believe it's a collaborative stress test, the calculus changes.

If the pattern persists, I'd have a direct conversation outside the review — not about the behavior in the abstract, but about the specific cost: "When you answered that question with a justification, we didn't actually get to examine the concern, and that concern is the kind of thing that shows up in verification." Framing it around the review's effectiveness rather than the presenter's attitude tends to land better with strong engineers, because it's a technical argument rather than a personal one.

And I'd model the behavior myself — when I present, I'd visibly engage with challenges, change my mind when the challenge is good, and say so. The fastest way to make defensiveness look out of place is for the senior people in the room to be visibly non-defensive.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the presenter's defensiveness is costing the team real review value and a direct conversation hasn't changed it? How would you handle a review where the presenter is defensive but turns out to be right — the concerns raised were genuinely unfounded?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — something outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team with more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?

**Answer:** The honest starting point is to separate my own appetite for the stretch from what the assignment actually needs. It's easy to rationalize "I should take this to grow" when the real driver is visibility or control, and equally easy to rationalize "I should delegate" when the real driver is avoiding something uncomfortable. So I'd try to be explicit about which is which before deciding.

The main criteria I'd weigh: who can actually deliver this well, what the cost of a wrong call is, and what each option does to the team over time. If someone on the team has genuinely more relevant expertise, the default should lean toward them — not because seniority doesn't matter, but because the person with the deepest relevant knowledge is usually the best bet on a high-visibility outcome, and giving them the assignment builds the team's bench. My accountability doesn't require me to be the one doing the work; it requires me to make sure the work gets done well and that I can stand behind it.

But there are cases where taking it myself is right: if the assignment is genuinely a stretch for the whole team and nobody has the depth, if the risk of a wrong call is high enough that my experience is the deciding factor, or if the assignment is specifically about me building a capability the team will need later. In those cases I'd want to be clear with the team about why I'm taking it, so it doesn't read as a seniority grab.

Either way, I'd set up the assignment so accountability is real: clear ownership, a defined review cadence, and me genuinely engaged in the reviews rather than hovering or disappearing. If I delegate, I'd want the person to have real decision authority, not just execution authority, and I'd want to be explicit about where I'll weigh in and where I won't. If I take it, I'd want someone with relevant expertise reviewing my work — the person I didn't delegate to is often the best reviewer.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the person you delegated to is less senior and the stakeholders keep routing questions to you instead of them? What would you do if you took the stretch assignment and it became clear partway through that you were the wrong choice?
