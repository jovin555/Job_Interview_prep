# behavioral-leadership — Day 65

## Q1: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?
**Answer:** The first move is to separate containment from correction. Containment is whatever stops the failure from reaching patients or customers right now — a firmware guard, a screening step, a usage restriction — and it can be decided quickly because it doesn't require knowing the root cause. Correction is the permanent fix, and it must not be rushed into the same decision. Once containment is in place, the pressure to "just ship something" drops, and the investigation can be run properly.

For an intermittent failure, the core problem is usually observability, not diagnosis. I would treat the first phase as instrumentation: add logging, counters, or a debug build that captures state around the failure window, and define what "the failure" actually looks like in measurable terms — a specific error code, a timing violation, an out-of-range reading. Without a crisp definition, the team will argue about whether they've reproduced it. I'd also build a structured capture plan: what conditions correlate with occurrence (temperature, supply voltage, specific sequence of operations, time since power-on), and run a matrix of those conditions rather than random retries.

Then I'd apply a structured method — 8D or fishbone plus 5 Whys — to organize hypotheses rather than jump to the first plausible one. Each hypothesis gets a test that could falsify it, and results get recorded even when negative, because ruling things out is progress. I'd keep a written investigation log so the reasoning is traceable and doesn't get re-litigated. Finally, verification of effectiveness: the fix isn't done when the symptom disappears in the lab, it's done when there's evidence the mechanism is addressed and a defined way to confirm it stays addressed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide when containment is sufficient to buy time versus when it's masking the problem? What do you do if the failure rate is so low that you can't gather enough data to confirm a fix?

## Q2: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?
**Answer:** The firmware team doesn't need the schematic, they need the model. I'd express the constraint as a small set of numbers and behaviors they can design against: the effective resolution at the point of interest (not the ADC's nominal bits), the noise floor and its character (random vs periodic vs correlated with a switching event), the update rate the analog front end can actually support, and the conditions under which behavior degrades. That's the interface contract.

The key is to frame it in terms of what firmware can do about it. If the noise is periodic and synchronous with a known source, that's actionable — averaging over a full period cancels it, whereas averaging over a partial period doesn't. If it's random, the trade-off is bandwidth versus variance, and firmware needs to know how much latency the control loop can tolerate. If there's a settling time after a multiplexer switch or a gain change, firmware needs that number to know how long to discard samples. I'd give them a short written note with the numbers, a couple of representative captured waveforms or data sets, and a clear statement of what's a hardware limitation versus what's negotiable.

I'd also set up a joint session rather than throwing a document over the wall, because the useful questions come out of dialogue — firmware often discovers a constraint matters only in a specific mode, and hardware often discovers a filter assumption that isn't valid. The goal is a shared understanding of the error budget: how much of the total measurement error is allocated to the analog front end, how much to filtering, how much to calibration.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if firmware proposes a software fix that you believe is compensating for a hardware problem that should be fixed properly? How do you decide when a hardware constraint is acceptable to design around versus when it needs to be fixed at the source?

## Q3: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is a strong engineer but has a history of becoming defensive when their work is questioned, and you need the review to be genuinely critical to be useful?
**Answer:** The defensiveness is usually about how critique is framed, not about the person being unable to accept feedback. So I'd change the frame before the review starts. I'd set the ground rule explicitly: the purpose is to find problems while they're cheap to fix, and every reviewer — including me — is expected to have their own work challenged the same way. If the presenter sees the review as a shared process rather than a verdict on them personally, the temperature drops.

Practically, I'd prepare the presenter in advance. A short pre-review conversation where I ask them where they think the weak points are does two things: it surfaces the issues they already know about, so they're not ambushed, and it signals that I'm treating them as the expert on their own design. I'd also ask them to present the trade-offs and the alternatives they rejected, not just the final answer — that makes the review about the reasoning rather than the person's competence.

During the review, I'd model the behavior I want. Questions phrased as "what happens if..." or "how did you weigh X against Y" invite explanation; "why did you do it this way" invites defense. I'd direct the first round of critique at the design, and if the presenter starts defending rather than explaining, I'd redirect: "let's park that and come back to it — is there data that would settle it?" And I'd make sure the action items are captured neutrally, so the outcome of the review is a list of things to resolve, not a scorecard.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the defensiveness persists across multiple reviews despite your efforts? How do you make sure quieter reviewers still get heard when one person tends to dominate the discussion?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?
**Answer:** The default should be to resolve it within the team, because escalation that could have been avoided erodes the team's ownership and trains people to route around each other. But there are clear cases where escalation is the right call, and I'd want to be deliberate about which one I'm in.

I'd first check whether the disagreement is actually technical or whether it's about something else — scope, ownership, risk tolerance, or a history between the two people. If it's technical, the path is usually to make it empirical: define the criteria that matter (performance, power, cost, schedule, regulatory risk), agree on how to test or estimate each option against those criteria, and let the data decide. If the data is genuinely ambiguous, then the decision is a judgment call about priorities, and that's a legitimate thing to escalate — not because the engineers failed, but because the trade-off belongs to whoever owns the priorities.

I'd escalate when: the decision has cross-team or cross-project impact beyond these two; the disagreement is blocking a milestone and the team can't make progress; the two engineers have reached an impasse after a genuine attempt to resolve it; or the decision involves risk the team isn't authorized to accept. I'd escalate with a clear framing — here's the decision, here are the options, here's the trade-off, here's our recommendation — not "these two can't agree." And I'd tell both engineers I'm escalating and why, so it doesn't feel like going behind anyone's back.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if your manager's decision goes against your own technical judgment? What would you do if the same two engineers end up in repeated disagreements that keep requiring escalation?

## Q5: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each give different estimates and there's no historical data from similar projects?
**Answer:** With no historical data, the estimates are guesses, and the useful thing isn't to average them into a single number — it's to make the uncertainty visible and structure the work so the biggest unknowns get resolved earliest. I'd start by breaking each team's work into tasks at a granularity where someone can say "this is a few days" versus "this is a few weeks," and flag which tasks depend on decisions or results from another team. The dependencies are usually where the schedule actually breaks, not the task durations.

Then I'd identify the unknowns explicitly and turn them into early investigation tasks — a prototype, a bench test, a regulatory pre-submission question — rather than letting them sit as assumptions inside a long task. The point is to convert "we think this will take six weeks" into "we'll know in two weeks whether this approach works, and then we can estimate the rest." That gives the timeline a decision point instead of a single fragile date.

For the timeline itself, I'd present ranges rather than points, and be explicit about what drives the range. I'd also build in the regulatory and verification phases as fixed constraints rather than estimates, because those are often gated by external review cycles that don't compress. And I'd set a cadence to revisit the estimate as unknowns resolve, so the timeline is a living document rather than a commitment made once and defended thereafter. The communication to stakeholders is: here's what we know, here's what we don't, here's when we'll know more, and here's the range.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you handle it when a stakeholder wants a single date rather than a range? What would you do if the unknowns resolve in a way that makes the timeline significantly longer than anyone expected?
