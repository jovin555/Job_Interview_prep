# behavioral-leadership — Day 60

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is a strong engineer but has a history of becoming defensive when their work is questioned, and you need the review to be genuinely critical to be useful?
**Answer:** The core problem is that defensiveness is usually a response to *how* critique is delivered, not to critique itself — so the leverage is in restructuring the review, not in trying to change the person's temperament in one meeting. Before the review, I'd set the frame explicitly: state that the purpose is to find issues early, that every design has weaknesses, and that the reviewer's job is to surface them while they're cheap to fix. I'd also separate the person from the artifact in the language I model — "this net has a return path problem" rather than "you routed this wrong." During the review, I'd front-load genuine strengths so the critique doesn't read as an ambush, then move through the design in a structured order (power, grounding, interfaces, thermal, mechanical, compliance) so questions feel systematic rather than personal. When a concern is raised, I'd ask the presenter to walk through their reasoning first — often they'll identify the gap themselves, which is far less threatening than being told. For anything contentious, I'd park it as an action item with an owner and a date rather than forcing resolution in the room. Afterward, I'd follow up privately to reinforce that the review did its job. If defensiveness persists across multiple reviews, that becomes a coaching conversation about impact on the team, not a technical one.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer's defensiveness is causing junior reviewers to stop speaking up entirely? What would you do if the engineer disputes a finding you're confident is correct?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent and cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?
**Answer:** Intermittent failures are where the pressure to "just fix it" does the most damage, because an unverified fix creates false confidence and the problem returns. I'd start by resisting the urge to change anything until I've characterized the failure: how often does it occur, under what conditions, on which units, at what temperature, supply voltage, and load, and does it correlate with a specific firmware build or a specific operator? That characterization often narrows the search space dramatically before any hypothesis is formed. In parallel, I'd instrument the system to capture evidence when the failure does occur — logging, a scope or logic analyzer triggered on the fault condition, or a watchdog that dumps state — because an intermittent fault that leaves no trace is nearly impossible to diagnose. I'd then build a fault tree or fishbone across the plausible categories (timing, marginal signal integrity, thermal drift, power sequencing, firmware race condition, connector or solder joint) and design targeted experiments to eliminate branches rather than guess. Containment is separate from correction: if units are in the field, I'd contain first (disable the feature, add a guard, tighten screening) while the investigation continues. Critically, I'd define up front what evidence would prove the root cause, and I wouldn't close the investigation until the fix is verified against that evidence — including a stress test that reproduces the original conditions.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when to stop investigating and ship a mitigation instead? What would you do if the failure rate is so low that you can't gather statistically meaningful data in the time available?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?
**Answer:** The firmware team doesn't need the schematic; they need to know what the signal actually looks like and what they can and can't do with it. I'd start by characterizing the constraint in the units the firmware cares about — effective number of bits, noise floor in LSBs, sample rate, settling time after a channel switch, and where the noise sits in the frequency domain. Then I'd frame it as a set of implications: "you have roughly N effective bits at this sample rate, so a single-sample threshold comparison will be unreliable; averaging over M samples gets you closer to the ideal but costs latency." That gives them a design space rather than a verdict. I'd back it with a concrete artifact — a short measurement note, a plot of noise versus sample rate, or a reference capture they can replay through their filter — so they can validate their algorithm against real data instead of a spec sheet. I'd also be explicit about what's a hard limit (the ADC is what it is) versus what's a design choice (front-end gain, filter topology, sample timing) that we could revisit together if their algorithm needs more headroom. The goal is a shared model of the signal chain, not a one-way handoff.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team proposes a software fix that you believe is masking a hardware problem? What would you do if the constraint only shows up under conditions the firmware team can't easily reproduce on their bench?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?
**Answer:** My default is to resolve it within the team, because escalation that could have been handled locally erodes the team's ownership and signals that disagreements are someone else's problem. But there are clear cases where escalation is the right call: when the decision has cross-team or cross-project impact that the two engineers can't authorize, when it's blocking a milestone and the team has genuinely exhausted its ability to decide, when it involves a regulatory or safety judgment that needs a documented owner, or when the disagreement has become personal and the technical merits are no longer the real issue. Before escalating, I'd make sure the disagreement is actually about the technical question and not about something else — scope, ownership, or a past conflict. I'd try to reframe it as a decision to be made rather than a winner to be picked: what are the criteria, what evidence would settle it, can we prototype or test to get data, and if not, who owns the call and by when? If we can't converge, I'd escalate with a clear summary — the options, the trade-offs, the team's recommendation if there is one, and the specific decision needed — rather than dumping the conflict upward. That way escalation is a decision-making tool, not a failure.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if your manager's decision goes against the team's recommendation? What would you do if one of the engineers escalates over your head before you've had a chance to work it through?

## Q5: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each give different estimates and there's no historical data from similar projects?
**Answer:** With no historical data, the estimates are really expressions of uncertainty, and the useful move is to make that uncertainty explicit rather than average it away. I'd start by decomposing the work into the smallest units each team can actually reason about — not "hardware: 12 weeks" but "schematic capture, layout, first-article build, bring-up, EMC pre-scan, formal EMC" — because different teams are often estimating different scopes without realizing it. Then I'd ask each team for a range rather than a point estimate, and specifically for the assumptions behind the optimistic and pessimistic ends. That surfaces the real disagreements: the firmware team may be assuming hardware is stable at a certain date, the regulatory team may be assuming test articles are available earlier than the hardware team plans to deliver them. I'd map the dependencies explicitly, because in medical device development the critical path usually runs through build-and-test cycles, not through any single team's work. I'd build the timeline around those dependencies and identify the few unknowns that most affect the schedule — a new sensor, an unfamiliar standard, a first-time regulatory pathway — and treat those as risks with owners and early checkpoints rather than as line items. Finally, I'd present the timeline as a range with the key assumptions and risks called out, and commit to updating it as the unknowns resolve, rather than presenting a single date that implies false precision.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if leadership pressures you to commit to the optimistic end of the range? What would you do if one team's estimate turns out to be badly wrong partway through the project?
