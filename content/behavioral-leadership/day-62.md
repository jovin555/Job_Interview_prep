# behavioral-leadership — Day 62

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection every session, and the team is starting to route around the review process to make progress?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two problems: the review is not converging, and the team is losing faith in it. Those need different responses. On the convergence side, I'd look at whether the objections are genuinely new technical concerns or whether they're symptoms of an unclear decision criterion — often a review stalls because nobody has stated what "good enough to proceed" means for this gate. I'd restate the entry and exit criteria for the review, capture every open objection in a written action log with an owner and a resolution path, and distinguish blocking issues from items that can be tracked and closed later. That converts an open-ended debate into a finite list. If one reviewer is consistently the source of late objections, I'd talk to them privately, not to shut them down but to understand whether they feel the review package is inadequate or whether they're surfacing real risk that the process isn't capturing. Sometimes the "difficult" reviewer is the only one reading the safety-critical sections carefully. On the routing-around side, I'd treat that as a process integrity problem, not just a morale problem — in a regulated environment, undocumented design decisions are a liability regardless of how good the engineering is. I'd make the case that the review exists to protect the team, not to slow it down, and that skipping it moves risk from the schedule onto the design history file. The goal is a review that is rigorous and finite, so people don't feel they have to choose between quality and progress.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the persistent objector turns out to be right about a real risk that the rest of the team has been dismissing? What would you do if the review process itself is genuinely too heavyweight for the pace the project needs?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?
**Answer:** I'd structure the presentation in layers rather than trying to find one level of detail that works for everyone, because there isn't one. The top layer is the decision itself and its consequences in terms the whole room cares about: what changes, what it costs in schedule or risk, and what the alternatives are. That layer should be understandable without any domain knowledge. The second layer is the engineering rationale — the specific trade-off, the constraints that drive it, and the evidence behind it — aimed at the technical reviewers. The third layer is the supporting data, calculations, and test results, which I'd have ready but not present unless someone asks. The discipline is to make the first layer genuinely complete on its own, so a non-technical stakeholder can make an informed decision without feeling talked down to, and to make the deeper layers available on demand so the engineers don't feel the substance has been hidden. I'd also be explicit about which parts are settled and which are still open, because mixed audiences often assume that anything presented is already decided. And I'd avoid the trap of using jargon as a shortcut — if I can't explain the trade-off in plain terms, it usually means I haven't fully understood it myself. The quality manager in particular needs to see the risk framing, not the circuit-level detail, so I'd make sure the safety and compliance implications are stated in their language.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the product manager makes a decision based on your simplified summary that the engineers feel misrepresents the technical reality? How do you decide in advance which trade-offs are worth bringing to a mixed audience versus resolving within the engineering team?

## Q3: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never had to own a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a gradual transfer of ownership rather than a single conversation, because deference is usually a habit built from never having been given real stakes. The first step is to give them a decision that is genuinely theirs — small enough to be safe, but real enough that the outcome matters and no one else is going to quietly override it. I'd be explicit that I'm not going to make the call for them, and then actually hold to that, which is harder than it sounds. The second step is to make the reasoning visible: after they decide, I'd ask them to walk me through the alternatives they considered and why they rejected them, not to second-guess the answer but to build the habit of articulating rationale. That's the skill that's actually missing — not technical judgment, but the confidence to defend a judgment. The third step is to put them in situations where they have to present a decision to people more senior than themselves, starting in a low-stakes review and building up. I'd also model the behavior I want: when I make a decision, I'd say out loud what I was uncertain about and how I resolved it, so they see that senior people also operate with incomplete information rather than some authority they lack. The failure mode to avoid is protecting them from consequences, because ownership that never has stakes attached doesn't build anything.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if they make a decision that turns out to be wrong — how do you handle that without undoing the confidence you're trying to build? How would you tell the difference between healthy deference and someone who genuinely isn't ready for ownership yet?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?
**Answer:** When a disagreement turns personal, the technical merits are usually still there but they've become entangled with identity — people are defending a position because they've publicly committed to it, not because the evidence still supports it. The first move is to depersonalize the decision by changing what's being argued about. Instead of "which architecture is better," I'd reframe it as "what does each architecture have to be true for it to be the right choice," and then test those assumptions against evidence rather than opinion. That shifts the conversation from who is right to what the data says, and it gives people a face-saving way to update their view, because they're no longer abandoning a position, they're responding to new information. Where the assumptions can be tested cheaply — a prototype, a bench measurement, a targeted simulation — I'd push for that, because a shared piece of evidence does more to resolve a dispute than any amount of discussion. I'd also make the decision criteria explicit before evaluating the options, so the choice isn't made on the basis of who argued most forcefully. If the disagreement persists after the evidence is in, that's a signal that the decision needs to be made by a designated owner with documented rationale, not by consensus — and I'd be clear that a documented decision with a stated rationale is not the same as one side winning. Finally, I'd watch for the interpersonal damage separately from the technical question, because even a well-resolved decision can leave a team fractured if the process felt unfair to the losing side.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the two sides can't agree on what evidence would even be relevant? What would you do if the person whose position loses is a senior engineer whose buy-in you need for the implementation to succeed?

## Q5: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when the failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?
**Answer:** Intermittent failures are the hardest case because the pressure to ship a fix is highest exactly when the evidence is weakest, and a fix applied without a confirmed root cause is a guess that may mask the problem rather than solve it. I'd start by resisting the framing that "ship a fix" and "find the root cause" are in tension — they're only in tension if you accept an unverified fix as a solution. The first priority is containment: if the failure has a safety or field impact, I'd put a temporary mitigation in place that's clearly labeled as containment, not corrective action, so the pressure to act is relieved without pretending the problem is solved. In parallel, I'd focus on making the failure observable rather than trying to reproduce it directly. Intermittent failures usually leave traces — logs, error counters, timing data, environmental conditions at the moment of failure — and instrumenting the system to capture those is often more productive than trying to force a reproduction. I'd also look hard at the boundary conditions: temperature, supply voltage, timing margins, startup sequences, and interactions between subsystems, because intermittent failures cluster around edges rather than nominal operation. I'd apply a structured method — 8D or a fishbone with 5-whys on each branch — not as ceremony but because it forces the team to consider causes they'd otherwise skip past. Critically, I'd define in advance what evidence would confirm a candidate root cause, so the investigation has a stopping condition rather than ending when someone gets tired. And I'd verify effectiveness after the fix by testing against the original failure conditions, because a fix that makes the symptom disappear without a confirmed mechanism is not a fix.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when to stop investigating and ship a containment measure as a permanent fix, if the root cause can't be confirmed within the available time? How would you handle it if the intermittent failure only appears in the field and never on the bench, so you can't instrument it directly?
