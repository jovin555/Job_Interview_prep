# behavioral-leadership — Day 102

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically sound but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** I'd separate the two problems — the missing justification and the milestone gate — because conflating them is what usually turns this into an all-or-nothing fight. First, I'd run the review on what's actually reviewable: architecture, interfaces, component selection, and the qualitative reasoning behind the safety-critical choice. That lets the team make real progress and gives the presenter a fair hearing on the parts that are complete. Then I'd be explicit that the review cannot be *closed* on the safety-critical item until the calculations are in the record, because an unreviewed safety justification is exactly the kind of gap that becomes a finding later. The milestone gate is a scheduling fact, not a technical one, so I'd take it to the people who own the schedule with a clear statement: the design is likely fine, but the evidence isn't reviewable yet, and here is the minimum calculation set needed to close it. I'd offer a concrete path — a focused follow-up review with two named reviewers within a defined window, with the calculation package circulated in advance. The key discipline is not letting "the meeting is the only slot" pressure the team into signing off on an unverified safety decision; a conditional pass with a documented open action is almost always available and is far better than a rubber stamp.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the presenter pushes back and says the calculations exist but just weren't written up — do you accept verbal justification in the review? How would you make sure the conditional pass doesn't quietly become a permanent pass once the milestone pressure is gone?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** The first move is to resist the pull toward a fix before there's a mechanism, because an intermittent failure with an unknown mechanism will usually come back, and a premature fix burns the team's credibility and the schedule. I'd structure it in two parallel tracks. The containment track addresses the immediate pressure: what can we do to limit exposure — tighter screening, added margin, a monitoring or logging build, a conservative operating restriction — without claiming it's the cure. The investigation track is about converting an intermittent into a reproducible: instrument the system to capture state at the moment of failure, characterize the boundary conditions (temperature, supply, timing, load, specific sequences), and look for the pattern in field or test logs rather than waiting for a bench reproduction. I'd use a structured method — 8D or fishbone plus 5 whys — to keep the hypothesis space explicit and to force the team to state what evidence would confirm or eliminate each candidate. I'd also deliberately widen the net early across hardware, firmware, and test setup, because intermittent failures are where teams most often anchor on their own domain's usual suspects. Verification of effectiveness matters here: the fix isn't done when the symptom stops, it's done when you can explain why it stopped and demonstrate the mechanism is gone.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide when containment is good enough to ship while the investigation is still open? What's your approach if the failure rate is so low that statistically demonstrating the fix worked would take months?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** I'd aim for a shared model rather than a data dump. The firmware team doesn't need the full noise analysis, but they do need to know what the signal actually looks like at the pin, what the error budget is, and which of their choices move the needle. So I'd frame it as: here is the usable signal range and resolution after the analog front end, here is the noise floor and its character (random vs. correlated with switching events), here is what's fixed in hardware and what's still open, and here are the specific firmware levers — sampling timing relative to switching, averaging and filter design, calibration strategy, how you handle the rails and saturation. I'd back it with a short characterization artifact: a few scope or FFT captures, a table of measured noise under representative conditions, and a clear statement of the assumptions. The goal is that a firmware engineer can reason about trade-offs themselves rather than being handed a rule. I'd also set up a joint review of the filter and sampling design, because that's where the two domains actually meet and where misunderstandings are cheapest to catch. The failure mode to avoid in both directions is the hardware team handing over a spec with no context, or drowning the discussion in analog detail that doesn't change any firmware decision.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filter would mask a real sensor fault along with the noise? What artifact would you produce so the constraint stays documented after the conversation is over?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** I'd treat escalation as a tool with a specific purpose, not a verdict on who's right. The first question is whether the disagreement is actually technical or has become about something else — ownership, history, or face. If it's genuinely technical, my default is to keep it in the team and resolve it with evidence: define the decision criteria up front (safety, reliability, cost, schedule, manufacturability), agree what data or prototype would discriminate between the options, and commit to being persuaded by the result. Many senior disagreements dissolve once the criteria are explicit, because the two engineers were optimizing different things. I'd escalate when the decision exceeds the team's authority — it commits budget, affects a regulatory submission, or crosses into another team's ownership — or when the disagreement is blocking work and the team has genuinely exhausted its ability to resolve it, or when it's become personal enough that a neutral party is needed to reset it. What I'd avoid is escalating to offload the discomfort of making a call, and equally avoiding sitting on a blocked decision out of a wish to keep it internal. Either way, I'd document the decision and its rationale so the record shows *why*, not just *what*.

**Possible follow-ups:** If you do escalate, how do you frame it so it doesn't read as "these two can't work together"? What would you do if your manager overrides the team's evidence-based conclusion?

## Q5: How would you approach building a risk management culture on a medical device team that currently treats ISO 14971 documentation as a checkbox exercise done after the design is complete?

**Answer:** The root of the checkbox behavior is usually that risk work is bolted on at the end, so it can only ever be retrospective paperwork. The fix is to move it into the design flow so it does real work. Concretely: make risk analysis a live input to design decisions rather than a deliverable — when the team weighs an architecture or a component choice, the hazard analysis and the failure-mode thinking should be part of that conversation, not a form filled in afterward. I'd start small and visible: pick one active design decision, run a proper risk discussion on it in a design review, and show the team that it changed or confirmed the decision. That's more persuasive than a policy memo. I'd also make the artifacts usable — a risk register that people actually read, traceability from hazard to control to verification that's maintained as the design evolves, and DFMEA treated as an engineering tool rather than a compliance tax. Leadership behavior matters: if I ask "what's the hazard here and how is it controlled?" in every design review, and treat a well-reasoned risk discussion as good engineering rather than overhead, the culture follows. The measure of success is that the team raises risks early because it helps them design better, not because an auditor will ask.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a senior engineer who is technically excellent but openly dismissive of risk documentation as busywork? How do you keep the risk file current without it becoming a full-time maintenance burden?

**Possible follow-ups (Q5 continued):** What would you do if the team adopts the language of risk management but the analysis stays shallow — hazards listed but never really thought through?
