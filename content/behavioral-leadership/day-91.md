# behavioral-leadership — Day 91

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but the review package arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct is to either cancel the review or wave it through on trust in the presenter, and both are wrong for a safety-critical decision. I would start by separating the two problems: the process failure (late, incomplete package) and the substantive question (is the safety-critical decision adequately justified?). The review still happens, but its scope is explicitly narrowed. I would open by stating what the package does and does not support, so the room isn't lulled into thinking a full review occurred. Then I'd focus the session on the safety-critical item: walk through the decision logic verbally, capture the missing calculations as a formal action item with an owner and a hard date, and record in the minutes that the decision is *conditionally* accepted pending those calculations being reviewed by a named second reviewer. That keeps the milestone gate honest — the gate is passed on a documented condition, not on an assumption. Separately, I'd have a direct, non-public conversation with the presenter about the package arriving late: not as a reprimand, but to understand whether it's a workload problem, a misunderstanding of what the package needs to contain, or something else. If the review-package standard isn't clear, that's a process gap I own, not an individual failing. The key principle is that a review's value comes from what it actually examined — a review that quietly skips the safety-critical justification is worse than no review, because it creates a false record that scrutiny happened.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the presenter pushes back and says the calculations exist and are obviously correct, how do you respond without either capitulating or turning the review into an argument?
- How would you make sure the conditional acceptance doesn't quietly become permanent acceptance if the follow-up calculations never get reviewed?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures under schedule pressure are where teams most often substitute a plausible fix for a verified root cause, and the discipline has to come from the investigation structure itself. I would start by resisting the framing that "ship a fix" and "find the root cause" are in tension — they only conflict if you skip steps, and a fix without a root cause is a guess that may mask the problem rather than solve it. First, containment: if there's any safety or field-risk dimension, contain it (limit operating conditions, add a monitoring flag, increase screening) so the pressure to rush the *corrective* action drops. Second, treat non-reproduction as a data problem, not a dead end. If it can't be reproduced on demand, the goal becomes capturing it when it happens: instrument the system to log the state leading up to the failure, widen the environmental or timing envelope in stress testing, and gather field returns or logs. Third, build a structured hypothesis set — a fishbone across hardware, firmware, environment, and use-case factors — and design discriminating tests that separate hypotheses rather than tests that merely confirm a favorite one. Fourth, be explicit with stakeholders about the difference between "we have a fix" and "we have verified the fix addresses the root cause," and give a realistic timeline for the latter. Verification of effectiveness is the step that gets skipped under pressure, so I'd name it up front as a required gate. The honest message to leadership is that an unverified fix trades schedule risk now for field-failure risk later, which for a medical device is the worse trade.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you decide when you've gathered enough evidence to declare a root cause versus continuing to investigate?
- What would you do if leadership overrides the investigation and directs the team to ship the plausible fix anyway?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is the same: the firmware team ends up making design choices based on a wrong mental model of the hardware. Oversimplify ("the ADC is 12-bit") and they assume ideal behavior; bury them in noise spectral density plots and they disengage and guess. What works is translating the constraint into the *decisions it affects*. I start by asking what the firmware team actually needs to decide — filtering strategy, sampling rate, calibration approach, what counts as an out-of-range reading — and then express the hardware reality in those terms. For an ADC, that means not just resolution but effective resolution after noise, the input range and where the signal of interest sits within it, and the dominant noise sources and their character (random vs. periodic vs. correlated with a switching event). A short table or a couple of annotated plots usually beats prose. I'd also make the constraint testable from their side: give them a way to observe the raw signal and a reference for what "good" and "bad" look like, so they can validate their filtering against reality rather than against my description of it. Where the boundary is genuinely coupled — say, whether a filter belongs in hardware or firmware — that's a joint decision, and I'd rather have that conversation early than have each team assume the other owns it. The test of a good translation is whether the firmware engineer can explain the constraint back to me correctly and point to the design choice it drove.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filtering approach would work in theory but interacts badly with a hardware characteristic they didn't account for?
- Where do you draw the line between a hardware constraint the firmware must live with and a hardware problem that should be fixed on the board instead?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This pattern usually isn't a capability gap — the engineer can do the work — it's a confidence-and-experience gap around *ownership*: they've learned that deferring is safe, and they've never had a low-stakes opportunity to be wrong and recover. So the mentoring approach is to create those opportunities deliberately and safely. I'd start by giving them a scoped decision to own outright — something with real consequences but bounded blast radius, and with a clear point where the decision gets reviewed — and then genuinely not make the decision for them. That last part is the hard discipline: when they look to me for the answer, I reflect the question back, ask what they'd choose and why, and let them commit to it. I'd also work on the "in the room" behavior specifically, since that's where the deferring shows up: agree beforehand that in a given review they will present and defend a particular decision, and that I will stay quiet unless they signal for help. Afterward, debrief on what went well and what they'd do differently. Over time I'd raise the stakes and reduce the scaffolding. The measure of success is not that they make the same decision I would — it's that they can articulate a defensible rationale and stand behind it, and that they've internalized that a well-reasoned decision that turns out imperfectly is not a failure. I'd also be explicit that I'm doing this and why, so it reads as investment rather than abandonment.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if, given a decision to own, the engineer makes a choice you believe is clearly wrong — do you let it proceed?
- How would you tell the difference between healthy deference to experience and a confidence problem that needs a different kind of support?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a technical disagreement turns personal, continuing to argue the technical merits in the same forum usually makes it worse, because each side now hears every point as an attack. The first move is to change the frame. I'd pause the debate and reset the terms: both architectures are viable — that's why this is hard — and the question is not who is right but which set of trade-offs best fits *this* product's priorities. I'd make those priorities explicit and agreed before re-engaging: cost, schedule, risk, manufacturability, regulatory burden, long-term maintainability. Often the personal heat comes from each side arguing for different unstated priorities, and naming them defuses it. Then I'd try to convert opinion into evidence — a time-boxed prototype, a benchmark, a failure-mode analysis, a rough cost or schedule model — so the decision rests on data rather than advocacy. If the disagreement survives that, I'd make the decision myself with a documented rationale, including why the losing option was reasonable, and commit the team to it. I'd also address the personal dimension directly but privately with each party: name that it's become personal, that I value both engineers, and that I expect them to argue the technical case without impugning the other's judgment. The goal is to preserve the relationship and the rigor while removing the incentive to "win."

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you make a decision between two viable options without the losing side disengaging from the project?
- What would you do if the personal friction persists after the decision is made and documented?
- How would you prevent this kind of disagreement from becoming personal in the first place on future decisions?
