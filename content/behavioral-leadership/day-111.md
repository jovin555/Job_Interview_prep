# behavioral-leadership — Day 111

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two problems, because they have different fixes. The stalling review is a process problem; the routing-around is a trust problem, and the second is more dangerous because it means the team has stopped believing the review adds value.

On the stalling: I'd look at whether the objections are genuinely new or whether they're the same underlying concern resurfacing in different clothes. Often a reviewer who raises a fresh objection every session is not being obstructive — they're trying to articulate a systemic worry they haven't been able to name, and each session they attack it from a new angle. So I'd have a one-to-one conversation outside the review and ask directly: "What's the concern underneath these objections? If you could only raise one thing about this design, what would it be?" That conversation usually converts a diffuse anxiety into a specific, addressable issue, and once it's named it can be dispositioned like any other review item — accepted, mitigated, or rejected with rationale.

I'd also tighten the mechanics: a review agenda circulated in advance with a defined scope, a parking lot for out-of-scope concerns, and a rule that new objections after a certain point go into a follow-up review rather than blocking the current gate. That's not about silencing the reviewer — it's about making the review a bounded decision-making forum rather than an open-ended seminar.

On the routing-around: I'd name it openly and without accusation. The team isn't doing it out of malice; they're doing it because the review has become a bottleneck and they still have a schedule. But I'd be clear that bypassing review on a medical device is not a neutral act — it creates undocumented decisions, and that's exactly the kind of gap that surfaces in an audit or a field failure. I'd want to understand what specifically they're routing around and why, because if the review process is genuinely too slow, that's a legitimate complaint and I need to fix the process rather than just enforce it. The goal is to make the review fast enough and useful enough that nobody wants to skip it.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if the one-to-one conversation revealed the reviewer's concern was legitimate but had been raised so poorly that nobody had understood it?
- How would you handle it if the team's routing-around had already produced a design decision that now needs to be retroactively documented?

## Q2: How would you approach leading a technical decision when two senior engineers disagree on a hardware-versus-software implementation of the same safety function — say, a watchdog — and the disagreement is blocking the project timeline?

**Answer:** I'd start by reframing what's actually being decided, because "hardware versus software watchdog" is usually shorthand for a deeper disagreement about where the safety function should live and how it should be verified. Those are different questions with different answers, and separating them often dissolves the impasse.

Concretely, I'd get both engineers to state their positions as explicit criteria rather than preferences: what failure modes does each approach cover, what does each fail to cover, what's the verification burden for each, what's the independence argument for each, and what does each cost in board space, BOM, firmware complexity, and schedule. A hardware watchdog with an external supervisory IC gives you independence from the main processor's clock and firmware — a strong argument for a life-support-class function — but it adds a component, a qualification, and a supply-chain consideration. A software watchdog is cheaper and more flexible but shares failure modes with the very firmware it's supposed to be supervising. Written down side by side, the trade-off usually becomes obvious for the specific safety classification involved, and the disagreement collapses into a decision.

If it doesn't collapse — if both are genuinely viable — then I'd look for a way to resolve it with evidence rather than argument. That might mean a short prototype or bench evaluation of the failure-detection behavior, or it might mean deferring to the risk analysis: which approach better addresses the hazards identified in the FMEA, and which is easier to verify convincingly. The decision should trace back to the risk file, not to who argues more forcefully.

On the timeline: I'd set a decision date and be explicit that if the two engineers can't converge by then, I'll make the call myself with their input, document the rationale, and we move. Blocking a project on an unresolved technical debate is itself a decision — a bad one — and the team needs to see that a documented decision with a stated rationale is better than an indefinite standoff. I'd also make sure both engineers feel their position was genuinely heard and recorded, because the one whose approach isn't chosen still has to execute it well.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the engineer whose approach wasn't chosen remained visibly uncommitted to the decision?
- What would you document, and where, so that the rationale survives an audit and a change of personnel?

## Q3: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument?

**Answer:** The core problem is that "gut feeling" concerns are usually real signal wrapped in insufficient evidence, and most engineering cultures implicitly teach people that only fully-formed arguments are welcome. So the first move is to make it safe and normal to raise a half-formed concern, and to make the burden of articulating it a shared one rather than the raiser's alone.

Practically, I'd build in explicit prompts. In design reviews, I'd routinely ask "does anyone have a discomfort they can't fully justify yet?" and treat the answer as legitimate input rather than noise. When someone raises a vague concern, my job as the lead is to help them sharpen it — "what would have to be true for that to be a problem?" or "what's the failure you're imagining?" — rather than to dismiss it for lack of rigor. That modeling matters: if the lead visibly takes a half-formed concern seriously and helps develop it, the team learns that's acceptable behavior.

I'd also separate the act of raising a concern from the act of deciding. Raising a concern should never be framed as blocking or opposing; it's contributing information. A concern can be logged, investigated, and dispositioned as "considered, not actionable" without anyone losing face. That distinction lets senior people hear concerns about their decisions without feeling challenged, and lets junior people raise them without feeling insubordinate.

On the "decision came from a senior person" part specifically: I'd be explicit that decisions are owned by the team, not by the person who happened to propose them, and that the senior person's job is to make the decision robust by inviting challenge, not to defend it. If I'm the senior person in the room, I'd deliberately voice the strongest counter-argument to my own proposal before asking for others — it lowers the social cost of disagreement and often surfaces the real issue faster.

Finally, I'd close the loop visibly. When a gut-feeling concern turns out to be right, say so publicly. When it turns out not to be actionable, explain why. The culture is built by what gets reinforced, and a single well-handled "you were right to flag that" does more than any amount of exhortation.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a case where a gut-feeling concern was raised, dismissed, and later turned out to be correct?
- How do you keep this from becoming a culture where every decision is endlessly relitigated?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than systemic issues?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is set the frame before the meeting, explicitly and in writing: the purpose is to understand the system that produced the outcome, not to identify who to blame. I'd say plainly that anyone who uses the session to point at a person will be redirected, and that I'll be as rigorous about my own decisions as anyone else's — because if the lead isn't visibly in scope, nobody believes the framing.

Then I'd structure the analysis to make systemic causes the natural output. A timeline reconstruction is the backbone: what actually happened, in order, with decision points marked. For each major slip, I'd ask not "who dropped this" but "what made this the reasonable thing to do at the time" — because most schedule failures are people making locally sensible choices inside a system that didn't give them better options. Missing estimates, unclear ownership, late-arriving requirements, optimistic sequencing, unstated dependencies, a review process that didn't catch a problem early enough — those are the findings I'm looking for, and they're usually visible once you stop looking for culprits.

I'd use a structured method rather than open discussion, because open discussion in a low-morale room defaults to the loudest or most defensive voices. Something like a fishbone across categories — requirements, estimation, staffing, process, technical unknowns, external dependencies — gives everyone a place to contribute without it becoming personal. And I'd separate containment from corrective action: what do we do right now to protect the next milestone, versus what do we change structurally so this class of slip doesn't recur.

On morale: I'd acknowledge it directly rather than pretending the session is neutral. Missing a deadline by months is demoralizing, and people need to hear that the goal is to come out with a better system, not a verdict. I'd also make sure the output includes things the team did well and things that were outside anyone's control, because a post-mortem that only catalogs failure teaches people to hide.

Finally, I'd assign owners and dates to the corrective actions and review them at a follow-up — a post-mortem whose actions quietly evaporate confirms everyone's cynicism about the exercise.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the analysis genuinely did surface an individual performance issue alongside the systemic ones?
- What would you do if senior leadership wanted a simpler "who's responsible" answer than the post-mortem produced?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?

**Answer:** I'd design the presentation around the decision the audience needs to make, not around the analysis I did to reach it. Those are different documents, and conflating them is the usual failure mode — either you drown the non-engineers in detail, or you strip so much out that the engineers don't trust the conclusion.

My structure would be layered. The top layer is the decision itself, the options considered, the criteria that matter, and a recommendation — stated in plain language, with the "so what" for each stakeholder made explicit. For the quality manager that's the risk and compliance implications; for the product manager it's cost, schedule, and user impact; for the engineers it's the technical basis. Everyone gets that layer, and it should stand alone.

Below that, I'd put the supporting detail in appendices organized by stakeholder concern rather than by engineering discipline — a risk/compliance appendix, a cost/schedule appendix, a technical analysis appendix. That way each person can go as deep as they need without forcing the whole room through it. In the meeting itself, I'd present the top layer and then explicitly invite each stakeholder to the appendix relevant to them, rather than walking through everything linearly.

The judgment call is what belongs in the top layer. My rule is: anything that would change someone's vote belongs up front, even if it's technical. If a trade-off hinges on a thermal margin or an ADC noise floor, that has to be visible to everyone, explained in terms of its consequence rather than its mechanism — "this option runs closer to the thermal limit, which affects long-term reliability" rather than a recitation of the thermal calculation. What can go to the appendix is the derivation, the alternatives that were ruled out early, and the sensitivity analysis.

I'd also be careful not to use "simplify for the non-technical audience" as an excuse to hide uncertainty. If a trade-off rests on an assumption that isn't fully validated, that needs to be stated in the top layer in plain terms, because the product manager is the one who'll be held to the schedule if the assumption is wrong. Translating technical uncertainty into schedule and risk language is the actual skill here — not removing the uncertainty.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the product manager pushed for a decision before the engineers felt the technical analysis was complete?
- What would you do if a stakeholder consistently ignored the appendices and then challenged the recommendation on technical grounds in the meeting?
