# behavioral-leadership — Day 9

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design review for a medical device PCB when you discover that the layout engineer has used a single ground plane for both the analog sensor section and the high-current motor drive section, and the original designer is not present to explain their reasoning?

**Answer:** I would first pause the review and acknowledge the finding without assigning blame, then structure the discussion around understanding the design intent and evaluating the risk. I'd start by examining the schematic to see if there's any isolation strategy documented — perhaps the designer intended a split-plane approach that wasn't implemented, or they may have relied on component placement and trace routing to manage return currents. I'd ask the team to look at the physical layout: where are the high-current return paths relative to the analog sensor reference? Is there a star-point grounding scheme attempted? Are there any ferrite beads or isolation gaps that suggest intent? If the documentation is silent, I'd treat this as a design risk that needs formal assessment. I'd document the finding in the review action items, assign someone to simulate or calculate the expected noise coupling between the motor drive and analog section, and schedule a follow-up review with the original designer if possible. In parallel, I'd consider whether the board can be re-spun with a proper grounding strategy, or if there are mitigation options like adding shielding cans, ferrite beads on sensor power, or software filtering that could be validated before committing to a layout change. The key is to avoid assuming the worst — sometimes a single solid ground plane with careful component placement works better than a poorly implemented split plane — but to force a data-driven decision rather than letting it pass unexamined.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific measurements or simulations would you request to quantify the noise coupling risk? How would you decide whether to halt the project for a layout revision versus accepting the current design with mitigation?

## Q2: How would you approach handling a situation where a team member who is responsible for a critical design verification test repeatedly delays completing the test report, and you suspect they are avoiding it because they know the results will show a failure?

**Answer:** I would start with a private, non-confrontational conversation to understand what's blocking them. The framing matters — I'd ask about their workload, whether they need additional resources or training, and whether they've encountered unexpected results that they're unsure how to interpret. If they acknowledge the test is showing marginal or failing results, I'd thank them for raising it and immediately shift to problem-solving mode: what does the data actually show, what are the possible root causes, and what containment actions can we take while we investigate? The goal is to remove the fear of delivering bad news by demonstrating that finding a failure early is a success, not a failure — it's exactly why we do verification testing. If they continue to avoid the conversation, I'd escalate carefully: I'd involve their direct manager or project lead, framing it as a resource or support issue rather than a performance problem, and ensure the team member is present so they can speak to their concerns. I'd also reinforce that delaying the report doesn't make the problem go away — it only compresses the time available to fix it, which increases risk to the project and patient safety. Once the test results are documented, I'd lead a structured root-cause investigation (using 5 whys or fishbone) to address the underlying design issue, and I'd make sure the team member is recognized for ultimately bringing the issue to light.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a genuine resource constraint and avoidance behavior? What would you do if the team member continues to delay even after you've offered support?

## Q3: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each have different estimates for how long their work will take, and there's no historical data from similar projects?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that without historical data, all estimates are uncertain, and the goal is to build a timeline that accounts for that uncertainty rather than pretending it doesn't exist. I'd bring the leads from each team together for a structured estimation session. For each major work package, I'd ask each lead to provide three estimates: optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic. This forces them to think about what could go wrong, not just the ideal case. I'd then use a simple PERT-style weighted average (optimistic + 4× most likely + pessimistic, divided by 6) as a baseline, but I'd also flag the pessimistic estimates as contingency triggers. I'd identify dependencies explicitly — for example, firmware can't start motor control development until hardware delivers a prototype, but they can start communication protocol work earlier. I'd look for opportunities to parallelize work: can regulatory begin drafting the 60601 test plan based on preliminary block diagrams before the final schematic is done? Can hardware order long-lead components before the layout is complete? I'd also build in explicit review gates between phases, not just calendar dates, so that progress is measured by deliverables completed, not time elapsed. Finally, I'd communicate the timeline to stakeholders with clear confidence levels: "We're 80% confident we can deliver by this date based on these assumptions, and here are the top three risks that could push us beyond that." This sets realistic expectations and gives the team room to adjust without overpromising.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where one team's pessimistic estimate is significantly longer than another team's optimistic estimate for a dependent task? What would you do if a stakeholder demands a single-date commitment despite the uncertainty?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when team morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than systemic issues?

**Answer:** I would structure the post-mortem to focus on process and system factors, not people. Before the meeting, I'd set the tone by sending a written framework: we're looking for what in our development process allowed this outcome, not who made which decision. I'd use a structured format like a fishbone diagram or a timeline-based "what happened, what should have happened, what can we learn" approach. I'd start the meeting by acknowledging the disappointment and frustration openly, then state explicitly that blame is off the table — we're here to improve the system, not assign fault. I'd ask each team to present their timeline and decision points without interruption, then collectively identify where information was missed, assumptions went untested, or dependencies were poorly managed. I'd look for patterns: were there repeated scope changes without timeline adjustments? Did early warning signs get dismissed? Were there communication breakdowns between hardware and firmware teams? I'd capture each finding as a process improvement opportunity, not a failure. For example, if the root cause was that a critical component had a 20-week lead time that wasn't identified until late in the project, the corrective action isn't "the hardware lead should have checked earlier" — it's "we need a formal long-lead-item review at project kickoff." I'd assign owners to each action item and schedule a follow-up to verify implementation. Finally, I'd close by thanking the team for their honesty and reinforcing that every project teaches us something, and the goal is to make the next one better.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a team member who insists on blaming a specific individual during the post-mortem? What would you do if the same systemic issues appear in multiple post-mortems without improvement?

## Q5: How would you approach convincing a regulatory affairs team to accept a hardware design change late in a medical device development cycle, when the change improves safety but introduces schedule risk?

**Answer:** I would approach this as a risk-benefit analysis, not a negotiation. First, I'd prepare a clear, data-driven justification for the change: what specific safety improvement does it provide, what is the quantified risk reduction (e.g., eliminates a single-point failure mode, reduces the likelihood of a hazardous situation), and what is the evidence supporting it — simulation results, bench testing, or field data from similar devices. I'd also prepare a thorough impact assessment: which design history file documents would need updating (schematics, BOM, layout, verification test plans, risk management file), what additional testing would be required, and what is the realistic schedule impact with contingency. I'd present this to the regulatory team as a formal change request with options: Option A is to implement the change now with a three-month delay; Option B is to defer the change to a post-market revision with a field safety notice; Option C is to accept the current design with the identified risk documented in the risk management file. I'd include the regulatory implications of each option — for example, Option A might require a new 510(k) submission if the change affects safety or performance, while Option B might require a correction report to the FDA. I'd ask the regulatory team to evaluate the options against the regulatory framework and help me understand which path is most defensible. The key is to frame it as a shared decision: we both want a safe device that meets regulatory requirements, and we need their expertise to navigate the trade-offs. If they ultimately reject the change, I'd document the decision with rationale and ensure the risk is formally accepted in the risk management file.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the regulatory team agrees the change is safer but insists it cannot be accommodated within the current submission timeline, and the business is unwilling to delay the launch? How would you document the decision to proceed without the safety improvement?
