# behavioral-leadership — Day 105

## Q1: How would you approach leading a team through a situation where a design decision you championed turns out to be wrong, and the team needs to change course without losing confidence in your judgment?

**Answer:** The first move is to own it explicitly and early — name the decision, name what the evidence now shows, and separate the decision from the people who executed it. If the team senses you're hedging or quietly looking for someone else to absorb the blame, the real damage isn't the wrong decision, it's the loss of psychological safety that follows. So I'd frame it plainly: "I chose this approach based on what we knew at the time; here's the new data; here's why it changes the picture."

The second move is to make the reversal a structured decision rather than a scramble. I'd want the same rigor applied to the correction that should have been applied to the original call — what are the options now, what's the cost of each, what do we know versus assume. That does two things: it produces a better answer, and it demonstrates that the process is sound even when a particular outcome wasn't. A team can trust a process that self-corrects; what erodes trust is a leader who treats being wrong as unacceptable and therefore hides it.

Third, I'd be deliberate about the retrospective. The useful question isn't "who picked the wrong option" but "what signal did we have, or could we have gotten cheaply, that would have pointed the other way sooner?" Sometimes the honest answer is that the decision was reasonable given the information and the outcome was genuinely uncertain — and saying that out loud matters, because otherwise the team learns to be paralyzed rather than decisive. I'd want to extract a concrete lesson (a prototype we should have built, a test we should have run earlier) and change the process, not just the design.

Finally, I'd watch for the second-order effect: after a public reversal, teams often over-correct into excessive caution. I'd explicitly signal that the goal is calibrated risk-taking, not risk avoidance, and that I'd rather make a well-reasoned call that occasionally needs revisiting than freeze on every decision.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a decision that was genuinely reasonable given the information and one that reflected a process gap you should have caught? What would you do if a team member had raised the concern that turned out to be correct, but was overruled in the original decision?

## Q2: How would you approach setting technical direction for a team when you're not the deepest expert in the room on the specific technology involved?

**Answer:** I'd start by being clear about what my role actually is in that situation. Technical leadership isn't being the best engineer on every topic — it's making sure the right expertise is surfaced, the trade-offs are framed honestly, and a decision gets made and documented with its rationale. If I pretend to expertise I don't have, I lose credibility fast and I risk overriding the person who actually knows.

Practically, I'd identify who holds the real depth — sometimes one person, sometimes it's split — and structure the decision so their input carries appropriate weight without letting a single voice become unchallengeable. That means asking the expert to explain their reasoning in terms the rest of the team can evaluate, not just stating a conclusion. If the reasoning holds up to scrutiny from people outside the specialty, that's a good sign; if it can't be explained, that's worth probing, not because the expert is wrong but because unexamined reasoning is where errors hide.

I'd also be honest with the team about the boundary of my knowledge. Saying "I don't have the depth to adjudicate this detail, but I can see the system-level implications and here's what I need to understand to make the call" is more useful than bluffing. It also models the behavior I want — people flagging the edges of their own competence rather than papering over them.

Where I add the most value is at the interfaces: how this decision interacts with schedule, with regulatory constraints, with the rest of the system, with the team's capacity to maintain it. The deep expert often isn't tracking those, and that's exactly the gap a lead should fill. And I'd make sure credit flows visibly to the people whose expertise carried the decision — that's both fair and it reinforces that the team's depth is valued.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the deepest expert on the team turned out to be wrong, and you didn't have the background to catch it yourself? How do you build enough technical literacy to ask useful questions of a specialist without pretending to be one?

## Q3: How would you approach a situation where your team is consistently delivering to plan, but you suspect the plan itself has become too conservative — the team is comfortable and no longer being stretched?

**Answer:** First I'd want to test whether the suspicion is real or just a feeling, because "the team seems too comfortable" is exactly the kind of impression that can be wrong in both directions. The evidence I'd look for: are estimates consistently beaten by a wide margin, are the same people doing the same class of work without new challenge, is technical debt accumulating because nobody's taking on the harder problems, are the interesting or risky problems being quietly avoided? Comfort can look like competence from the outside.

If the evidence supports it, I'd be careful about the instinct to just pile on more work or raise targets. That tends to produce either burnout or sandbagging, and it treats the symptom rather than the cause. The more useful question is *why* the plan got conservative — often it's a rational response to a past where over-commitment was punished, or a lack of visibility into what's actually achievable, or genuinely a team that's mastered its domain and needs a new challenge rather than more of the same.

So I'd look at it as a growth problem, not a throughput problem. What's the next capability the team should build — a technology they haven't used, a class of problem they've been routing around, ownership of a decision they've always deferred upward? Stretching a team is more durable when it's toward something they want to get good at rather than just a higher number on the same work. I'd also want to make the stretch safe: if I'm asking people to take on harder problems, I have to accept that estimates will be less reliable and some attempts will fail, and I need to say that explicitly or nobody will take the risk.

I'd also check my own contribution to the conservatism. If I've been the one quietly padding estimates or shielding the team from hard problems, that's on me to change first. And I'd involve the team in the diagnosis — asking them directly where they feel under-challenged often surfaces better answers than my outside read, and it makes the resulting change something they own rather than something imposed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between a team that's under-challenged and one that's simply efficient because it has genuinely mastered the work? What would you do if raising the ambition level caused a drop in delivery reliability that stakeholders noticed?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding what to delegate and what to keep, as a technical lead, when you're accountable for outcomes but can't personally do everything?

**Answer:** The default should be to delegate more than feels comfortable, but the criterion isn't "can someone else do this" — it's "what does the team and the person need to grow, and what's the cost if this goes wrong." I'd sort work along two axes: how much the outcome matters and is hard to reverse, and how much growth value there is in someone else owning it. High-stakes and hard-to-reverse work I'd keep closer, at least until I trust the person's judgment on that specific class of problem. High-growth-value work I'd push out even if I could do it faster myself, because the short-term speed isn't worth the long-term capability.

The trap I'd actively avoid is delegating only the work I don't want to do. That's task-offloading, not delegation, and it teaches the team that ownership means getting the leftovers. The work worth delegating deliberately is the work that builds judgment — owning a design decision end-to-end, running a review, being the one who has to defend a trade-off to another team. Those are the experiences that turn a strong executor into someone who can be trusted with the next level.

I'd also be explicit about the difference between delegating a task and delegating a decision. If I hand someone a decision but then second-guess every call, I've delegated responsibility without authority, which is worse than not delegating at all. So when I delegate a decision, I'd set the boundaries up front — what they can decide alone, what they should check with me on, what's genuinely theirs — and then actually stay out of it unless a boundary is crossed. Reviewing outcomes rather than every step is how you build people who can operate without you.

And I'd keep a deliberate eye on what I'm holding. If I'm the only person who can do something important, that's a single point of failure I've created, and it's my job to fix it — by pairing, by documenting, by handing it over with support — before it becomes a crisis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a delegated decision went badly and you were still accountable for the outcome? How do you calibrate how much rope to give someone without either micromanaging or setting them up to fail?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a team through a period of significant organizational change — a reorganization, a change of ownership, or a shift in strategy — when the technical work still has to get done and morale is unsettled?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate what I can control from what I can't, and be honest with the team about which is which. During organizational change, the most corrosive thing is ambiguity dressed up as reassurance — people can tell when they're being managed, and vague optimism reads as either ignorance or dishonesty. What they need from a technical lead is a clear, honest picture of what's known, what's not, and what it means for their work specifically. "I don't know yet whether this affects our headcount, and I'll tell you as soon as I do" builds more trust than a confident-sounding non-answer.

The second thing is to protect the work that gives people stability. Change is disorienting partly because it disrupts the routines and relationships that make work feel coherent. Where I can, I'd keep the technical cadence steady — the reviews, the rituals, the clarity of what we're building and why — because that continuity is grounding. I'd also be deliberate about reconnecting the work to purpose: during upheaval, people lose sight of why the work matters, and a lead who can keep that thread visible is doing real leadership.

Third, I'd watch for the people who go quiet. In unsettled periods, the loudest concerns are usually visible, but the people who are quietly disengaging — updating their résumés, disinvesting — are often the ones you most need to keep. I'd make time for individual conversations, not to extract loyalty but to understand what each person is worried about, because those worries are usually concrete and sometimes addressable even when the big picture isn't.

Finally, I'd be realistic that I can't fully insulate the team, and I shouldn't pretend to. What I can do is be a stable, honest presence, keep the technical work meaningful and well-run, and make sure people aren't blindsided by things I could have told them earlier. Change is survivable when people trust the person leading them through it; that trust is built on candor, not on promises I can't keep.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the organizational change meant you had to deliver news to your team that you personally disagreed with? What would you do if the change created a genuine risk to a safety-critical deliverable and the decision-makers didn't seem to appreciate it?
