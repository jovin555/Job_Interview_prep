# behavioral-leadership — Day 118

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two problems, because they have different fixes. The stalling review is a process problem; the team routing around it is a symptom that the process has lost credibility, and that's the more dangerous of the two because it means changes are happening without the scrutiny the review was supposed to provide.

On the stalling itself, I'd want to understand whether the reviewer's objections are substantive or whether they're using the review as a design session. Those look similar from the outside but need different responses. If the objections are substantive — genuine risks being surfaced late — then the real problem is that the review is happening too late in the design cycle, and the fix is upstream: earlier, cheaper checkpoints where concerns can be raised before the design is frozen. If the objections are more like ongoing design preferences, then the review has drifted from "is this fit for purpose and safe" into "would I have done it differently," and that needs to be named explicitly with a clear scope for what the review is and isn't for.

Practically, I'd introduce a few structural changes: require objections to be submitted in writing before the session so they can be triaged and batched rather than discovered live; timebox the review and park genuinely new concerns into a follow-up action rather than letting them extend the meeting; and track objections as action items with owners and closure criteria, so the same concern can't be re-raised indefinitely without new evidence. If a reviewer keeps raising new material, that becomes a conversation with them directly — not punitive, but asking whether there's an underlying concern they haven't been able to articulate, because sometimes serial objections are a symptom of a reviewer who doesn't feel heard on something bigger.

On the routing-around, I'd address it openly rather than pretending it isn't happening. The team isn't doing it maliciously — they're doing it because the process is blocking them and they still have a job to do. I'd acknowledge that, explain what's changing to make the review worth their time, and be clear that bypassing review on safety-relevant decisions isn't acceptable regardless of how frustrating the process is. The goal is to make the legitimate path faster than the workaround, because you can't sustain compliance by willpower alone.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the serial objector is more senior than you and doesn't accept the process changes? What would you do if you discovered the team had already shipped a change that bypassed review?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether to escalate a technical disagreement between two senior engineers to your manager, versus resolving it within the team?

**Answer:** My default is to resolve it within the team, because escalation has a real cost — it signals the team can't self-govern, it puts the decision in the hands of someone with less context, and it can damage the relationship between the two engineers if it feels like one of them "went over." But "default to resolving" isn't the same as "always resolve," and I'd use a few criteria to decide when escalation is actually the right call.

First, is the disagreement technical or is it about something else — ownership, credit, history between the two people? If it's genuinely technical, it's usually resolvable with data: a prototype, a bench measurement, a calculation, a review of the requirements. If it's personal, no amount of data will settle it, and escalation or mediation may be the only path. Second, does the decision have a deadline that the team can't meet on its own? If the two engineers are deadlocked and the schedule is bleeding, escalation to unblock is legitimate even if the technical question is still open — the manager can make a call with the information available. Third, is the decision above the team's authority — does it affect commitments, budget, or cross-team interfaces that the manager owns? If so, it's not really the team's decision to make, and escalating is correct.

If I do decide to resolve internally, I'd structure it: get both engineers to write down their position and the evidence for it, identify the specific points of disagreement (often there are fewer than it feels like), and design a test or analysis that would distinguish between them. If a prototype is feasible, that's usually the fastest path to a decision both people can accept, because it moves the argument from opinion to evidence. I'd also make sure the decision and its rationale get documented, so it doesn't get relitigated.

If I escalate, I'd do it transparently — both engineers in the room or at least aware, framing it as "this needs a decision above our level" rather than "one of you is being unreasonable." And I'd bring the manager a clear statement of the options and trade-offs, not just "these two disagree."

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if your manager made a decision you thought was technically wrong? How would you prevent the same two engineers from deadlocking again on the next decision?

## Q3: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument?

**Answer:** The hardest part of this is that "gut feeling" concerns are usually right more often than teams give them credit for, but they're also the easiest to dismiss, so the culture has to make it safe and worthwhile to voice them without requiring the person to have already done the analysis.

I'd start by modeling the behavior myself — explicitly inviting dissent in reviews, and when someone raises a half-formed concern, treating it as worth investigating rather than something to be batted down. The phrase I'd try to normalize is something like "I can't fully articulate why, but something about this worries me" being a legitimate thing to say in a review, followed by "let's figure out what's bugging you" rather than "come back when you have data." A lot of the time, five minutes of questioning turns a vague unease into a specific, checkable concern — and occasionally it turns out to be nothing, which is fine, because the cost of investigating is low and the cost of suppressing is high.

Structurally, I'd build in mechanisms that don't depend on someone being brave in a room full of senior people: anonymous or low-stakes channels for raising concerns before a decision is finalized, a "pre-mortem" step where the team is explicitly asked to imagine the design has failed and say why, and a norm that decisions get a short "what would change our mind" note attached so concerns have somewhere to land. I'd also make sure that when someone raises a concern and it turns out to be valid, that's visible — publicly credited — because the culture is built by what gets rewarded, not what gets announced.

The senior-person part is the trickiest. If a senior engineer's decision gets challenged and the challenger is quietly penalized — even subtly, through tone or later treatment — the culture dies. So I'd be deliberate about how senior people respond to being questioned, and if I'm the senior person, I'd go out of my way to thank people for raising concerns and to change my mind visibly when the concern is right. A senior person changing their mind in public is worth more than any amount of exhortation about speaking up.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a "gut feeling" concern turned out to be wrong and cost the team time — how do you keep that from discouraging future concerns? What would you do if a senior engineer consistently responded to concerns with visible irritation?

## Q4: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct is to either approve conditionally to keep the schedule or to cancel and reschedule, and both are wrong in different ways. Approving without the calculations means the safety-critical decision hasn't actually been reviewed — the review becomes theater, and if something goes wrong later, the record shows the team signed off on a decision nobody verified. Cancelling outright burns the only slot and probably doesn't get the calculations any faster.

What I'd do is run the review on everything that *can* be reviewed — the architecture, the interfaces, the parts of the design that don't depend on the missing calculations — and explicitly carve out the safety-critical decision as "not reviewed, pending calculations." That keeps the milestone moving on the parts that are ready while being honest that one decision is still open. I'd set a hard, short deadline for the calculations to be submitted and reviewed asynchronously, with a named reviewer and a clear criterion for what "sufficient" looks like, and I'd make sure the milestone gate records the open item rather than papering over it.

I'd also want to understand *why* the package was late and incomplete, because that's the real problem. Was the presenter overloaded? Did they not know the calculations were expected? Is this a one-off or a pattern? If it's a one-off, a direct conversation about the impact is enough. If it's a pattern, the fix is upstream — clearer review-package standards, earlier check-ins before the review, or workload rebalancing. And if the calculations genuinely weren't done because the presenter didn't think they were needed, that's a much bigger issue about what the team understands a safety-critical decision to require, and it needs addressing at the culture level, not just this one review.

The one thing I would not do is let the milestone gate pass as if the review were complete. A gate that can be passed without the safety-critical work being done isn't a gate.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the presenter pushed back and said the calculations were obvious and didn't need documenting? How would you handle it if this were the third time this presenter had arrived at a review with an incomplete package?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than systemic issues?

**Answer:** The first job is to set the frame before anyone walks into the room, because a post-mortem that turns into a blame session makes the next one impossible to run honestly. I'd be explicit up front: the purpose is to understand what happened and change the system so it doesn't happen again, not to identify who to punish. That's not just a platitude — I'd back it up by structuring the discussion around the system, not the people: what information was available and when, what decisions were made and on what basis, what constraints the team was operating under, where the process had gaps. If someone made a call that in hindsight was wrong, the useful question is "what made that call look reasonable at the time," because that's what tells you what to fix.

I'd also want to acknowledge the morale problem directly rather than pretending the meeting is neutral. Missing a deadline by months is demoralizing, and people come in defensive or checked out. Naming that — "this has been a hard few months, and the point of today is to make sure the next project doesn't repeat it" — tends to lower the temperature and lets people engage.

On the analysis itself, I'd use a structured method rather than an open discussion, because open discussion defaults to the loudest or most senior voice and to the most recent cause. A timeline reconstruction first — what happened, when, what was known — then a structured cause analysis (fishbone or 5 whys applied to the major decision points) tends to surface the systemic contributors: estimation that didn't account for unknowns, a gate that was passed without the work being done, a dependency that wasn't tracked, a resourcing gap that everyone knew about but nobody escalated. Those are the things that are actually fixable. Individual mistakes usually trace back to one of them.

The output should be a small number of specific, owned, checkable actions — not a long list that nobody follows up on. And I'd schedule a follow-up to verify the actions actually happened and had the intended effect, because a post-mortem whose actions quietly die is worse than no post-mortem, since it teaches the team that the exercise is theater.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the post-mortem surfaced that a specific individual's performance was a genuine contributing factor — how do you handle that without turning the session into blame? How would you keep the team's morale from dropping further during the post-mortem itself?
