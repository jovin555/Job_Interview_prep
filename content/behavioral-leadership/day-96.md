# behavioral-leadership — Day 96

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but the review package arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The first move is to separate the two problems: the process failure (late, incomplete package) and the substantive risk (a safety-critical decision without documented justification). Conflating them in the room tends to make the presenter defensive and the reviewers either rubber-stamp or grandstand. I'd open by stating the purpose of the meeting explicitly — we are here to decide whether the safety-critical item is adequately justified, not to review the whole design — and then run the review in two passes. Pass one: walk the safety-critical path only, and for each claim ask "what is the calculation or evidence behind this, and where is it recorded?" If the calculation exists but wasn't written up, the presenter can often reconstruct the reasoning verbally, and the action item becomes "document it in the DHF with the reviewer sign-off." If the calculation genuinely doesn't exist, that is a finding, not a formality — the item cannot be closed at this gate, and I'd say so plainly and propose the smallest path to closure (a targeted calculation, a bench measurement, or a bounded analysis) with an owner and a date. Pass two, if time allows: everything else, logged as action items rather than resolved live. Throughout, I'd keep the critique aimed at the artifact and the evidence trail, not the person — "the package doesn't yet show the derating basis for this part" rather than "you didn't do the calculation." Afterwards I'd follow up in writing with the action list and, separately, a short conversation with the presenter about the pattern of late packages, because one late package is a scheduling accident and three is a process problem that needs fixing upstream (review package checklist, a pre-review gate a day before, etc.). The milestone gate itself is a decision I'd make on evidence, not on schedule pressure — if the evidence isn't there, the honest outcome is a conditional pass with a hard closure date, or a slip, and I'd communicate that to stakeholders rather than let the review quietly absorb the risk.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the presenter pushes back that the calculation is "obvious" and doesn't need writing down, how do you handle that in the room? What would you do if the milestone owner pressures you to close the review anyway because the schedule can't move?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures punish the instinct to fix first and understand later, because a fix you can't verify against a reproduction is a guess, and in a regulated device a guess that "seems to work" is worse than an open problem — it closes the investigation without closing the cause. So the first thing I'd do is resist the pressure explicitly and reframe it: the fastest path to a durable fix is to convert the intermittent into something observable, and that is a bounded, time-boxed activity, not an open-ended science project. Concretely: contain first — if the failure mode has patient-safety or field implications, contain it (screening, added test coverage, a conservative firmware guard, limiting shipment) so the schedule pressure is reduced while the investigation runs. Then instrument: add logging or tracing around the suspect subsystem, capture the state at failure (timestamps, register snapshots, bus traffic, environmental conditions, power rail behavior), and gather the field or test data that already exists — how often, under what conditions, which units, which firmware/hardware revisions. Build a hypothesis list (fishbone across hardware, firmware, environment, manufacturing, use) and rank by how well each explains the observed pattern, then design experiments that discriminate between hypotheses rather than confirm a favorite — stress the boundary conditions (temperature, supply margin, timing margin, EMI), run accelerated loops, and use statistical thinking: if it fails one in a thousand cycles, you need enough cycles to see it, and you need to know what "enough" means before you start. I'd also make sure the investigation has a single owner and a shared log so findings aren't lost between shifts. On the pressure: I'd give stakeholders a clear statement of what is known, what is being done to make it observable, and what decision points exist — including the option of shipping with containment if the risk can be bounded — so they're making an informed call rather than being told "we're working on it." Verification of effectiveness comes last and matters most: once a candidate cause is identified, the fix is only accepted when the reproduction (or a designed equivalent) no longer fails, and the reasoning is documented with the evidence.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide when to stop investigating and ship with containment versus hold the release? What does "verification of effectiveness" look like when you never had a reliable reproduction in the first place?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is the same: the firmware team ends up either ignoring a real constraint or over-engineering around a phantom one. So I'd aim for a shared, quantified model rather than a prose description. That means sitting down with the firmware lead and expressing the constraint in the units and terms their code actually sees: not "the analog front end is noisy" but "at the sensor's full-scale output, the noise floor is roughly X LSBs peak-to-peak at the ADC input, dominated by this frequency band, and here is the measured distribution." From there the conversation becomes concrete: what does that imply for effective resolution, what filtering or averaging is appropriate, what settling time the filter costs, whether the sampling rate and the filter budget fit the control loop, and where the firmware should and shouldn't try to compensate in software. I'd bring the evidence — a noise measurement, a spectrum, a scope capture, a characterization plot — because firmware engineers rightly distrust verbal constraints, and a plot settles arguments faster than a meeting. I'd also be explicit about what is a hard limit versus a design margin versus an assumption that could change, so the firmware team knows which numbers are load-bearing. And I'd close the loop: agree on a joint verification — a test that exercises the analog path end-to-end and confirms the combined hardware-plus-firmware behavior meets the system requirement — so neither side is guessing whether the other held up its end. The goal is that the firmware engineer can point to a number and a rationale in their own design notes, and the hardware engineer can see their constraint reflected faithfully rather than caricatured.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the firmware team proposes a software workaround that masks the hardware noise but degrades a different performance parameter? How do you keep the shared model current when either the hardware or the firmware changes later in the project?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** Deference to seniority is often a rational response to never having been given real ownership — if every decision gets overridden, people stop making them. So the mentoring goal isn't to build confidence in the abstract; it's to create a series of bounded situations where the engineer owns a decision, lives with its consequences, and gets feedback on the reasoning rather than just the outcome. I'd start by scoping a decision that is genuinely theirs: something with real trade-offs but contained blast radius — a component selection, a filter topology, a test approach — where I commit in advance to accepting their call unless it crosses a safety or compliance line, and where I say so out loud so the team knows the decision is theirs. Before the decision, I'd work with them on framing: what are the options, what are the criteria, what evidence would discriminate, what's the cost of being wrong. During, I'd resist the urge to answer when they ask "what would you do?" — instead reflect the question back or ask what their recommendation is and why, so the muscle gets exercised. After, a short retrospective: was the reasoning sound, what would they do differently, what did the outcome teach. In group settings, I'd deliberately route questions to them first and visibly back their answer when it's sound, which changes how the room treats them as much as how they see themselves. Over time I'd increase the scope and the ambiguity, and shift from reviewing decisions to reviewing the decision-making process. The measure of success is when they start disagreeing with me in a design review with a reasoned argument — that's the signal the deference has been replaced by judgment.

**Possible follow-ups:** What do you do if one of their early owned decisions goes badly — how does that affect the mentoring plan? How would you handle it if a more senior engineer keeps overriding them despite your efforts to give them ownership?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a technical disagreement turns personal, more debate makes it worse — each side starts arguing to win rather than to find the better answer, and the actual trade-offs get buried. So the first job is to de-escalate and re-frame before any technical work resumes. I'd separate the people from the problem explicitly: name that the disagreement has become about positions rather than evidence, and reset the ground rules — we are going to decide on criteria and evidence, and we are going to write down the decision and its rationale so it doesn't get re-litigated. Then I'd do the technical work that should have happened earlier: get both sides to agree, in writing, on the criteria that matter for this product (safety, reliability, cost, schedule, manufacturability, power, regulatory risk, team familiarity), and weight them. Often the disagreement shrinks once the criteria are explicit, because the two camps were optimizing different things without saying so. Where a criterion is genuinely contested, I'd look for a way to settle it with data rather than opinion — a prototype, a benchmark, a targeted analysis, a review of similar designs — and time-box it. If after that the architectures are still close, that's useful information: it means the decision is low-stakes on the merits, and I'd make the call, document the reasoning and the dissenting view, and set a review point where we'd revisit if the assumptions change. I'd also address the personal dimension directly but privately with each lead — not to assign blame, but to make clear that attributing motives to colleagues isn't acceptable and that reasoned dissent is welcome while disparagement isn't. The public resolution should leave both sides able to say "I disagreed, the decision was made on stated criteria, and I can live with it," which is what a healthy engineering culture looks like after a hard call.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if one side's position is technically weaker but held by the more senior or more influential engineer — how do you keep the process fair? How do you document a decision with a dissenting view in a way that satisfies a regulatory reviewer without looking like unresolved conflict?
