# behavioral-leadership — Day 8

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design review for a medical device PCB when you discover, mid-review, that a critical safety-related trace width calculation was never documented, and the original designer is no longer with the company?

**Answer:** I would first pause the review at that specific item and clearly flag it as a gap rather than trying to guess the designer's intent. The immediate priority is containment — determining whether the board as currently laid out could pose a safety risk if manufactured as-is. I'd ask the team to check if the trace in question carries fault current or is part of a safety-critical path (e.g., a fuse-to-load connection or a protective earth bond). If it is, I'd recommend we stop the review and treat this as a potential non-conformance until we can reconstruct the rationale.

For the reconstruction, I'd gather whatever artifacts exist — email threads, earlier schematic versions, simulation files, or even test data from prototype builds. I'd also look at the adjacent circuitry: if the trace connects to a component with known current ratings, we can work backward to estimate the intended current. If we still can't determine the design intent, I'd treat it as an unknown and require a fresh calculation and verification test before the design can proceed. I'd document the entire investigation in the design history file, including the gap found, the reconstruction effort, and the final decision. For future reviews, I'd add a checklist item requiring that critical trace width calculations be documented with their assumptions and referenced standards (e.g., IPC-2221 or IEC 60601-1 Table 6 for medical devices).

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the trace width appears adequate by calculation but the original designer's notes suggest they used a different standard than what your team currently follows? What if the board has already been sent to fabrication and the gap is discovered during incoming inspection?

## Q2: How would you approach managing a situation where two senior engineers on your team have a fundamental disagreement about whether to use a hardware-based watchdog timer or a software watchdog with a separate supervisory IC for a life-support medical device, and the decision is blocking the project timeline?

**Answer:** I'd start by framing the disagreement as a technical trade-off that needs data, not a personal conflict. I'd schedule a focused session where each engineer presents their case with specific references to the applicable standards — for a life-support device, IEC 60601-1 Clause 14 (programmable electrical medical systems) and IEC 62304 would be the relevant frameworks. I'd ask each to address: what failure modes does their approach cover or miss? What is the diagnostic coverage for each? How does each approach handle the single-fault condition requirements?

If the disagreement persists after the data is on the table, I'd look for a middle-ground solution that captures the strengths of both. For example, a hardware watchdog timer (external IC with independent clock) combined with a software supervisory loop that checks task execution — this is common in medical devices because it provides redundancy without relying solely on the main processor's internal watchdog, which could fail if the clock fails. I'd also consider prototyping both approaches on a development board and running fault injection tests to compare behavior under realistic failure conditions (clock failure, brownout, firmware lock-up).

If we still can't reach consensus, I'd escalate to the system architect or a senior technical reviewer from outside the team, framing the decision as a risk-based choice with documented trade-offs. The key is that the decision gets made with a clear rationale recorded in the design history file, not that everyone agrees perfectly. I'd also set a firm deadline for the decision — no more than one week — and communicate to the project manager that we'll have an answer by that date, even if it requires an external tie-breaker.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if one engineer's approach is clearly safer but significantly more expensive, and the product manager is pushing for the cheaper option? What if the disagreement is actually about interpretation of a specific clause in IEC 60601-1?

## Q3: How would you approach building a risk management culture on a medical device team that currently treats ISO 14971 documentation as a checkbox exercise done after the design is complete?

**Answer:** I'd start by reframing risk management as a design tool rather than a documentation burden. The most effective way to shift this mindset is to integrate risk analysis activities directly into the design process so engineers see the value in real time. For example, during the architecture phase, I'd facilitate a hazard identification session where the team brainstorms what could go wrong with their design — not to fill out a template, but to identify genuine design weaknesses early. When someone suggests a hazard, I'd ask "how would we detect this in the field?" and "what could we change in the circuit to prevent it?" This makes the connection between risk analysis and better design immediately tangible.

I'd also change how we review risk management files. Instead of reviewing the risk document separately from the design, I'd pair them: in a design review, we'd look at a specific circuit block and then immediately review the corresponding risk control measures. If a risk control is "software checks sensor value before acting," we'd verify that the firmware team actually implemented that check and that the test plan covers it. This closes the loop and shows that risk management isn't abstract paperwork — it's directly tied to what gets built and tested.

For teams that are particularly resistant, I'd start small. Pick one subsystem on the current project — ideally one that's already causing reliability issues — and do a thorough FMEA on it. Show the team how the analysis identifies failure modes they hadn't considered, and how the resulting design changes actually improve the product. Once they see the practical benefit, they're usually more willing to adopt the process for other subsystems. I'd also make sure to celebrate wins: when a risk analysis catches a real design flaw before prototype testing, that's a success story worth sharing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a senior engineer who argues that risk management slows down innovation and that their previous non-medical projects succeeded without it? What metrics would you use to measure whether the culture is actually improving?

## Q4: How would you approach handling a situation where a regulatory audit is scheduled in two weeks, and you discover that several key design verification test reports are missing signatures or have incomplete data that cannot be re-run in time?

**Answer:** The first step is triage — I'd categorize each gap by its severity and impact on the audit. Some missing signatures might be administrative (the test was performed and data is valid, but the reviewer never signed off), while incomplete data could indicate that a test was never properly executed. I'd work with the quality team to determine which gaps are critical for the audit scope and which can be addressed with a corrective action plan rather than completed test reports.

For missing signatures on otherwise complete reports, I'd track down the original reviewers and get signatures as quickly as possible. If a reviewer is unavailable (on leave, left the company), I'd follow the company's deviation process — typically, a qualified alternate can review and sign, with documentation explaining the substitution. For incomplete data, I'd assess whether the missing data can be reconstructed from raw test logs, oscilloscope captures, or other records. If the test was recorded but the report was never finalized, we might be able to complete the report with the existing data and a clear explanation of the reconstruction.

For tests that genuinely cannot be re-run or reconstructed, I'd prepare a gap analysis document that explains what happened, why the data is missing, and what corrective action is planned. During the audit, I'd proactively present this to the auditor rather than waiting for them to find it. Auditors generally appreciate transparency and a clear plan — hiding gaps or trying to fabricate data would be far worse. I'd also work with the regulatory team to determine if the missing data affects the device's conformity assessment or if it can be addressed through a post-audit corrective action plan. Finally, I'd use this as a learning opportunity to improve the test report review process — for example, implementing a checklist that requires sign-off before test data is archived, or setting up automated reminders for reviewers.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the missing data is for a test that was required by a specific standard (e.g., IEC 60601-1-2 EMC testing) and the test lab no longer has the original setup? What if a team member suggests backdating signatures to avoid the audit finding?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a cross-functional root-cause investigation when a critical issue is discovered during regulatory compliance testing of a medical device, and the initial reaction from the team is to blame the test setup rather than the design?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that test setup issues are a legitimate possibility — regulatory compliance testing often involves complex fixtures, cables, and measurement equipment that can introduce artifacts. Dismissing that concern outright would damage trust and discourage open discussion. However, I'd frame the investigation so that we systematically rule out test setup issues before concluding they're the root cause, rather than assuming them from the start.

I'd structure the investigation using a fishbone diagram, with the team contributing potential causes across categories: test equipment, test procedure, environmental conditions, design, manufacturing variance, and operator error. This visually demonstrates that we're considering all possibilities, including the test setup, but also forces us to look at design-related causes. I'd then prioritize the investigation based on which causes are easiest to verify or eliminate. For example, we might start by reviewing the test setup against the standard's requirements — if the test lab used a different cable length or grounding configuration than specified, that's a quick check that could either confirm or eliminate the setup as a factor.

If the test setup checks out, I'd move to design-related hypotheses. I'd ask the hardware team to review the schematic and layout for the specific circuit that failed, looking for anything that could cause the observed failure mode. I'd also ask the firmware team if any software behavior could trigger or exacerbate the issue. Throughout the investigation, I'd keep the focus on data and evidence rather than opinions. If someone says "the test setup is definitely the problem," I'd ask "what specific evidence would confirm or disprove that?" and assign someone to gather that evidence.

If the investigation ultimately points to a design issue, I'd present the findings in a way that doesn't assign blame but instead highlights the value of the testing process for catching the problem before the device reached patients. This helps the team see compliance testing as a safety net rather than an adversarial gate. I'd also document the investigation thoroughly in the design history file, including how we ruled out test setup issues, so that future reviewers can see the rigor of the analysis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the test lab insists their setup is correct, but your team's analysis suggests otherwise? What if the investigation reveals that the design issue was actually caused by a last-minute component substitution that wasn't properly documented?
