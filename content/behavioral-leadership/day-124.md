# behavioral-leadership — Day 124

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a team has quietly stopped escalating problems early — not because anyone decided to, but because past experience taught them that raising an issue early gets the messenger blamed or handed extra work?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is treat this as a systems problem rather than a discipline problem. If a team has learned that speaking up is punished, that's a rational adaptation to their environment, not a character flaw — so lecturing people about "speaking up" would just confirm their belief that the system is tone-deaf. I'd start by trying to surface the actual history: what happened the last few times someone flagged a risk early? Was the messenger pulled onto a firefighting task force? Was the person who raised the concern the one who ended up owning the remediation? Was there a visible moment where someone was blamed in a review? Getting concrete examples matters, because the fix has to address the specific pattern, not a generic "we value transparency" slogan.

Then I'd work on separating the act of raising a concern from the act of owning the fix. A lot of this pattern comes from conflating the two — people learn that flagging a problem means volunteering to solve it, on top of their existing work. So I'd try to make escalation cheap: a lightweight channel for flagging, an explicit norm that the person who raises an issue isn't automatically the person who owns it, and visible examples where someone flagged something early and the response was "thank you, here's how we'll resource it" rather than "great, it's yours." I'd also want to model it myself — raise my own uncertainties early and visibly, including ones that make me look less than fully on top of things, because the team will calibrate on what leadership actually does, not what it says.

Finally, I'd want to close the loop publicly. When someone raises a concern and it turns out to be real, that should be acknowledged. When it turns out not to be real, that should also be acknowledged as a good-faith flag rather than a false alarm — because if only correct escalations get rewarded, people learn to self-censor anything they're not certain about, which kills exactly the early, fuzzy signals you most want. I'd measure success informally by whether the volume and earliness of escalations goes up over a few months, and by whether the same issues stop showing up late.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the pattern traces back to a specific manager or lead rather than the team as a whole? What would you do if leadership above you genuinely does want to blame messengers, and you can't change that?

## Q2: How would you approach leading a technical decision when a proposed design change would improve performance but requires re-qualifying a component already approved in the design history file, and the schedule has no slack?

**Answer:** I'd start by being honest that this is not really a technical decision — it's a risk-and-evidence decision, and the right frame is "what do we actually know, and what would we be trading?" The performance improvement is one side of the ledger; the other side is the cost of re-qualification, the regulatory implications of touching an approved component, and the schedule risk if re-qualification surfaces something unexpected. I'd want the person proposing the change to quantify the benefit as concretely as possible — not "better performance" but "here's the margin we gain, here's the failure mode it removes, here's what happens if we don't do it." If the benefit is marginal, the answer is usually no, and it's a cleaner no when the benefit is stated precisely.

If the benefit is real, I'd look for ways to decouple the decision from the schedule. Is there a path where we ship the current qualified design and carry the improvement as a planned change for the next revision? Is there a subset of the change that doesn't touch the qualified component? Can we do the re-qualification work in parallel with the current schedule so the decision point moves later, when we have more information? The goal is to avoid a false binary between "ship as-is" and "blow the schedule," because there's often a third path that preserves optionality.

If it genuinely comes down to a binary, I'd make the trade-off explicit to the people who own the schedule and the regulatory risk, not decide it unilaterally in the engineering room. My job as lead is to make sure the decision is made with the real costs visible on both sides, and that whatever we decide is documented with the rationale — because if we decline the change, someone will ask in six months why we didn't take an obvious improvement, and "the schedule had no slack" needs to be written down as a conscious trade, not reconstructed from memory.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the performance improvement addresses a safety concern rather than a performance one? What would you do if the person proposing the change keeps re-litigating the decision after it's made?

## Q3: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across hardware, firmware, and verification teams whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?

**Answer:** The divergence is almost always invisible early because each team's definition of done is locally reasonable — it's just scoped to their own deliverable. Hardware thinks done means the board is built and brings up; firmware thinks done means the code runs on the board; verification thinks done means the requirements are tested. None of those is wrong, but none of them is "the system works and we can prove it," which is what the project actually needs. So the first move is to make the divergence visible on purpose, early, rather than waiting for it to surface as a late-stage surprise.

Practically, I'd want a single integration-level definition of done that's written down and agreed at the start of the project, expressed in terms of demonstrable system behavior rather than team deliverables. Something like: for a given milestone, here's the specific end-to-end scenario that must work, here's the evidence that proves it, and here's who signs off. That forces the conversation about interfaces and dependencies early — for example, if firmware's "done" assumes a hardware bring-up state that hardware doesn't consider done yet, that mismatch shows up in the definition rather than in week 40.

I'd also want staged integration points rather than a single big-bang integration at the end. If hardware, firmware, and verification only meet at the end, their definitions of done have no opportunity to collide until it's expensive. Regular joint checkpoints — even lightweight ones — where each team demonstrates against the shared definition, surface the gaps while they're still cheap to fix. And I'd want the verification team involved early enough that their definition of done shapes the design, rather than being applied to it after the fact. The cultural piece is framing this as "we're aligning on what success looks like" rather than "your definition of done is wrong," because the latter just makes people defensive about their own scope.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the teams agree on the definition in principle but quietly revert to their own working definitions under schedule pressure? What would you do if verification's definition of done is stricter than the project can afford, and the gap is a real cost rather than a misunderstanding?

## Q4: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but has a habit of answering every question with a justification of why their original choice was correct, rather than actually engaging with the concern being raised?

**Answer:** I'd separate the behavior from the intent. In my experience this pattern usually isn't arrogance — it's a defensive reflex that comes from associating "my design was questioned" with "my competence was questioned," and the justification is an attempt to close that gap quickly. So the intervention needs to make it safe to engage with a concern without it feeling like a concession of failure. If I frame it as "you're being defensive," I'll get exactly the defensiveness I'm trying to reduce.

In the review itself, I'd change the shape of the questions rather than the content. Instead of "why did you choose X?" — which invites a justification — I'd ask "what would have to be true for X to be the wrong choice?" or "what's the failure mode you're most worried about here?" Those questions are much harder to answer with a restatement of the original rationale, because they ask the presenter to reason forward rather than defend backward. I'd also model the behavior I want: when someone raises a concern about my own work, engage with the substance visibly, including saying "that's a fair point, I hadn't considered it" when it's true. The team calibrates on what leadership does.

Outside the review, I'd have a direct conversation with the presenter — not about the pattern in the abstract, but about a specific instance, framed around the cost: "when that concern came up, we didn't get to the bottom of it, and I think we lost something." I'd also want to check whether the review format itself is part of the problem — if reviews are structured as a presentation followed by a Q&A where the presenter is on the defensive, the format invites exactly this. A format where the presenter states the risky decisions and open questions up front, and the reviewers are explicitly tasked with probing those, changes the dynamic from "defend your work" to "help me stress-test it."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the presenter's designs are genuinely excellent and the defensiveness never causes a visible problem? What would you do if other reviewers have started avoiding raising concerns with this person because it's not worth the friction?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — something outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team with more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?

**Answer:** I'd start by being honest about why I'm tempted to take it. If the answer is "because I'm accountable and I want to control the outcome," that's a real consideration but it's also the exact reasoning that keeps leads from developing their teams and keeps them from doing the work only they can do. If the answer is "because I genuinely need to build this capability for the team's future," that's a different and better reason. So the first question is whether my taking it serves the project or serves my comfort.

Then I'd look at the actual risk profile. High-visibility and outside-my-depth is a bad combination if the cost of a miss is severe and there's no safety net — in that case, the responsible move might be to take it myself with explicit support, or to delegate with a much tighter review cadence than I'd normally use. But "high-visibility" often just means "people will notice," not "a miss is catastrophic," and those are different. If the downside is recoverable, delegating to someone with more relevant expertise is usually the better call even if they're junior, because the expertise gap is real and the accountability gap can be closed with structure — clear checkpoints, a defined review process, and me staying close enough to catch problems early without doing the work myself.

If I delegate, I'd want to be explicit about the accountability arrangement: the person owns the technical work, I own the outcome, and we both know what the checkpoints are and what "I need to escalate" looks like. I'd also want to be visible about the delegation — not in a way that undercuts them, but so that the people watching the high-visibility outcome know who's actually doing it and that I'm backing them. And I'd want to use the assignment as a genuine development opportunity, which means letting them make some calls I might have made differently, as long as those calls are within the risk envelope we agreed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the person you delegated to is clearly struggling and the visibility is making you nervous? What would you do if you took the stretch assignment yourself and discovered mid-way that you're further outside your depth than you thought?
