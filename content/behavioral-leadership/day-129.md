# behavioral-leadership — Day 129

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is under pressure to close out a design and one engineer keeps proposing "just in case" additions — extra filtering, an extra protection component, a spare sensor channel — that aren't justified by the requirements, and the additions are quietly accumulating scope?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements-traceability problem wearing the costume of an engineering-judgment problem. The first move is to make the accumulation visible: list every proposed addition, map each one to a specific requirement or identified risk, and mark the ones with no trace. That reframes the conversation from "is this engineer being difficult" to "which of these additions has a documented justification," which is a much less personal question. Some of the additions will turn out to be legitimate — a protection component that mitigates a real failure mode belongs in the risk file, not dismissed as scope creep — so I'd want to separate genuine risk mitigations from precautionary habits. For the ones without a trace, I'd ask the engineer to articulate the failure mode the addition protects against and its likelihood and severity; if it's a real concern, it should enter the risk management process properly, and if it can't be articulated, that's useful information for them too. I'd also make the cost of additions explicit — BOM cost, board area, test coverage, qualification effort — because "just in case" additions are rarely free, and seeing the accumulated cost often changes the conversation. Finally, I'd set a decision point: additions proposed after a certain stage need a documented rationale and a sign-off, not because I want to stifle caution but because unmanaged late changes are themselves a safety risk in a regulated device. The goal is a team that still raises concerns but does so through the risk process rather than through quiet accumulation.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer's "just in case" instinct turns out to be right — one of the additions later prevents a real field failure? How do you keep the requirements-traceability discipline from becoming bureaucratic enough that engineers stop proposing legitimate mitigations?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where an engineer on your team is technically excellent but has become the de facto gatekeeper for a subsystem — every change routes through them, they review everything, and the team has quietly stopped proposing improvements to that area because "it's not worth the friction"?
**Answer:** The quiet part is the real problem: the team has stopped proposing improvements, which means I'm losing the collective judgment of everyone except one person, and I may not find out until something breaks that nobody flagged. I'd start by understanding how this happened — often it's not ego but a genuine history of being the person who caught the subtle bugs, which earns them the gatekeeper role informally. I'd want to preserve what's valuable about that expertise while breaking the bottleneck. Concretely: make the review path explicit and shared rather than implicit and personal, so changes to that subsystem have a defined review process that doesn't depend on one person's availability or mood. Pair the gatekeeper with one or two other engineers on reviews so knowledge spreads and the friction of proposing a change drops. Talk to the gatekeeper directly and frame it as a scaling problem, not a criticism — "you're the only person who can review this, and that's a risk to the project and to you" — and ask them to help design the path to distributing that expertise. I'd also want to hear from the engineers who stopped proposing changes, because their reasons will tell me whether this is about review friction, interpersonal dynamics, or something else. The measure of success is that the subsystem still gets rigorous review but the review no longer depends on a single person, and the team feels safe proposing changes again.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the gatekeeper experiences the redistribution of review responsibility as a loss of status or trust? How would you detect this pattern early, before the team has already gone quiet?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether a technical problem the team is stuck on warrants bringing in outside expertise — a consultant, a specialist from another group, or a vendor's field application engineer — versus continuing to work it internally?
**Answer:** I'd frame it as a cost-of-delay versus cost-of-humility trade. The first question is whether the team is actually stuck or just slow: if there's a clear hypothesis and a plan to test it, more internal time is usually the right investment, because the team learns and the knowledge stays. If the team has cycled through the same few hypotheses without progress, or the problem sits in a domain where nobody on the team has real depth, that's a signal that internal effort is unlikely to converge. I'd also weigh the nature of the problem — a vendor FAE is often the fastest path for a question about a specific part's behavior or errata, a specialist from another group is right for a domain the team lacks, and a consultant makes sense when the problem is broad enough that you need sustained outside perspective. Against that I'd weigh the real costs: onboarding time, the risk of an outside answer that doesn't fit the team's constraints, and the knowledge that walks out the door when the engagement ends. So I'd want any outside help structured to transfer knowledge, not just deliver an answer — ask them to work alongside the team, document their reasoning, and leave the team able to handle the next occurrence. I'd also set a checkpoint: define in advance what "the team is making progress" looks like, so the decision to bring in help is based on evidence rather than on how frustrated everyone feels on a given day.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation where the team resents bringing in outside help, seeing it as a vote of no confidence? What would you do if the outside expert's recommendation conflicts with the team's own analysis?

## Q4: How would you approach building a technical leadership roadmap for a team that is transitioning from a single-product medical device focus to a multi-project portfolio, where engineers are used to deep specialization rather than cross-project flexibility?
**Answer:** The core tension is that deep specialization is exactly what makes a medical device team good — you want people who know the analog front end or the regulatory file cold — but a multi-project portfolio punishes single points of expertise. So the roadmap has to preserve depth while reducing the number of places where one person is the only one who knows something. I'd start by mapping the current expertise: who owns what, where the single points of failure are, and which skills are genuinely transferable across projects versus product-specific. Then I'd build the roadmap around a few deliberate moves. First, define a shared core of practices — design review standards, risk management process, documentation conventions — that every project uses, so engineers moving between projects aren't relearning the process each time. Second, create structured rotation or pairing so that a specialist on one project mentors someone on another, spreading depth without diluting it. Third, be honest about capacity: a multi-project portfolio with a fixed headcount means some projects get less senior attention, and the roadmap should say which ones and why. I'd also set expectations with leadership about the transition cost — the first portfolio cycle will be slower than steady state, and pretending otherwise sets the team up to be judged against an unrealistic baseline. The roadmap itself should be a living document with milestones the team can see progress against, because a transition this large needs visible momentum to keep morale up.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle an engineer who is deeply specialized and genuinely doesn't want to broaden — is that a problem to solve or a preference to accommodate? What would you do if leadership expects the portfolio transition to happen without any slowdown in delivery?

## Q5: How would you approach a situation where you need to give critical feedback to a senior engineer — someone with more tenure and deeper domain expertise than you in a specific area — about how they're working with the rest of the team, and you expect them to receive it poorly?
**Answer:** I'd separate the two things that are easy to conflate: their technical expertise, which I respect and should say so, and their impact on the team, which is what needs to change. The feedback should be about observable behavior and its effect, not about their character or their competence — "when questions come up in review and get waved off as obvious, junior engineers have stopped asking, and we're losing their input" is a statement they can engage with; "you're dismissive" is one they'll defend against. I'd deliver it privately, in a setting where they don't feel ambushed, and I'd come prepared with specific examples rather than a general impression, because a senior engineer will reasonably push back on vague criticism. I'd also be genuinely open to their side — there may be context I'm missing, and a senior engineer who's seen a lot may have real reasons for how they operate. But I'd be clear about what needs to change and why it matters, and I'd ask them what would help them make that change, since someone with their experience often has good ideas about how to fix a team dynamic. I'd expect defensiveness and not treat it as failure; the first conversation is often just planting the seed. I'd follow up, watch for change, and be specific about what improvement looks like. And I'd be prepared for the possibility that the conversation doesn't land, in which case the issue becomes a performance-management question rather than a coaching one — but I'd want to have genuinely tried the coaching path first.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the senior engineer's response is that the junior engineers simply need to be tougher? What would you do if the behavior improves in your presence but continues when you're not in the room?
