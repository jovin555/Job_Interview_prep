# behavioral-leadership — Day 76

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter has done solid technical work, but the review package arrives late and is missing key calculations, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct to cancel or push through are both wrong here — cancelling burns the only slot before the gate, and pushing through without the missing calculations means the review can't actually evaluate the safety-critical or worst-case portions of the design. I'd split the review into two passes within the same meeting. First, spend the available time on what *can* be reviewed properly: architecture, interfaces, component selection rationale, and any sections where the calculations are present. Explicitly park the sections that depend on missing numbers, and capture them as named action items with an owner and a date — not as "presenter to follow up," which tends to evaporate. Second, I'd make the gap itself a visible output of the review: the minutes should state that the review is *conditional*, list exactly which calculations are outstanding, and specify who verifies them and how they'll be closed out. That way the milestone gate isn't silently bypassed — it's passed with a documented, tracked condition. I'd also ask the presenter, without blame, what caused the package to arrive late, because if it's a recurring pattern it's a process problem (review prep time not budgeted, or the presenter overloaded), not a personal failing. The tone matters: the goal is to protect the review's integrity as a safety mechanism, not to punish the presenter in front of peers.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the missing calculations later reveal a design problem, how do you handle the fact that the milestone gate was already passed?
- How would you change the review process so packages reliably arrive with enough lead time to be read properly?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures are the case where the pressure to "just fix it" is most dangerous, because a fix you can't verify against a reproduction is a guess, and in a medical device a guessed fix can mask the real cause while appearing to work. I'd start by resisting the framing that containment and root cause are the same thing. Containment — a conservative workaround, an added margin, a tightened screening test — can ship quickly if it genuinely reduces patient risk, but it must be labeled as containment and paired with a commitment to keep investigating. Then I'd attack reproducibility systematically rather than by intuition: characterize the failure population (which units, which environmental conditions, which firmware/hardware revision, which point in the operating cycle), instrument the system to capture state at the moment of failure (logging, triggers, watchdogs that snapshot registers), and try to widen the failure window deliberately — temperature cycling, voltage margining, timing stress, accelerated aging — to turn a rare event into a frequent one. I'd use a structured method like fishbone to enumerate candidate causes across hardware, firmware, environment, and use, then design experiments that can *falsify* each hypothesis rather than confirm a favorite. The key discipline is defining, up front, what evidence would prove the root cause — because without that, the team will declare victory the first time the symptom doesn't recur. Verification of effectiveness means running the fix against the stress conditions that provoked the failure, not just observing that it "seems fine now."

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you decide when containment is good enough to ship versus when you must hold the release?
- What do you do if, after extensive investigation, you still can't reproduce the failure but it keeps occurring in the field?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode on both sides is the same: the firmware team ends up treating the hardware as a black box and either over-trusts the numbers or applies a software fix that fights the physics. My approach is to hand over a *characterization*, not a spec sheet. That means giving the firmware team the actual behavior of the signal chain: the effective noise floor in LSBs at the relevant sample rates, the frequency content of that noise (so they know whether averaging helps or whether it's low-frequency drift that averaging won't touch), the nonlinearity and offset behavior near the rails, and the settling time after a channel switch or a power-mode transition. I'd express these as concrete, testable statements the firmware can design against — "at this sample rate you have roughly this much usable resolution, and here's the filter bandwidth that gets you there" — rather than "the ADC is 12-bit." I'd also be explicit about what the hardware *cannot* fix, so the firmware team doesn't spend weeks trying to filter out a problem that's really a layout or reference issue. Where possible I'd back it with a shared measurement: a capture both teams look at together, so the conversation is about the same data instead of competing mental models. The goal is to give them enough to make good trade-offs (sample rate vs. resolution vs. latency vs. power) without turning the handoff into a lecture on analog design.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you handle it when the firmware team's proposed filtering approach would add latency that violates a system timing requirement?
- What if the firmware team insists the noise is a hardware problem and the hardware team insists it's a software problem — how do you break that deadlock?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This is usually not a confidence problem so much as a habit formed by never having been given the whole decision. The engineer has learned that deferring is safe, and that pattern won't break just by encouragement. I'd start by handing over genuine ownership of a bounded decision — something with real trade-offs but a contained blast radius — and then deliberately *not* answering when they look to me for the call. Instead I'd ask them to lay out the options, the criteria, and their recommendation, and I'd commit to backing their choice if the reasoning holds up. The critical part is that they have to feel the weight of the decision and live with its consequences, including the parts that turn out to be wrong. I'd pair that with making the reasoning visible: after a decision, walk back through why it was made, what was assumed, and what would change the answer — so they build a reusable framework rather than just accumulating answers. In group settings I'd actively redirect "what do you think?" questions away from the most senior person and toward them, which also signals to the rest of the team that their judgment is expected to carry weight. Over time the goal is that they stop asking permission and start bringing decisions to review, which is a different and much healthier relationship.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you handle it if they make a decision you disagree with, but the reasoning was sound?
- How do you tell the difference between healthy deference and a genuine lack of technical judgment?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a disagreement turns personal, more technical argument usually makes it worse, because each side reads new evidence as ammunition rather than information. The first move is to separate the *decision* from the *people*. I'd name the dynamic explicitly but without accusing anyone — something like "we've each got a real concern here, and I want to make sure we're deciding on criteria rather than on who argued hardest." Then I'd try to get the team to agree on the criteria *before* re-litigating the options: what does this system actually need to do, what are the hard constraints (regulatory, power, cost, schedule, manufacturability, serviceability), and how do we weight them? Often the two camps are optimizing for different criteria and talking past each other. Where the disagreement is genuinely empirical — performance, power, thermal behavior, bring-up risk — I'd propose a bounded prototype or benchmark to settle it with data rather than opinion, and I'd define in advance what result would favor which option, so the test can't be spun afterward. If it's a values or risk-tolerance disagreement rather than a factual one, that's a decision for the accountable owner to make and document, with the dissenting rationale recorded so it isn't lost. Throughout, I'd keep the focus on the decision being reversible or not — if it's cheap to change later, we should decide fast and move; if it's expensive, it's worth the extra time. And I'd make sure both sides leave feeling heard, because a decision that's technically right but leaves half the team disengaged will cost more downstream than the architecture choice itself.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What do you do if the prototype results are ambiguous and don't clearly favor either option?
- How do you document the decision so that the losing side's concerns are preserved for future reference?
