# behavioral-leadership — Day 127

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design review where the presenter is technically strong, the package is thorough, but the review has drifted into a rubber-stamp — reviewers are nodding along and the risky decisions aren't being probed at all?
**Answer:** A review that produces no friction is usually not a review — it's an approval. The first move is to diagnose why the room has gone quiet. Often it's a social dynamic: the presenter is respected, the package is long, and reviewers assume someone else has already scrutinized the hard parts. The lead's job is to break that diffusion of responsibility deliberately. I'd restructure the session around the decisions that carry the most risk rather than walking the document front to back — pull the two or three choices that, if wrong, would be expensive or unsafe, and put each one up explicitly with the question "what would have to be true for this to be the wrong call?" Assigning specific reviewers to specific risk areas ahead of time, rather than leaving it open, converts passive attendance into ownership. I'd also model the behavior I want: ask a genuinely probing question myself, phrased as curiosity rather than challenge, so that critique becomes normal rather than personal. Finally, I'd capture the outcome as explicit decisions with rationale and dissent noted, so the review produces a record rather than a silent consensus. The measure of a good review isn't that it ran smoothly — it's that the risky parts got real scrutiny.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the presenter took your probing questions as a signal that you didn't trust their work? What would you do if, after restructuring, reviewers still deferred — would you change the format or the people?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is consistently high quality, but they have a habit of quietly redefining the scope of their own tasks — delivering something different from what was agreed — and the drift only surfaces when it disrupts someone downstream?
**Answer:** The instinct is to treat this as a discipline problem, but it's usually a communication and definition problem wearing a discipline costume. Someone who quietly redefines scope is often solving what they believe is the real problem, and the fact that it only surfaces downstream means the interfaces between tasks aren't explicit enough to catch drift early. I'd start by making the agreement itself more concrete — a clear statement of what "done" means for the task, including the interfaces and assumptions it depends on, so that a redefinition is visible as a change rather than absorbed silently. Then I'd have a direct conversation framed around impact rather than intent: describe the downstream disruption concretely, ask what they saw that prompted the change, and make clear that redefining scope isn't forbidden — it just has to be surfaced before it's acted on, because someone else is building against the original contract. If the underlying cause is that they see genuine problems with the agreed scope, that's valuable signal and I want it early, not after the fact. The goal is to convert a silent drift into an early conversation, which usually means making it safe and cheap to raise the change rather than making the rule stricter.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the redefinitions were actually improvements, just delivered without coordination? How would you distinguish this from a team member who is simply not clear on priorities?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — something outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team with more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?
**Answer:** The accountability doesn't change with the choice, so the question is really about which arrangement produces the best outcome and the best use of the team. I'd separate two things that get conflated: who does the work, and who owns the outcome. I can own the outcome while someone else does the work — that's the normal shape of technical leadership. The deciding factors are risk and growth. If the assignment is genuinely outside my depth and someone on the team has real expertise, taking it myself is often ego dressed up as diligence — I'd be slower, more likely to miss something, and I'd be denying a growth opportunity to the person who's actually equipped. On the other hand, if the assignment is a stretch for me specifically because it's a domain I need to build credibility in, there's a case for doing it with support — but I'd want to be honest about whether that's a real development need or just discomfort with delegating something visible. My default would be to delegate to the person with the expertise, stay close as a reviewer and sounding board, and make the accountability structure explicit so the visibility doesn't become pressure without support. If I do take it, I'd pair with someone who has the depth so I'm not learning in isolation on something high-stakes.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the person you delegated to was less senior and the stakeholders kept routing questions to you anyway? What would make you decide the stretch assignment was genuinely yours to take?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a technical decision when a proposed design change would improve performance but requires re-qualifying a component already approved in the design history file, and the schedule has no slack?
**Answer:** The trap here is framing it as performance versus schedule, when the real question is whether the improvement is worth the requalification cost — and that's a decision that should be made on evidence, not on who argues hardest. I'd start by making the trade-off explicit and quantified as far as possible: what performance gain does the change actually deliver, against what the requalification requires in time, testing, and regulatory interaction. Often the "improvement" is real but marginal, and once it's on paper next to the requalification cost the decision becomes obvious. If the gain is significant, then the honest conversation is about schedule, not about whether to do it — because "no slack" doesn't mean "no change," it means someone has to decide what gives. That's a decision for the people who own the schedule and the risk, not something to absorb quietly within the team. I'd also look for a middle path: can the change be staged so the current approved component ships and the improvement lands in a later revision, with the requalification work started now? That preserves the schedule without discarding the improvement. Whatever the outcome, I'd document the decision and its rationale in the DHF, because a decision to *not* change is as much a design decision as one to change, and an auditor will want to see the reasoning.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the performance improvement was driven by a customer commitment rather than an internal preference? What would you do if the requalification revealed the new component was actually worse in some other respect?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a gut feeling rather than a fully articulated argument?
**Answer:** The hardest part of this is that "gut feeling" concerns are usually pattern recognition that hasn't been verbalized yet, and teams tend to discount them because they can't be defended in a review. A culture that only accepts fully-articulated objections loses exactly the early signal that's most valuable. I'd work on three fronts. First, make it structurally cheap to raise a concern: a standing invitation at the start of reviews for "anything that feels off, even if you can't yet say why," and a norm that the person raising it isn't obligated to have the answer — the group's job is to help articulate it. Second, protect the messenger in practice, not just in principle: when someone raises an early concern, the response has to be visibly appreciative even if the concern turns out to be unfounded, because the team is watching how that lands far more than they're listening to what's said about psychological safety. Third, close the loop — when an early concern turns out to be right, say so publicly, because that's what teaches the team that speaking up has value. The seniority dimension matters too: if a concern challenges a decision from a senior person, the senior person's reaction in that moment sets the norm for everyone. If they engage with it genuinely rather than defending, the culture follows. If they don't, no amount of process will fix it.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a senior engineer consistently dismissed early concerns in a way that shut down the room, despite the stated norm? How would you distinguish a genuine gut-feeling signal from noise without discouraging people from raising either?
