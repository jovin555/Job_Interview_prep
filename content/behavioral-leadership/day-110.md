# behavioral-leadership — Day 110

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design review where the presenter is technically strong, but the review package arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** I'd separate the two problems — the schedule constraint and the missing justification — and handle them in that order, because conflating them tends to produce a review that either rubber-stamps the decision or gets cancelled outright. First, before the meeting, I'd contact the presenter directly and ask for the missing calculations as a short pre-read, even if it's a rough spreadsheet or a hand sketch with the numbers filled in. The goal is to get the reasoning in front of reviewers in some form, not to insist on a polished package. If the numbers genuinely don't exist yet, that itself is the most important finding of the review, and the meeting should be reframed around it rather than pretending the decision is ready to approve.

In the meeting, I'd be explicit about what the review can and cannot conclude. A useful framing is: "We can review the architecture and the approach today; we cannot approve the safety-critical margin until the calculations are on the table. Here's what we need, from whom, and by when." That keeps the milestone gate honest — the review happened, the team got real feedback, and the open item is tracked with an owner and a date rather than buried. I'd record the decision status as conditional or pending rather than approved, and make sure that status is visible to whoever owns the gate.

I'd also treat the late package as a process signal worth a brief, non-blaming conversation afterward. Was the presenter overloaded, unclear on what the package needed to contain, or unaware that the calculations were considered mandatory? Each of those has a different fix — workload rebalancing, a package template with a required-contents checklist, or simply restating the expectation. The point is to make the next review easier to prepare well, not to make this one an example.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the milestone gate owner pressures you to mark the review as approved so the schedule holds, how do you respond? How would you decide whether the missing calculation is a genuine gap or just undocumented reasoning the presenter holds in their head?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** The first move is to resist the pressure to jump to a fix, because an intermittent failure is exactly the case where a plausible-sounding fix is most likely to be wrong — you can't verify it if you can't reproduce the failure. I'd start by reframing the goal for the team: the immediate objective is not "fix it" but "make it observable." That means instrumenting the system to capture state at the moment of failure — logging, counters, watchdog breadcrumbs, bus captures, whatever the platform supports — so that the next occurrence produces evidence rather than just a symptom report. I'd also widen the capture net: gather field or test data on when it happens (load conditions, temperature, timing, sequence of operations) and look for patterns before assuming a mechanism.

Structurally, I'd run it as a proper investigation rather than an ad hoc hunt. That means a clear problem statement, a containment action if there's any safety or customer impact (which is separate from a corrective action and should be labeled as such), and a structured hypothesis-generation step — a fishbone or fault-tree exercise across hardware, firmware, power, timing, and environment — so the team isn't just chasing the first theory. Each hypothesis gets a cheap discriminating test where possible. I'd keep a visible log of hypotheses tested and ruled out, because with intermittent issues the team's memory of what's already been eliminated is often the weakest link.

On the pressure to ship: I'd be honest that a fix shipped without a verified mechanism is a guess, and propose a two-track plan — a containment or mitigation that reduces exposure now, plus the investigation running in parallel with a defined evidence bar for calling it resolved. Verification of effectiveness matters here too: once a candidate fix exists, define in advance what evidence would convince you the failure rate actually dropped, rather than declaring victory after a quiet week.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide when you've gathered enough evidence to stop investigating and commit to a fix? What do you do if the failure stops occurring before you've found the mechanism — do you close it?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The key is to translate the constraint into the firmware team's own vocabulary — what they can observe, decide, and control — rather than handing them a schematic and a physics lecture, or a one-line "the analog is noisy, deal with it." I'd start by characterizing the constraint quantitatively in terms the firmware can use: what's the effective noise floor in LSBs at the sample rate they'll actually run, what's the usable dynamic range, what's the worst-case offset and drift, and how those numbers change with temperature or supply variation. That gives them something to design against — filter cutoffs, averaging windows, threshold margins, calibration routines — instead of a vague warning.

Then I'd be explicit about the boundary of responsibility. Some of the problem is theirs to solve in firmware (filtering, oversampling, calibration at startup, rejecting samples during known-noisy transitions like a motor ramp or a wake-up event); some of it is a hardware limitation they cannot fix and shouldn't try to paper over (if the analog front end can't resolve the signal at the required rate, no amount of firmware cleverness creates information that isn't there). Drawing that line clearly prevents the firmware team from burning weeks on a software fix for a hardware problem, and prevents hardware from assuming firmware will magically clean up a marginal design.

Practically, I'd deliver this as a short written interface note — a few pages with the numbers, the assumptions, and the specific behaviors firmware should and shouldn't rely on — plus a working session where we look at real captured data together. Real traces are worth more than any spec table, because they let the firmware engineers see the noise, the transients, and the timing for themselves and ask questions against something concrete. I'd also make sure the note states what hardware will guarantee versus what's typical, since firmware built against "typical" numbers fails in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team proposes a filtering approach that would mask a real signal feature the clinical use case depends on? What would you do if the hardware constraint turns out to be worse than the interface note promised, after firmware has already been built around it?

## Q4: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?

**Answer:** The routing-around behavior is the urgent symptom, because once a team starts bypassing review, you lose the review's actual value — catching problems early — and you also lose the record of why decisions were made. So I'd address that first, but not by simply telling people to stop. I'd want to understand what's driving it: is the reviewer raising genuinely new and valid concerns that the process has no way to close, or is the review lacking a mechanism to ever declare something resolved? Those are different problems, and the fix differs.

My working assumption would be that the review process itself is the root cause — specifically, that it has no closure mechanism. A review where any participant can reopen any topic indefinitely isn't a review, it's an open-ended forum, and people route around it because it never terminates. So I'd introduce structure: a defined scope for each review, a tracked action-item list with owners and due dates, and an explicit rule that new concerns raised after a review's scope is closed go into the next review's agenda or a separate focused session rather than reopening the current one. Concerns raised in good faith get a home; they just don't get to indefinitely block progress.

Then I'd talk to the reviewer directly, privately, and without accusation. The framing I'd use is collaborative: "I've noticed we keep generating new concerns each session and the team is getting frustrated — help me understand what's not getting resolved for you." Sometimes the reviewer is raising the same underlying worry in different guises because it was never actually addressed, and naming it once and resolving it properly ends the pattern. Sometimes they're genuinely seeing things others miss, and the answer is to give their concerns a proper channel rather than suppress them. Occasionally the pattern is more about control or anxiety than technical content, and that's a conversation to have honestly and, if needed, with management support.

Finally, I'd make the bypass itself visible and costly in a constructive way: reinforce that decisions made outside review still need to be documented and reviewed, so routing around the process doesn't actually save time — it just defers the review to a worse moment. The aim is to make the legitimate path the fastest path.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the reviewer's concerns turn out to be consistently valid, and the real problem is that the team has been too eager to move on? How do you handle it if the reviewer is more senior than you and resists the structural changes?

## Q5: How would you approach building a risk management culture on a medical device team that currently treats ISO 14971 documentation as a checkbox exercise done after the design is complete?

**Answer:** The core problem with after-the-fact risk documentation is that it can only ever describe risks the team already happened to avoid — it can't influence the design, which is the entire point of risk management. So the cultural shift I'd aim for is moving risk thinking from a documentation task to a design input: risks identified while there's still time to change the design in response. I'd start by making that case concretely rather than as a compliance lecture, because "ISO says so" persuades nobody. A good framing is to walk through a real design decision the team already made and show what a hazard analysis would have surfaced at the time — not to criticize the decision, but to demonstrate that the analysis would have been useful, not bureaucratic.

Structurally, I'd embed risk work into the design process at natural checkpoints rather than as a separate phase. Early in a project, a hazard identification session with hardware, firmware, and clinical/quality input, producing an initial hazard list that feeds into design requirements. At design reviews, risk items on the agenda alongside technical ones — "what failure modes does this introduce, and how are they mitigated?" During verification, evidence that mitigations actually work. The documentation then becomes a byproduct of work the team was doing anyway, which is the only sustainable way to keep it current. I'd also make the risk file a living document the team actually opens, not a PDF assembled at the end.

I'd pay attention to incentives, because checkbox culture usually reflects them. If risk documentation is only ever examined by auditors and never by engineers, engineers will treat it as someone else's problem. So I'd have engineers own risk items in their areas, review the risk file in design reviews, and credit good hazard identification visibly. I'd also model the behavior myself — raising risks in my own reviews, including ones I'm not sure about, and treating "I'm not certain, but this worries me" as a valuable contribution rather than noise. Culture changes through what gets rewarded and what gets modeled far more than through what gets mandated.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality team is comfortable with the checkbox approach and sees your changes as creating extra work? How do you keep risk management from becoming a large up-front exercise that the team then ignores for the rest of the project?
