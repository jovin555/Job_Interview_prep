# behavioral-leadership — Day 121

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but has a habit of answering every question with a justification of why their original choice was correct, rather than actually engaging with the concern being raised?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the behavior from the person, because a strong engineer who defends rather than engages is usually not being arrogant — they're being asked to defend a decision they made carefully, and the review format itself is putting them in an adversarial posture. So I'd change the format before I change the person. Instead of "here's my design, what's wrong with it," I'd reframe the review around specific decision points: for each significant choice, state the alternatives considered, the criteria used, and what evidence would change the decision. That last part is the key move — asking the presenter to pre-commit to "here's what would make me change my mind" turns the review from a defense of the choice into a test of the reasoning, and it gives the presenter a face-saving path to update their position because they've already defined the conditions under which updating is correct rather than a concession.

In the room, when a concern gets deflected with a restatement of the original rationale, I'd redirect explicitly: "I hear that the original choice was made for reason X — the question on the table is whether concern Y changes the calculus. Can we sit with Y for a minute?" That names the deflection without accusing anyone of it. I'd also model the behavior myself by being the first to say "that's a good point, I hadn't weighted that" when a reviewer catches something in my own work — it signals that changing your mind in a review is normal and not a status loss.

If the pattern persists across multiple reviews, that's a private conversation, not a public one. I'd describe the specific behavior and its cost — "when a concern gets answered with a restatement of the original rationale, reviewers stop raising concerns, and then we lose the value of the review" — and ask whether something about the review setup is making them feel ambushed. Sometimes the root cause is that the engineer has been burned before by reviews that turned into pile-ons, and the defensiveness is armor. Fixing the review culture fixes the behavior.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between healthy defense of a well-reasoned decision and the defensive pattern you're describing? What would you do if the presenter's manager was in the room and implicitly endorsed the deflecting behavior?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** My default is that escalation is a tool, not a failure — but it's the wrong tool for most disagreements, and reaching for it too early trains the team to route every conflict upward instead of building the skill of resolving it. So the first question I ask is: is this a disagreement about facts, about values/priorities, or about authority? Facts disagreements should almost never be escalated — they should be resolved by getting better data, running a test, or building a prototype. If two senior engineers disagree about whether a design will meet a thermal spec, the answer is a thermal measurement, not a management decision. Escalating a factual dispute is an admission that we'd rather have someone's title settle it than spend a day getting the answer.

Values and priorities disagreements are different, because they often can't be resolved with data — one engineer weights manufacturability higher, the other weights performance, and both are right about their own weighting. Those are legitimately mine to resolve as the technical lead, or to take to my manager if the trade-off has product or regulatory implications beyond my authority. And authority disagreements — where the real question is "who gets to decide" — usually need escalation because they're about role clarity, not engineering.

The other factor is time and reversibility. If the decision is cheap to reverse and we have time, I'd rather let the team try one approach and learn than spend a week in mediation. If it's expensive to reverse or blocks a milestone, I need a decision faster, and escalation may be the efficient path even if it's not the ideal one. Before escalating, though, I'd always do one thing: make sure both engineers can state the other's position to the other's satisfaction. If they can't, the disagreement isn't ripe for escalation — it's ripe for a conversation where each person actually hears the other, and that conversation is mine to facilitate first.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if you escalated and your manager's decision went against the engineer you thought was right? How do you keep an escalation from feeling like a loss to the engineer whose position didn't prevail?

## Q3: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — including concerns that are only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument — on a team that has learned from experience that speaking up early gets the messenger blamed or handed extra work?

**Answer:** The critical insight is that this is a rational response to a real incentive, not a culture problem to be fixed with exhortation. If raising a concern early historically meant getting assigned the investigation, or being seen as the person who slows things down, then the team is behaving correctly given the incentives — and no amount of "we have an open culture" messaging will override what people have actually experienced. So I'd focus on changing the consequences, not the messaging.

Concretely, three things. First, separate raising a concern from owning the resolution. When someone flags a risk, the default response should be "thank you, let's log it and decide who investigates" — not "great, you found it, you fix it." The person who raises a concern should get credit for surfacing it, and the work of investigating should be allocated deliberately, sometimes to someone else. Second, make the "gut feeling" channel legitimate and cheap. A structured way to log an unarticulated concern — a risk register entry that just says "something about the power sequencing during wake-up bothers me, not sure why yet" — costs the raiser almost nothing and gives the team a place to triage it later. The point is to lower the activation energy for speaking up before you have a full argument, because most real problems are first noticed as unease, and requiring a fully-formed case before anyone speaks filters out exactly the early warnings you most want. Third, when a concern turns out to be a false alarm, respond in a way that makes raising it again safe — "good that we checked, that's the system working" — because the fear of being wrong is a big part of what silences people.

I'd also watch for the moment where the culture is actually tested: when a concern is raised about a decision made by a senior person or by team consensus. That's the case where the old lesson — "speaking up gets you blamed" — is strongest, and it's the case where my response is being watched by everyone. Handling one of those well, visibly, does more than any amount of stated policy.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a "gut feeling" concern turned out to be correct but the person who raised it had been dismissed at the time? How do you keep the low-cost concern channel from becoming noise that the team learns to ignore?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than look at systemic issues?

**Answer:** The first move is to set the frame before anyone speaks, because a post-mortem's tone is set in its first five minutes and is very hard to change afterward. I'd open by stating explicitly what the post-mortem is for and what it isn't: we're here to understand the system that produced the outcome, not to identify a person to blame, and the test of a good post-mortem is whether we come out with changes to how we work, not with a name. I'd also say plainly that individual mistakes are data about the system — if one person's error could sink the project, that's a system with no margin, and the interesting question is why there was no margin.

Then I'd structure it to make systemic analysis the path of least resistance. A timeline reconstruction first — what actually happened, in order, without judgment — because blame narratives usually collapse when people see the full sequence and realize how many small decisions interacted. Then, for each major slip, a structured "why" chain that's required to go at least three levels deep and is not allowed to terminate at "human error." "The engineer missed the deadline" is not a root cause; "the estimate had no buffer because the schedule was built from a best-case plan, and there was no early-warning mechanism to flag the slip until it was unrecoverable" is closer. I'd use something like a fishbone to make sure we're looking at process, tooling, staffing, requirements, and communication, not just individual execution.

On morale: low morale after a miss often comes from people feeling the outcome was inevitable and invisible — that they saw it coming and couldn't get anyone to act. So part of the post-mortem's job is to surface those moments and take them seriously, because "we raised it and it wasn't heard" is both a systemic finding and, handled well, a morale repair. I'd also be careful to separate the post-mortem from any performance consequences. If people believe the post-mortem feeds into blame, they'll give me a sanitized story and I'll learn nothing. The post-mortem has to be genuinely consequence-free for individuals to be worth anything.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a senior stakeholder wanted the post-mortem to name individuals? What would you do if the systemic findings pointed at decisions made by people more senior than you?

## Q5: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is technically competent but they consistently optimize for the wrong thing — for example, chasing measurement precision in an area where robustness and manufacturability matter far more — and they don't seem to recognize the mismatch?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd check is whether this is a values mismatch or an information mismatch, because the fix is different. If the engineer genuinely believes precision is the priority, that's a values conversation about what the product needs. But more often, the engineer is optimizing for what they can measure and what they were trained to value — precision is visible, testable, and satisfying, while manufacturability is diffuse and shows up months later as yield loss on someone else's line. So the mismatch is often that the engineer has never been given visibility into the consequences of the trade-off they're implicitly making.

The most effective intervention I know is to put them in contact with the consequence. If precision is being chased at the cost of manufacturability, get them into a design-for-manufacturing review with the people who build the thing, or into a yield data review, or a conversation with the contract manufacturer about what actually causes assembly problems. Engineers are usually responsive to evidence once they see the downstream effect directly — the problem is that the feedback loop is long and they've never been in the room when it closes. That's a much better lever than me asserting "robustness matters more," because it makes the priority real rather than a management preference.

I'd also make the priority explicit in the requirements and review criteria, because "optimize for robustness and manufacturability" is not actionable unless it's operationalized. What does good look like? What's the precision target, and what's the robustness target, and how do we know when we've hit each? If the requirements don't say, the engineer will default to the thing they know how to optimize, and that's a requirements failure as much as an individual one. Finally, I'd be direct with the engineer about the pattern — not as criticism of their work, which is competent, but as a calibration conversation: "your work is good; the thing I want to shift is what you're optimizing for, and here's why." Naming it explicitly is fairer than hoping they infer it from review comments.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer pushed back and argued that precision genuinely was the right priority? What would you do if the pattern was costing real schedule time and you needed it to change faster than a culture shift would allow?
