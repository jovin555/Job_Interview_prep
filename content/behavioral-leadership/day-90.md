# behavioral-leadership — Day 90

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** I would treat this as a decision about what the review can legitimately accomplish, not as a scheduling problem. The first move is to separate the review into two parts: what can be evaluated now, and what cannot be evaluated without the missing calculations. A safety-critical justification that isn't in the package isn't reviewable — reviewers can't verify a number they can't see, and approving on the strength of the presenter's verbal assurance defeats the purpose of the gate. So I'd open the meeting by stating that explicitly and framing it as protecting the presenter, not attacking them: the review's job is to catch problems before the gate, and it can't do that on trust.

Then I'd run the review on everything that is reviewable — architecture, interfaces, component selection, layout constraints, test coverage — and capture the safety-critical calculation as a formal, blocking action item with an owner and a date. If the milestone gate genuinely cannot slip, the honest path is to record the gate as conditionally passed with the calculation as a documented open item, escalate that condition to whoever owns the gate, and make sure the risk is visible rather than absorbed silently. What I would not do is let the meeting end with a vague "send it around later," because that's how undocumented safety decisions accumulate.

The follow-up is making sure this doesn't recur: a review-readiness checklist that the presenter confirms before the slot is booked, and a norm that packages go out with enough lead time for reviewers to actually read them. Late packages are usually a symptom of an overloaded engineer, not indifference, so I'd also want to understand what's consuming their time.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the presenter pushes back and says the calculation exists but just wasn't written up, how do you handle that in the room? What would you do if the milestone owner overrules you and passes the gate anyway?

## Q2: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is the same: the firmware team ends up making design choices based on a mental model that doesn't match the hardware. If I oversimplify to "the ADC is noisy," they'll add filtering that may not address the actual noise character. If I hand over a full noise analysis, they'll disengage. What I aim for is a small number of actionable statements, each tied to a number and a condition.

Concretely, I'd characterize the constraint in the terms the firmware will actually operate in: effective resolution at the sample rate they intend to use, the noise floor and its spectral character (is it broadband, or is there a switching component at a frequency they'll alias?), the input range and where saturation occurs, and any timing constraints like settling time after a multiplexer channel switch. Then I'd state the implication: "at this sample rate you have roughly this many effective bits, so a single-sample threshold comparison will be unreliable — you need averaging over N samples or a digital filter with this cutoff." That gives them a design decision they own, with the reasoning visible.

I'd also offer to co-own the verification: a bench test where firmware toggles its filter settings while hardware captures the analog signal, so both teams see the same data. That converts an abstract constraint into a shared measurement, which is far more durable than a document. And I'd write the characterization down in a short interface note that lives with the design, so it survives past the current conversation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filter would add latency that violates a clinical timing requirement? What do you do when the hardware characterization itself is uncertain because the noise depends on operating conditions you can't fully replicate on the bench?

## Q3: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** Deference in a strong executor usually isn't a confidence problem — it's that they've never been given a decision with real consequences attached, so they've never had to build the judgment that comes from owning one. The mentoring approach should therefore be structured around progressively handing over ownership, not around encouragement.

I'd start by giving them a decision with a bounded blast radius and a clear definition of done: own the component selection for a subsystem, including the trade-off analysis and the recommendation, and present it at the design review yourself rather than deferring to me. The key is that I commit in advance to not overriding them in the room unless there's a safety or compliance issue — and I say that out loud so they know the decision is genuinely theirs. If I undercut them the first time they present, the lesson they learn is that deference was correct.

I'd also make the reasoning visible after the fact: ask them to write a short decision record with the alternatives considered and why they chose what they chose. That does two things — it forces the trade-off thinking to be explicit, and it gives me a concrete artifact to coach against. Over time I'd increase the scope and the ambiguity, moving from "pick between these three parts" to "here's a vague requirement, define the options yourself." And I'd deliberately route questions back to them in group settings — when someone asks me something in their area, I'd say "that's their call, let's hear it" — which quietly establishes their authority with the rest of the team.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if they make a decision you believe is wrong but isn't unsafe — do you let it play out? How do you tell the difference between deference that's a development gap and deference that's a reasonable read of their own inexperience?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** The pressure to ship a fix is exactly what makes intermittent failures dangerous — the fastest-looking fix is usually a guess that suppresses the symptom, and if it appears to work, you've now made the real cause harder to find because the failure signature has changed. So the first thing I'd do is buy the investigation enough room to be honest: separate containment from correction. Containment is legitimate under time pressure — detect the failure in the field, fail safe, log it, notify — and it can ship quickly. Correction requires understanding, and I'd be explicit with stakeholders that those are two different timelines.

For the investigation itself, the core problem is that you can't study what you can't catch. So the priority is instrumentation: add logging or a capture mechanism that records the state leading up to the failure, ideally with enough context (timestamps, sensor values, task timing, supply voltage) to distinguish hypotheses. If the failure is rare, I'd try to accelerate it — run at temperature extremes, vary supply voltage, stress the timing, run many units in parallel — because a failure you can trigger on demand becomes a normal debugging problem. I'd also build a fault tree early and use it to decide what to instrument, rather than instrumenting everything and drowning in data.

Throughout, I'd keep a written log of hypotheses tested and ruled out, because intermittent investigations have a way of circling back to ideas already eliminated. And I'd define in advance what evidence would count as confirming the root cause, so the team doesn't stop at the first correlation that looks plausible.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide when containment has bought enough time and you need to escalate that the root cause is still unknown? What would you do if the failure rate is so low that even aggressive stress testing won't produce enough events to be statistically meaningful?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** The default should be to resolve it within the team, because escalating every disagreement trains the team to route conflicts upward and erodes the sense that they can settle technical questions themselves. But "resolve it within the team" doesn't mean "keep talking until someone gives in" — it means running a decision process with a defined endpoint.

My test for escalation has a few triggers. First, does the disagreement actually require authority I don't have — a budget commitment, a schedule change, a cross-team dependency, or a risk acceptance that belongs above my level? Second, is the team deadlocked after a genuine attempt to resolve it on evidence, such that further discussion is just repetition? Third, is there a compliance or safety dimension where the decision needs a documented owner outside the engineering team? And fourth, has the disagreement become personal in a way that's damaging the team, where an outside party may be better positioned to reset it? Any of those justifies escalation; none of them is "I'd rather not decide."

Before escalating, I'd make sure the disagreement is framed as a decision with options, trade-offs, and a recommendation — not as "A and B disagree, please fix." That framing is itself often enough to resolve it, because writing it down exposes where the disagreement is actually about unstated assumptions rather than the technical merits. And whatever the outcome, I'd document the decision and the rationale, including the dissenting view, so the team can revisit it later on evidence rather than relitigating it from memory.

**Possible follow-ups:** If you escalate and your manager decides against your own recommendation, how do you handle communicating that to the team? How would you handle it if one of the two engineers goes around you and escalates directly?

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you keep the dissenting engineer engaged after a decision goes against them? What would make you reopen a decision that was already documented and closed?
