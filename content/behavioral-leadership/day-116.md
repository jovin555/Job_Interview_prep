# behavioral-leadership — Day 116

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is under pressure to close out a design and one engineer keeps proposing a "just in case" addition — extra filtering, an extra protection component, a spare sensor channel — that isn't justified by the requirements, and the additions are quietly accumulating scope?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a requirements-traceability problem wearing the costume of a technical debate. The first move is to make the accumulation visible: list every proposed addition, what requirement or risk it traces to, and what it costs in board area, BOM, power budget, test coverage, and schedule. Often the "just in case" items have no traceable justification at all, and once that's on a page the conversation changes from opinion to evidence.

Then I'd separate the proposals into three buckets. Items that mitigate a real, documented risk — something that shows up in the risk analysis or a known failure mode — get evaluated on their merits and probably accepted. Items that are pure insurance against an unquantified worry get deferred, with the reasoning written down so it isn't lost. Items that duplicate existing protection get rejected outright, because redundant protection that isn't analyzed can actually degrade reliability — an extra filter stage can introduce its own failure mode or interact badly with the loop it's sitting in.

The cultural piece matters as much as the technical one. I'd want to validate the instinct — cautious engineers who flag risks are valuable, and I don't want to train that out of anyone — while being clear that "just in case" isn't a design rationale. The standard I'd set is: every addition needs a requirement, a risk, or a measured problem behind it, and if we can't articulate one, we write it down as a known unknown and move on. That gives the engineer a legitimate way to record the concern without blocking the design, and it gives the project a defensible record of what was considered and why it was deferred.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer's concern turned out to be legitimate later — the failure mode they worried about actually appeared in the field? And how do you keep this from becoming a bureaucratic gate that discourages anyone from raising anything?

## Q2: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically excellent but has structured the package so the riskiest decisions are buried in appendices, and reviewers are likely to approve without engaging with them?

**Answer:** The structural problem here is that the review's format is doing the presenter's arguing for them — the main body reads as settled, and the genuinely contestable decisions are in a place reviewers don't reliably go. I'd fix the format before the meeting rather than trying to fix it live, because once you're in the room with a large package and limited time, you've already lost the ability to redirect attention.

Concretely, I'd ask for a one-page decision summary up front: the three or four choices that carry the most risk or irreversibility, what alternatives were considered, and what evidence supports the choice. Anything that's a safety-critical calculation or a trade-off with real downside gets surfaced in the main body, not the appendix. Appendices are for supporting data, not for the argument itself. If the presenter pushes back that this is redundant work, the framing is that the summary is what the reviewers will actually use to make the approval decision — so it's the highest-leverage page in the package.

In the meeting itself, I'd run the review against the decision summary rather than page by page, and explicitly ask the room to engage with the riskiest item first while everyone's attention is fresh. I'd also make the approval conditional in a useful way: the record should show which decisions were reviewed and which were accepted on the presenter's assertion, so there's a distinction between "we examined this" and "we didn't." That protects the reviewers and the project, and it removes the incentive to bury things, because burying no longer produces a clean approval.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the presenter is resistant because they see the summary as an implicit criticism of their package? And how do you handle a reviewer who uses the summary to reopen every decision rather than focusing on the risky ones?

## Q3: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is consistently high quality, but they have a habit of quietly redefining the scope of their own tasks — delivering something different from what was agreed — and the drift only becomes visible when it disrupts someone downstream?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd want to understand is whether this is a communication failure or a judgment failure, because the response differs. Sometimes an engineer sees a genuine problem with the original scope and fixes it, but doesn't surface the change because they don't realize the change has downstream implications — that's a communication gap and it's fixable with process. Other times the engineer has decided, implicitly, that their judgment about what the task should be overrides the agreed scope — that's a different conversation about ownership and boundaries.

I'd start by making the drift visible without making it a confrontation: walk through the last few instances, what was agreed, what was delivered, and where the divergence surfaced downstream. Naming the pattern concretely is usually enough to get an honest answer about which of the two situations it is. If it's the communication gap, the fix is a lightweight one — a short note when scope changes, a heads-up to whoever's downstream, and an explicit norm that changing scope is fine but changing it silently isn't. If it's the judgment issue, the conversation is about the fact that scope is a shared contract, and unilateral redefinition — however well-intentioned — transfers risk onto people who didn't get a say.

I'd be careful not to over-correct into rigidity. An engineer who never deviates from a flawed plan is not what I want either. The message I'd want to land is: deviate when the evidence says you should, but surface it, and let the people downstream absorb the change deliberately rather than discovering it. I'd also want to check my own side of it — if the original scope was vague or the engineer had no way to raise a concern before committing, some of the drift is on the planning, not the person.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer's redefinitions were actually improvements, and the downstream disruption was the cost of a better outcome? And what would you do if the pattern continued after the conversation?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — something outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team with more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?

**Answer:** I'd start by being honest about what the assignment actually needs. If it's a genuinely novel technical problem where depth in that specific area is the binding constraint, then the person with the relevant expertise should do the work, and my job is to make sure the accountability structure supports them rather than substituting my judgment for theirs. Taking it myself to protect a high-visibility outcome is often the wrong instinct — it optimizes for my comfort and the project's optics, not for the project's actual chance of success or for the team's growth.

But there's a real counter-consideration: high-visibility work carries risk that a less-senior engineer may not be equipped to manage — stakeholder communication, schedule pressure, the political weight of the outcome. So the question isn't just "who's deepest technically," it's "who can carry both the technical and the non-technical load, and what support do they need." A reasonable structure is to delegate the technical work to the person with the expertise and take on the surrounding accountability myself — stakeholder management, risk communication, escalation — while staying close enough to the technical detail to catch problems early. That's not abdication and it's not doing it myself; it's splitting the role along the line where each of us adds the most.

I'd also want to be explicit with the person about why they're getting it, what I'll be watching, and what "good" looks like at the checkpoints. And I'd want a genuine off-ramp: if the work goes badly, the lesson should be about the support structure, not a permanent mark against them. The failure mode I'd most want to avoid is delegating and then hovering so closely that I've taken the work back without admitting it.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the person turned the assignment down because they found the visibility intimidating? And how would you handle it if, partway through, it became clear the technical depth wasn't there after all?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument?

**Answer:** The reason engineers stop raising concerns early is almost always that raising them has cost more than it returned — the messenger got blamed, or got handed the work, or got overruled in a way that felt dismissive. So the culture change has to start by making the cost of speaking up lower than the cost of staying quiet, and that has to be demonstrated, not announced.

Practically, I'd do a few things. First, I'd explicitly legitimize the half-formed concern — a "gut feeling" is often pattern recognition that hasn't been articulated yet, and the right response is to help the person turn it into a testable question rather than to demand they justify it on the spot. Second, I'd make it normal for senior people to invite challenge on their own decisions specifically, because the asymmetry is the hard part: a junior engineer challenging a principal is a different act from the reverse, and it only happens if the principal visibly wants it. Third, I'd separate the act of raising a concern from the act of owning the follow-up — if every flag becomes the flagger's new task, people learn quickly to keep quiet.

I'd also want a lightweight way to record concerns that don't get acted on, so "we heard you and decided otherwise, here's why" is a real response rather than silence. That matters because the worst outcome isn't a concern being overruled — it's a concern being ignored, which teaches the person that speaking up is pointless. And I'd model the behavior myself: when I'm wrong, say so plainly and credit whoever caught it, because one visible instance of a senior person being corrected without consequence does more than any amount of stated policy.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the culture change started producing a flood of low-quality concerns that consumed review time? And how do you distinguish a genuine gut-feeling signal from noise without discouraging the person who raised it?
