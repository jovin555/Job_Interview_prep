# behavioral-leadership — Day 112

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?

**Answer:** The routing-around behavior is the real signal here — it means the team has lost confidence that the review process can terminate, and that's a process failure, not a personality problem. I'd treat the two symptoms separately.

First, the stalling. A reviewer who raises a fresh objection every session is usually doing one of two things: reviewing incrementally because they never got a complete package to react to, or using the review as a design conversation rather than a gate. The fix for both is structural — require that objections be submitted in writing against a specific revision of the package before the meeting, with a stated severity (blocking vs. non-blocking). Anything raised for the first time in the room gets logged as a follow-up item rather than halting the session, unless it's genuinely safety-critical. That converts an open-ended objection stream into a bounded, trackable list, and it makes the reviewer's concerns visible and dispositionable rather than ambient.

Second, the routing-around. I wouldn't punish it — I'd name it openly as evidence the process isn't working, and bring the reviewer into fixing the process rather than being the problem the team works around. Often the serial objector has real technical concerns that were never being heard in a structured way; giving them a formal channel (a pre-review comment window, a written dissent that gets recorded) both respects the concern and removes the incentive to re-litigate live. If after that the pattern persists, it becomes a performance conversation about review behavior, not a technical one.

The thing I'd avoid is simply telling the reviewer to stop raising objections. That trades a slow review for a review that misses things, which in a regulated device context is a much worse outcome.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the serial objector's concerns turned out to be legitimate and the team had been approving flawed designs? What would you do if the reviewer was more senior than you and resisted any change to how they participate?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?

**Answer:** I'd structure the presentation in layers rather than trying to find one level of detail that suits everyone — because there isn't one. The top layer is the decision itself: what we're choosing between, what the options cost in schedule/risk/performance, and what I'm recommending. That layer has to be comprehensible to the product manager and defensible to the quality manager without any engineering background, and it should fit on one slide.

The second layer is the evidence that the recommendation is sound — the key numbers, the constraints that drove the shortlist, the failure modes considered. This is where the quality manager lives, because their question is "was this decided rigorously and is it traceable," not "is the analog front end well-designed." I'd make sure the risk-relevant content is explicit here rather than buried, since a quality reviewer who has to dig for the safety rationale will either disengage or distrust the package.

The third layer is the engineering detail — calculations, simulations, bench data — held in backup and referenced, not presented unless someone asks. Engineers in the room will want it, but presenting it up front loses the other two audiences and buries the decision.

The judgment call is what goes in layer two versus layer three. My rule: anything that changes the decision, or that a regulator would need to see to accept the decision, goes in the main body. Anything that only supports confidence in the analysis goes in backup. And I'd explicitly invite the quality manager to challenge the risk framing, because if they feel the safety case was glossed over, the meeting fails regardless of how well the engineering was presented.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the product manager pushed for a decision before the engineering evidence was complete? What would you do if the quality manager and the engineers disagreed about whether a risk was adequately addressed?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode on both sides is the same: the firmware team ends up either not knowing a constraint exists, or knowing it exists but not knowing what it means for their code. So the translation has to land as an actionable specification, not a description of the hardware.

I'd start from what the firmware actually controls and what it can't. For an ADC, the useful framing isn't "12-bit, 3.3V reference" — it's "the effective number of bits in this signal path is roughly X under these conditions, so the noise floor you'll see in the samples is Y, and any filtering or averaging you do operates on that floor." That tells them whether their resolution assumptions hold, whether a moving average buys them anything, and where the quantization limit sits. For a noisy analog front end, the useful framing is spectral and temporal: what the noise looks like, whether it's correlated with switching events, and what the sampling strategy can and can't reject.

I'd also be explicit about the boundary conditions — temperature range, supply variation, what happens during a wake-up transient — because firmware behavior often changes exactly at those boundaries, and a constraint stated only at nominal conditions is misleading.

The other half is closing the loop. I'd want the firmware team to feed back what they're assuming, so I can confirm or correct it against the hardware reality, and I'd want a shared measurement — a bench capture or a characterization run — that both teams look at, rather than each reasoning from a different mental model of the same circuit. That shared artifact prevents the "your hardware is noisy" / "your firmware is wrong" argument before it starts.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team's filtering approach was masking a hardware problem that should be fixed at the source? What would you do if the constraint couldn't be fully characterized before firmware development had to start?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** The deference is usually not a confidence problem — it's that they've never been given a decision with real consequences attached, so they've learned that the safe move is to align with the most senior person. Telling them to be more assertive doesn't change that; giving them ownership does.

I'd start by handing over a decision that's genuinely theirs, with a clear scope and a clear expectation that they'll bring a recommendation rather than a question. The key is that the decision has to be real — if I reserve the right to override it on a whim, they'll correctly conclude they don't own it. I'd set up the guardrails up front (constraints, what would make a decision unacceptable, when to escalate) and then stay out of the middle of it.

The harder part is the room. In design reviews, I'd deliberately direct questions to them before the senior people weigh in, and I'd resist the urge to rescue them when they're thinking. If a senior engineer jumps in with the answer, I'd redirect: "let's hear their reasoning first." That's a small move but it changes the social default, and over time it changes who the room expects to speak.

I'd also make the reasoning visible after the fact — walk through decisions they made and ask them to articulate why, including what they'd do differently. That builds the reflective habit that separates someone who executes well from someone who can own a decision and defend it. And I'd be explicit that deferring to seniority isn't wrong when the senior person genuinely has more information — the skill is knowing the difference between "they know something I don't" and "I'm avoiding the risk of being wrong."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a senior engineer kept overriding the mentee's decisions in front of the team? How would you tell whether the mentee was actually growing versus just performing confidence?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once the disagreement is personal, more debate makes it worse — each side reads the other's arguments as evidence of the bad motive rather than as technical content. So the first move is to change the format, not to referee the argument.

I'd separate the technical question from the relationship question. On the technical side, I'd try to convert the dispute into something testable: what would each architecture have to demonstrate to be acceptable, and can we get evidence on the discriminating criteria cheaply — a prototype, a bench measurement, a paper analysis of the failure modes that actually differ. If the two options are genuinely close, the decision may come down to criteria the team hasn't made explicit (manufacturability, regulatory burden, team familiarity, long-term maintenance), and surfacing those criteria often dissolves the personal framing because people realize they're optimizing for different things.

On the relationship side, I'd address the motive-attribution directly but privately — not in the room. Each side needs to hear that I don't believe the other is acting in bad faith, and that I expect them to extend the same assumption. Publicly, I'd set a norm that arguments are made against the criteria, not against the person.

I'd also be willing to make the call and own it if the evidence doesn't resolve it. A split team often needs a decision more than it needs the "right" decision, provided the decision is documented with rationale and the losing side's concerns are recorded. What I'd avoid is letting it drift, because unresolved personal conflict poisons everything downstream — reviews, integration, handoffs — long after the architecture question is settled.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if one side's concerns turned out to be correct after the decision was made? What would you do if the personal conflict predated this decision and had a longer history?
