# behavioral-leadership — Day 113

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the two strongest engineers on your team have each produced a fully documented, defensible design for the same subsystem — and both are refusing to concede, not because the data is ambiguous but because each has become personally invested in their own proposal?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the decision from the people. When two engineers are dug in, the disagreement has usually stopped being about the design and started being about identity — conceding feels like losing. So I'd reframe the goal explicitly: we are not choosing whose design wins, we are choosing the design that best serves the requirements, and the team will own the outcome regardless of authorship. I'd restate the decision criteria out loud — the actual requirements, constraints, and priorities — and get both engineers to agree that those criteria are the right ones before we evaluate either proposal against them. That step alone often defuses a lot, because it moves the conversation from "my design vs. yours" to "both designs vs. the criteria."

Then I'd force the comparison onto paper in a structured way: a decision matrix scored against the agreed criteria, with each engineer responsible for scoring both proposals, not just their own. Having to honestly evaluate the other person's design is a powerful corrective — it either surfaces genuine merit they'd been dismissing, or it exposes where their own proposal is weaker than they'd acknowledged. Where the matrix still leaves a real trade-off, I'd look for a cheap way to resolve it empirically rather than by argument: a bench measurement, a prototype, a simulation, a targeted test. Data settles disputes that rhetoric can't, and it lets both engineers save face because the answer came from the bench, not from a colleague.

If after all that the decision is still genuinely close, I'd make the call myself, document the rationale and the dissenting view, and be clear that the decision is mine to own. I'd also make sure the engineer whose proposal wasn't chosen has a real role in the chosen path — reviewing it, owning part of it — so they're invested in its success rather than waiting for it to fail. And I'd follow up after implementation to check whether the concerns the losing side raised actually materialized; if they did, that's important information, and treating those concerns seriously builds the trust that makes the next disagreement easier.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if, after you made the call, the engineer whose proposal lost continued to voice doubts about the chosen design in front of the wider team?
- How would you handle it if the decision matrix itself became a source of dispute — each engineer arguing the weightings were unfair?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is consistently high quality, but they have a habit of quietly redefining the scope of their own tasks — delivering something different from what was agreed — and the drift only becomes visible when it disrupts someone downstream?

**Answer:** The instinct is to treat this as a discipline problem, but I'd first try to understand what's driving it, because scope drift from a strong performer is usually not laziness or defiance — it's often someone who sees a "better" problem to solve and follows their judgment without realizing they've unilaterally changed the contract with the rest of the team. That's a real asset (initiative, technical judgment) attached to a real liability (no visibility, downstream surprises). I'd want to address both.

Concretely, I'd start by making the drift visible with specifics rather than generalities — a couple of concrete examples where what was delivered diverged from what was agreed, and what the downstream consequence was. Vague feedback like "you need to stick to scope" lands as a personality critique; specific examples land as a process observation. Then I'd get curious about their reasoning: in each case, why did they change course? Sometimes the answer reveals that the original scope was wrong and they were right to deviate — in which case the fix is about surfacing that earlier, not about stopping them. Sometimes it reveals they didn't realize the change was significant enough to flag. Either way, I learn something and they feel heard rather than scolded.

The structural fix is to make scope changes cheap to raise and hard to make silently. That means agreeing on what "this is a scope change worth flagging" looks like, and creating a low-friction way to raise it — a quick message, a standing check-in, a note in the task — so flagging doesn't feel like bureaucracy. I'd also make the downstream dependencies explicit, so the engineer can see who is affected when they change direction; often the drift happens because the person genuinely doesn't see the ripple. And I'd reinforce the behavior I want: when they do flag a proposed change early, respond positively even if I say no, so the lesson is "raising it is rewarded," not "raising it gets you overruled."

Finally, I'd set a clear expectation and check it over time. If the pattern continues after the conversation and the structural support, that becomes a performance conversation about judgment and collaboration, not just process — because a strong individual contributor who can't be relied on to deliver what was agreed is a risk to the whole team, however good their work is in isolation.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish between healthy initiative and problematic scope drift when the engineer's changes are genuinely improvements?
- What would you do if the downstream team had already started building against the originally agreed deliverable before the drift was discovered?

## Q3: How would you approach building a culture where engineers proactively flag risks and concerns early, on a team that has learned — from past experience — that raising a problem early tends to get the messenger blamed or handed extra work?

**Answer:** This is fundamentally a trust problem, and it won't be fixed by exhortation. If the team has learned that speaking up is punished, telling them "please speak up more" changes nothing — the behavior will only shift when the actual consequences shift, and they observe that shift repeatedly. So I'd focus on changing what happens after someone raises a concern, because that's what the team is actually watching.

The first lever is how I personally respond to bad news. When someone brings me a problem early, my reaction in that moment teaches the whole team what to expect. If my first response is "who caused this?" or visible frustration, I've confirmed their fear regardless of what I say afterward. If my first response is "thank you for catching this early — let's figure out what we do about it," and I genuinely mean it, that's the signal. I'd be deliberate about this, especially in the early days, because one bad reaction undoes a lot of good ones. I'd also make a point of publicly crediting people who surface risks early, so the team sees that the behavior is valued, not just tolerated.

The second lever is separating "raising a concern" from "being assigned the cleanup." If every time someone flags a risk they inherit the work of mitigating it, the rational move is to stay quiet. So I'd want a clear norm that flagging a risk is a shared responsibility — the person who raises it contributes to the response, but doesn't automatically own it alone, and the team decides together how to handle it. That removes the implicit tax on speaking up.

The third lever is making it safe to raise something half-formed. A lot of early concerns are gut feelings that aren't fully articulated yet, and if the only acceptable way to raise a concern is with a complete analysis, people will sit on their instincts until it's too late. I'd explicitly invite "I don't have this fully worked out, but something feels off about X" as a legitimate contribution, and treat those early flags as valuable rather than as noise to be dismissed. Over time, I'd want the team to see that the concerns raised early were the ones that saved us pain — that's the story that changes the culture.

I'd also be honest that culture change is slow and uneven. I'd watch for the moment someone raises a real concern and it goes well, and reinforce it; and if someone raises a concern and it goes badly despite my efforts, I'd address that quickly and visibly, because the team will be watching that case even more closely than the successes.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if a concern raised early turned out to be unfounded — how do you keep the "speak up" norm from degrading into noise?
- What would you do if you discovered that a manager below you was still reacting badly to bad news, undermining the culture you're trying to build?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — one that's outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team who has more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?

**Answer:** The honest starting point is to separate my ego from the decision. There's a pull to take the stretch assignment because it's interesting and because being accountable can feel like it means being the one who does the work — but those are not good reasons if someone else is better positioned to succeed. The question I'd actually ask is: what does this assignment need, and who is the best person to deliver it, given that I'm accountable regardless of who does it?

If a team member has more relevant expertise, the default should be to delegate to them, with me providing the accountability structure around it — clear success criteria, a review cadence, and a safety net for when they hit something they can't handle. That's usually the better outcome on multiple axes: the work gets done by someone with the right depth, the team member gets a growth opportunity, and I stay in the role I'm actually accountable for, which is ensuring the outcome, not personally producing it. The risk I'd manage is that "less seniority" doesn't mean "less capable," but it may mean less experience navigating the non-technical parts — stakeholder communication, escalation, knowing when to ask for help — and that's where I'd focus my support.

There are cases where I'd take it myself: if the assignment is genuinely time-critical and the ramp-up cost for me is lower than the coordination cost of delegating; if it's a stretch specifically because it requires judgment across areas I'm accountable for and can't easily transfer; or if delegating would leave a critical gap elsewhere. But I'd want to be honest with myself that those are real reasons and not rationalizations. And even when I take it, I'd look for ways to bring a team member in so the expertise doesn't stay siloed with me.

The thing I'd guard against most is the failure mode where the accountable person hoards the interesting work and the team never grows into it — that feels productive in the short term and hollows out the team over time. So my bias would be toward delegating with support, and taking the work myself only when I can articulate a specific reason why that's better for the outcome, not just more comfortable or more interesting for me.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you structure the support so that delegating a high-visibility assignment doesn't become "delegate and hope"?
- If you took the assignment yourself, how would you make sure the team member with the relevant expertise was still developed rather than sidelined?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a team through a period where the technical direction you advocated for is being challenged by new evidence — evidence that suggests a different approach would have been better — and you need to change course without the team concluding that your judgment can't be trusted?

**Answer:** The most important thing in that moment is to model the behavior I'd want from anyone on the team: follow the evidence, say so plainly, and don't defend a position just because it's mine. If I dig in or quietly hope the evidence goes away, I teach the team that being right matters more than being correct, and that's far more corrosive to trust than changing course. So I'd name it directly — "the evidence points a different way than the direction I argued for, and we should change course" — and I'd do it early, before the cost of changing grows.

But changing course well is more than admitting error. I'd want to be clear about what specifically the new evidence shows and what it doesn't, so the team understands the basis for the reversal rather than experiencing it as a flip-flop driven by whoever argued loudest most recently. I'd revisit the original reasoning honestly: what did we know then, what did we assume, and which assumption turned out to be wrong? That's useful beyond this one decision — it tells us something about how we make decisions, and it distinguishes "I was wrong" from "the information changed," which are different lessons. If I made a genuine judgment error, I'd own that specifically rather than hiding behind "new information," because the team can tell the difference and will respect the honesty more.

I'd also protect the people who supported the original direction. If team members backed my approach and now feel exposed, I'd make clear that the decision was mine and the accountability is mine — they weren't wrong to follow a reasoned direction, and I don't want them second-guessing themselves into paralysis. And I'd keep the focus forward: what does the new path require, what's the plan, who owns what. A clean, well-reasoned pivot that the team understands is far less damaging to confidence than a messy one, even if the original call was wrong.

Over the longer term, trust isn't built by never being wrong — it's built by being consistently reasoned and consistently honest when the reasoning changes. A team that has watched me change course on evidence, cleanly and without drama, is actually more confident in my judgment, not less, because they know I'll correct rather than defend.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if part of the team read the course change as indecisiveness rather than responsiveness to evidence?
- What would you do differently in the original decision-making process, knowing what the new evidence revealed?
