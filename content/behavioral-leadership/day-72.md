# behavioral-leadership — Day 72

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?
**Answer:** The first thing to recognize is that the team routing around the review is a symptom, not the disease — it means the review has lost credibility as a gate, and that's more dangerous than the original stalling. I'd separate the two problems and address the process one first. The root issue is usually that the review has no defined scope or exit criteria: if "done" is never defined, a determined reviewer can always find one more thing, and the rest of the team eventually concludes the review is unwinnable and works around it. So I'd restructure the review around a fixed agenda with explicit decision points — what is being approved today, what is deferred to a later gate, and what is explicitly out of scope. Objections raised outside the current scope get logged as action items with owners and target dates rather than blocking the current gate. That converts an open-ended objection into a tracked, time-boxed item, which is usually enough to unblock progress without silencing the reviewer.

The second part is the reviewer themselves. A pattern of a new objection every session often reflects genuine unease that isn't being articulated in a form the team can act on — sometimes the person can't quite name what worries them, so each session they reach for a new concrete-sounding objection. A private conversation is worth more than a public confrontation: ask what would need to be true for them to be comfortable signing off, and whether there's an underlying concern the individual objections are standing in for. If there is a real concern, it belongs on the agenda as a first-class item. If it turns out to be perfectionism or a need for control, that's a different conversation, and it may need to be handled at a management level rather than in the review itself.

I'd also be careful not to let "the team is routing around the review" become normalized. If people are quietly approving their own work to keep moving, that's a compliance and safety problem in a regulated environment, not just a process annoyance. I'd make the review's decisions and deferrals visible — a short written record of what was approved, what was deferred, and why — so that the workaround loses its appeal and the review regains its role as the legitimate path forward.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the persistent reviewer is more senior than you and their objections are technically valid but arriving too late to be actionable?
- What would you do if, after restructuring the review, the team still prefers to route around it because the new process feels slower than the old workaround?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?
**Answer:** Intermittent, non-reproducible failures are where the pressure to "just ship a fix" is most dangerous, because a fix you can't verify against a reproduction is a guess, and in a regulated device a guess that appears to work can mask the real cause until it surfaces in the field. So the first move is to resist treating "the symptom stopped" as evidence of a fix, and instead invest early in making the failure observable. That usually means instrumentation: adding logging, counters, or telemetry around the suspect subsystem so that when the failure next occurs, you capture the state that preceded it rather than just the fact that it happened. If you can't reproduce on demand, you have to make the device tell you what happened when it does.

In parallel, I'd structure the investigation so it doesn't depend on a single reproduction. That means building a fault tree or fishbone around the failure mode and systematically eliminating branches with evidence rather than intuition — environmental conditions, timing windows, power state transitions, temperature, specific sequences of user or system events. For an intermittent issue, the boundary conditions are often the whole story: it fails only at a particular supply voltage, only after a warm-up period, only when two operations overlap. Characterizing those boundaries is often more productive than chasing a single repro.

On containment versus correction: under schedule pressure, it's legitimate to put a containment in place — a watchdog, a retry, a defensive check — to protect users while the investigation continues, provided it's clearly labeled as containment and not mistaken for a root-cause fix. The trap is letting containment quietly become the permanent answer because the pressure eased. I'd keep the investigation open, with a defined verification-of-effectiveness step: how will we know the real cause is gone, and what evidence will demonstrate that? If the answer is "it hasn't happened again," that's not evidence — that's absence of data, and I'd say so explicitly rather than let the team declare victory.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide when the cost of continuing the investigation outweighs the risk of shipping with only a containment in place?
- What would you do if the instrumentation you added to catch the failure changes the timing enough that the failure no longer occurs?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?
**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is real: oversimplify and the firmware team makes assumptions that don't hold ("just average the samples"), over-detail and they disengage because it reads as a hardware problem they can't influence. The bridge is to translate the constraint into the firmware team's own vocabulary — what it means for the numbers they will actually see and the decisions they have to make. So rather than saying "the ADC is 12-bit with a noisy front end," I'd characterize it as: at this sample rate, the effective noise floor is roughly X counts, so a single reading is only trustworthy to about Y; averaging over N samples improves it as roughly the square root of N, but only if the noise is uncorrelated and the signal is stable over that window. That gives them a lever (averaging, filtering, sample rate) and a limit (what averaging can't fix), in units they can reason about.

I'd also be explicit about what the hardware cannot deliver, so the firmware team doesn't spend effort trying to extract resolution that isn't there. If the analog front end has a noise component that's correlated with, say, a switching supply, no amount of digital filtering removes it — that's a hardware fix, and the firmware team needs to know the boundary so they escalate rather than absorb it. Naming the boundary clearly is what prevents the "firmware team quietly owns a hardware problem" pattern.

Finally, I'd back it with data rather than assertion — a characterization plot, a noise histogram, a capture of the raw samples — because firmware engineers are rightly skeptical of hand-waved hardware claims, and a shared artifact becomes the reference both teams argue from. The goal isn't to make the firmware team experts in the analog front end; it's to give them an accurate mental model of the signal they're processing, including its limits, so their filtering and threshold decisions are grounded in reality rather than optimism.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filtering approach would work in theory but adds latency that violates a timing requirement elsewhere in the system?
- What would you do if, after you characterize the front end, the firmware team concludes the constraint is severe enough that the hardware should be redesigned — and the schedule doesn't allow it?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?
**Answer:** The instinct is to find a single level of detail that works for everyone, and that's usually the wrong target — a mixed audience doesn't need one depth, it needs a layered structure where each person can find the level they need without the others being forced through it. So I'd lead with the decision and its consequences in plain terms: what we're choosing between, what each option costs in schedule, risk, and performance, and what I'm recommending and why. That top layer has to stand on its own for the product manager and be defensible to the quality manager, because those are the people whose concerns are usually about risk and schedule rather than circuit behavior.

Below that, I'd have the engineering detail ready but not front-loaded — the specific numbers, the failure modes considered, the assumptions the recommendation rests on. The key is to make the assumptions visible, because that's where the quality manager's real interest lies: not in the resistor value, but in what happens if the assumption behind it is wrong. For a medical device, the quality manager is effectively asking "what's the risk if this trade-off is wrong, and how would we know?" — so I'd address that explicitly rather than leave it implicit.

I'd also be deliberate about not hiding behind jargon in either direction. Using technical shorthand to avoid explaining a risk to the product manager is a way of dodging accountability, and dumbing down the engineering to the point where the quality manager can't evaluate the safety argument is equally evasive. The test I'd apply to myself: can each person in the room state, in their own terms, what we're deciding and what could go wrong? If not, the presentation has failed regardless of how accurate the content was.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the product manager pushes for a decision before the engineering detail has been presented, because they see the discussion as already decided?
- What would you do if the quality manager raises a risk concern in the meeting that you hadn't considered, and it materially changes the trade-off?

## Q5: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?
**Answer:** The deferral is usually not a confidence problem in the way it first appears — it's often a rational response to an environment where they've never been given the authority or the consequences of a decision, so deferring has been the safe and rewarded behavior. If every time they formed an opinion a senior person overrode it, they learned that forming opinions out loud was pointless. So the mentoring has to change the conditions, not just encourage them to "speak up more," which is advice that doesn't address why they don't.

Practically, that means giving them a decision to own with real stakes and real boundaries — something where they're the one who has to weigh the trade-offs and commit, and where I deliberately don't step in with the answer even when I have one. The temptation as a mentor is to rescue them the moment they head toward a choice I wouldn't make, and that rescue is exactly what teaches them that their judgment doesn't count. Instead I'd let them make the call, then review the reasoning afterward: what did you weigh, what did you assume, what would change your mind? That builds the muscle of owning a decision without the fear that a wrong answer ends the conversation.

I'd also work on the specific skill of disagreeing upward, because that's often the missing piece. A lot of engineers who defer in the room are perfectly capable of forming a view; they just don't have a practiced way to express it to someone more senior without it feeling like insubordination. Rehearsing that — how to frame a concern as a question or a trade-off rather than a challenge, how to ask "have we considered X?" instead of "that's wrong" — is concrete and teachable, and it lowers the cost of speaking up. Over time the goal is that they stop needing permission to have a view, and start treating their own judgment as something worth putting on the table.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if, given a decision to own, the engineer makes a choice you consider clearly wrong — do you let it stand?
- What would you do if the deferral pattern is reinforced by a senior engineer on the team who consistently overrides junior opinions in reviews?
