# behavioral-leadership — Day 98

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two problems: the review isn't converging, and the team has lost confidence in it. Routing around review is a symptom, not the disease — if I only chase the bypass, I'll push the process underground. I'd start by looking at the objections themselves. Are they genuinely new technical concerns, or are they restatements of the same underlying worry in different clothes? If it's the latter, the real issue is that the reviewer doesn't feel their core concern has been addressed, so each session they re-litigate it. I'd have a one-on-one with that reviewer before the next session, ask them to articulate their top one or two concerns in writing, and then structure the review agenda around answering those explicitly. If the objections are genuinely new each time, that usually means the review scope was never bounded — the package didn't define what was in and out of scope, so every session becomes an open-ended design critique. I'd fix that by publishing a review charter: what decisions this review is meant to close, what's deferred to a later gate, and a parking lot for out-of-scope items with an owner and a date. I'd also introduce a rule that objections must come with a proposed resolution or a specific question — that converts vague discomfort into something the team can act on. On the routing-around behavior, I'd address it directly but without blame: acknowledge that the process has been painful, explain the changes, and be clear that bypassing review isn't acceptable because in a regulated context the review record is part of the design history. The goal is to make the process worth using again, not to punish people for working around a broken one.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the reviewer's concerns turned out to be legitimate and the team had genuinely been dismissing them? How would you handle it if the reviewer is more senior than you and resists the structured approach?

## Q2: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?
**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is real: oversimplify and firmware makes design choices that assume precision the hardware can't deliver; over-detail and you hand them a datasheet dump they can't act on. I'd start from what the firmware team actually needs to decide — filtering strategy, sampling rate, calibration approach, alarm thresholds — and frame the constraint in those terms. Concretely, that means giving them a small number of quantified statements rather than a narrative: effective number of bits at the sampling rate they'll use, the noise floor and its spectral character (is it white, is there a switching-supply spur sitting on the signal band), the input range and where the signal of interest sits within it, and the drift behavior over temperature. I'd pair that with a simple measurement they can reproduce — a capture of the raw ADC output with a known input, so they can validate their filter against real data rather than my description of it. The key is to state the constraint as a budget with margins, not a single number: "you have this much noise headroom, this much settling time after a mux switch, this much drift over the operating range." That lets firmware reason about trade-offs themselves instead of coming back to me for every decision. I'd also make clear which constraints are hard (regulatory, safety) versus which are soft (could be improved with a board spin), because that changes how much conservatism they should build in. And I'd close the loop: ask them to show me their filter response or calibration routine against the captured data, so we catch a mismatch before integration testing rather than during it.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filter would mask a real sensor fault rather than reject noise? What would you do if the hardware constraint turned out to be worse in production units than on your bench characterization?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?
**Answer:** My default is that escalation is a tool, not a failure — but it should be reserved for the cases where it actually adds something the team can't generate on its own. I'd work through a few questions. First, is the disagreement technical or is it about something else — ownership, credit, history between the two people? If it's technical, it's usually resolvable with data: define the decision criteria together, agree on a test or prototype that would discriminate between the options, and let the result settle it. That's the outcome I want in almost every case, because it builds the team's capability to resolve the next disagreement without me. Second, is the decision reversible or expensive? A reversible choice with a clear owner should just be made and documented with rationale; escalation for a low-stakes, easily-changed decision wastes everyone's time. Third, does the disagreement actually require authority I don't have — a budget, a schedule commitment, a cross-team resource, a regulatory position? That's a legitimate escalation, and I'd frame it as "we need a decision at this level," not "these two can't agree." Fourth, is there a values or safety dimension where the team genuinely shouldn't be the final arbiter? That goes up regardless of seniority. If I do escalate, I'd never just forward the conflict — I'd bring my manager a clear statement of the options, the trade-offs, the team's recommendation if there is one, and what decision is needed by when. Escalating a well-framed decision is good leadership; escalating a raw argument is offloading.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if your manager made a decision you believed was technically wrong? How would you handle it if one of the engineers went around you to escalate directly?

## Q4: How would you approach building a risk management culture on a medical device team that currently treats ISO 14971 documentation as a checkbox exercise done after the design is complete?
**Answer:** The root cause of checkbox risk management is almost always that the artifact is produced for the auditor rather than used by the engineers, so it has no feedback loop into design decisions. I'd attack that by making risk visible during design rather than after it. Practically, that means risk items get raised in design reviews as first-class agenda items — not "does this meet spec" but "what happens if this fails, how would we know, and what's the severity." I'd push the team toward thinking in terms of hazardous situations and harms early, even roughly, because a rough risk discussion during architecture is worth more than a polished FMEA after layout is frozen. I'd also make the linkage concrete: every mitigation should trace to a design element, a test, or a labeling control, and every one of those should be findable from the risk file. When engineers see that their design decisions actually populate the risk file, the file stops feeling like paperwork and starts feeling like a record of their thinking. I'd model the behavior myself — in reviews, I'd ask the risk question before the performance question, and I'd be willing to slow a decision down when the risk picture is unclear. I'd also celebrate the cases where risk analysis changed a design, because that's the evidence the team needs that the process has teeth. And I'd be honest about the regulatory consequence: a risk file that doesn't reflect the real design is a liability in an audit, not a formality. Culture change here is slow, and it comes from repeated small signals that risk thinking is how we design, not something we do to satisfy quality.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality team's template and process actively discourage early, rough risk discussion? What would you do if a senior engineer openly said risk analysis is a waste of engineering time?

## Q5: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but consistently becomes defensive when their work is questioned, and the review needs to be genuinely critical to be useful?
**Answer:** Defensiveness in review usually comes from a mismatch between what the presenter expects the review to be and what it needs to be. If they've experienced reviews as judgment of their competence, every technical question feels like an attack, and the rational response is to defend. So I'd work on both the framing and the mechanics. On framing, I'd be explicit before the review about its purpose: the review is there to find problems while they're cheap to fix, and a review that finds nothing is a failed review, not a successful one. I'd separate the person from the design in my own language — "this section" rather than "you," questions rather than verdicts, and I'd ask the presenter to walk through their reasoning before anyone critiques, so the review starts from understanding rather than from objection. On mechanics, I'd structure the review so critique is expected and normalized: a round where each reviewer must name at least one thing that could fail or one assumption that should be tested, so criticism isn't a signal that something is uniquely wrong with this design. I'd also take some of the heat myself by putting my own work up for review in the same forum and modeling how to receive critique well. If the defensiveness persists, I'd have a private conversation, framed around the review's effectiveness rather than their personality: "when the discussion gets defensive, we stop finding problems, and that costs us later — how can we make this easier for you?" Sometimes the answer is that they need more preparation time, or a pre-review with one trusted colleague so the first hearing isn't in front of everyone. The goal is a review that's genuinely critical and a presenter who can hear it, and those aren't in conflict once the framing is right.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the defensive behavior was affecting junior engineers' willingness to raise concerns in the same review? How would you handle it if the presenter's manager told you to back off because the engineer was already under pressure?
