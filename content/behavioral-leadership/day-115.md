# behavioral-leadership — Day 115

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the two strongest engineers on your team have each produced a fully documented, defensible design for the same subsystem, and both are refusing to concede — not because the data is ambiguous but because each has become personally invested in their own proposal?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the technical disagreement from the personal investment, because those need different handling. On the technical side, if both designs are genuinely defensible, then the decision criteria that matter are probably not the ones being argued about — they're the tiebreakers: manufacturability, test coverage, long-term maintenance burden, supply chain risk, how each option fails, and how each interacts with the rest of the system. I'd reframe the conversation around a shared decision matrix with weighted criteria agreed *before* scoring, so the discussion moves from "my design vs. yours" to "how do both designs score against the criteria the project actually cares about." That depersonalizes it — the engineers are now evaluating against a rubric rather than defending themselves.

On the personal side, I'd have a private conversation with each engineer, separately, and name the dynamic honestly but without accusation: "I've noticed this has become a point of pride for both of you, and I need us to get to a decision the team can commit to." I'd give each of them a face-saving path — for example, explicitly crediting the strengths of the losing design and, where feasible, folding a good idea from it into the chosen one, or capturing it as a documented alternative for a future revision. People dig in harder when conceding feels like admitting their work was wasted. If the matrix still doesn't break the tie, I'd look for a cheap decisive experiment — a prototype, a bench measurement, a simulation — because data resolves disputes that argument can't. And if it truly comes down to judgment, I'd make the call myself, document the rationale and the dissenting view in the decision record, and be clear that the decision is mine to own so neither engineer has to carry the loss personally.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the losing engineer continues to quietly advocate for their design after the decision is made? How do you make sure the decision record captures the dissent without it reading as a permanent mark against either engineer?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether to absorb a small schedule slip quietly within the team or escalate it, when you suspect the slip is the leading edge of a larger problem?

**Answer:** My default is to escalate early, but the *framing* matters more than the binary choice. A small slip that I genuinely believe is contained — a few days, a known cause, a clear recovery path — I'd absorb and simply report in the normal status cadence, because escalating every minor wobble trains stakeholders to ignore my escalations. But the key phrase in the question is "leading edge of a larger problem." If my instinct is that this slip is a symptom, then absorbing it quietly is the wrong move precisely because it hides the signal. The whole value of early escalation is that it buys the organization options while they're still cheap.

So what I'd actually do is escalate the *pattern*, not just the slip. I'd go to my manager with something like: "The task is three days late, which on its own is fine. But here's why I'm flagging it: the estimate assumed X, and X turned out to be wrong in a way that I think applies to two other tasks downstream. I don't have a revised number yet, but I want you to know the assumption may be broken before I've fully quantified it." That gives the manager the information without forcing me to overpromise a new date I can't defend. I'd also set myself a short deadline — say, a week — to either confirm the slip is contained (and say so, closing the loop) or come back with a real re-plan. What I'd avoid is the two failure modes: silently absorbing it and hoping it resolves, or escalating it as a crisis before I've done the basic work to understand it. The judgment call is about *confidence*, not size — a small slip I'm confident about stays internal; a small slip I'm suspicious of gets flagged with the uncertainty made explicit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if your manager's response to the early flag was "don't bring me problems without solutions"? What signals tell you a slip is genuinely contained versus the leading edge of something bigger?

## Q3: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across hardware, firmware, and verification teams whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?

**Answer:** The divergence happens late but it's created early — each team forms its definition implicitly, in its own language, and nobody notices the mismatch until integration. So the fix is to make "done" explicit and shared *before* the work starts, and to make it concrete enough that disagreement surfaces immediately rather than at the milestone. I'd start by getting the three leads in a room and having each write down, independently, what "done" means for a specific deliverable — say, a sensor subsystem. Inevitably the hardware lead says "board brings up, rails in spec, sensor reads correctly," the firmware lead says "driver integrated, data flowing to the application layer," and verification says "requirements traced, test cases executed, results documented and reviewed." Those are three different finish lines, and seeing them side by side is the whole point — the gap becomes visible in an hour instead of in month nine.

From there I'd build a single exit-criteria checklist per deliverable that all three sign off on, with each item owned by a named team and each item *objectively checkable* — not "firmware works" but "firmware passes the agreed interface test against the hardware with the specified tolerance." I'd tie it to the requirements and traceability so "done" is anchored to the spec rather than to anyone's intuition. The other half is cadence: a short, regular cross-team checkpoint where each team reports against the shared checklist, so drift shows up in weeks, not at the gate. And I'd expect the definition to be revisited at each phase boundary, because "done" for a prototype is legitimately different from "done" for design verification — the failure mode isn't having different definitions, it's having *unstated* ones. Making them explicit and versioned turns a late surprise into an early conversation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a team that agrees to the shared checklist in the meeting but quietly keeps its own internal standard? Where does the verification team's definition of "done" legitimately need to be stricter than the others', and how do you keep that from feeling like moving goalposts?

## Q4: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but consistently becomes defensive when their work is questioned, and the review needs to be genuinely critical to be useful?

**Answer:** The defensiveness is usually a response to *how* critique lands, not to critique itself — a strong engineer who feels their competence is being questioned will defend, whereas the same engineer given a problem to solve will engage. So I'd change the framing of the review rather than ask them to be less defensive. Concretely: I'd separate the review into two passes. The first pass is purely informational — the presenter walks through the design uninterrupted, and reviewers ask only clarifying questions, no judgments. That lets them establish the full context and feel heard before any critique begins, which removes the "you didn't understand what I did" trigger. The second pass is structured critique, and I'd steer questions into a form that's about the design, not the designer: instead of "why did you do it this way?" (which reads as an accusation), "what happens if this input goes out of range?" or "help me understand the trade-off you made here." Questions that invite the engineer to demonstrate their reasoning rather than justify it tend to convert defensiveness into engagement.

I'd also model the behavior myself as the lead — being visibly open to challenge on my own decisions in the same room sets the norm that critique is normal and not a status threat. And I'd set the expectation up front that the purpose of the review is to find problems *before* they reach a customer or an auditor, so a caught issue is a win, not a failure. Privately, if the pattern persists, I'd have a direct conversation: name the behavior, name its cost (reviewers start withholding, which is dangerous on a safety-critical design), and ask what would make the review feel less like an attack. What I would *not* do is soften the review to avoid the reaction — on a medical device, a review that can't be critical is worse than no review, because it creates false confidence. The goal is to keep the rigor and change the temperature.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if other reviewers started pulling their punches to avoid setting the presenter off? How do you distinguish legitimate defensiveness — the reviewer really is being unfair — from a pattern the presenter needs to work on?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument?

**Answer:** The reason people don't raise early concerns is almost never that they lack the instinct — it's that they've learned it's costly. Either the concern gets dismissed, or raising it gets them handed the work, or it's read as insubordination. So building the culture is mostly about lowering that cost and making the behavior visibly rewarded, especially the first few times. The most powerful lever is how I respond to the *first* half-formed concern someone raises. If I say "come back when you have data," I've just taught the whole room to stay quiet until they're certain — which is exactly backwards, because the value of a gut feeling is that it arrives *before* the data exists. So I'd explicitly make room for it: "That's a hunch — let's treat it as a hypothesis and spend twenty minutes seeing if it holds up." That reframes an inarticulate concern as the start of an investigation rather than an incomplete deliverable.

I'd also build lightweight mechanisms so concerns have a low-friction path that doesn't require confronting a senior person in a meeting: an anonymous or low-stakes channel to flag a design decision for a second look, a standing "devil's advocate" role in design reviews where someone is *assigned* to argue the opposite case, and a norm that the person who raises a concern isn't automatically the person who has to resolve it. On the senior-person dimension specifically, I'd make it explicit that a decision being made by a senior engineer or by consensus doesn't close the question — consensus can be wrong, and seniority isn't evidence. I'd model this by publicly changing my own mind when someone raises a good concern, and saying so out loud, because nothing teaches "it's safe to challenge" faster than watching a senior person be challenged and respond well. The gut-feeling piece needs deliberate protection: I'd tell the team that a vague unease is a legitimate reason to ask for a second look, and that "I can't articulate why yet" is an acceptable starting point — the articulation is the work that follows, not a precondition for speaking.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you keep the "raise it early even on a gut feeling" norm from turning into noise, where every decision gets relitigated? What would you do the first time someone raises a concern about a senior engineer's decision and the senior engineer reacts badly?
