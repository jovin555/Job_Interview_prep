# behavioral-leadership — Day 52

## Q1: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** The default should be to resolve it within the team, because escalation that could have been handled locally erodes the team's ownership and signals that technical disagreements are someone else's problem. But "resolve it locally" doesn't mean forcing a decision by authority — it means running a structured process first. I'd start by making sure both engineers are actually disagreeing about the same thing: often two people argue past each other because they're optimizing for different criteria (one for reliability, one for cost, one for schedule) without naming it. I'd get each to state their position, the criteria they're weighting, and what evidence would change their mind. Then I'd look for whether the disagreement can be settled empirically — a bench measurement, a prototype, a simulation, a literature or vendor-data check — rather than by opinion. If it can, we run the experiment and let the data decide. If it genuinely can't be settled with available evidence and the decision is consequential, I'd frame it as a risk trade-off and make a call myself, documenting the rationale and the dissenting view so it's on record.

Escalation becomes the right move in a few specific cases: the decision has cross-team or cross-project impact beyond my scope; it involves a regulatory, safety, or compliance interpretation where I'm not the authority; it's consuming disproportionate time and blocking a critical path; or there's a pattern where one party won't engage in good faith. In those cases I'd escalate with a clear framing — here's the decision needed, here are the two positions and their trade-offs, here's what we've already tried, here's my recommendation — rather than dumping the conflict upward. Escalating a well-framed decision is leadership; escalating an unresolved argument is abdication.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If you make the call yourself and the losing engineer remains unconvinced, how do you keep them engaged rather than disengaged?
- How would you distinguish a healthy technical disagreement from a personality conflict that needs a different kind of intervention?

## Q2: How would you approach a design review where a senior engineer's proposed architecture is technically sound but the documentation package is too thin for reviewers to evaluate the safety-critical portions, and the engineer is likely to take a request for more detail as a personal criticism?

**Answer:** The core problem here is separating the quality of the design from the quality of the evidence presented for it, and making sure the engineer hears that distinction. I'd open the review by explicitly crediting what's strong about the architecture — if it's technically sound, say so, and say why — so the engineer isn't primed to hear everything that follows as an attack. Then I'd reframe the documentation gap not as a personal failing but as a review-process requirement: the purpose of the package is to let reviewers independently verify the safety-critical reasoning, and if that reasoning isn't written down, the review can't actually discharge its function. That's a statement about what the review needs, not about the engineer's competence.

Practically, I'd avoid a vague "add more detail" request, which is easy to hear as criticism and hard to act on. Instead I'd be specific: for each safety-critical claim, what's the failure mode being addressed, what's the rationale for the chosen mitigation, what's the verification evidence, and where does it live? I'd offer to work through one section together as a template so the expected depth is concrete rather than abstract. If the engineer is sensitive to critique in public, I'd raise the documentation gap in a one-on-one before or after the review rather than turning the review into a referendum on their work. And I'd keep the review itself focused on the technical content that *is* reviewable, parking the under-documented portions as action items with owners and dates rather than blocking the whole session. The goal is to get the evidence we need without making the engineer defensive enough to stop engaging.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What if the engineer pushes back that the documentation standard is bureaucratic overhead and the design is obviously safe?
- How would you handle it if this pattern repeats across multiple reviews with the same engineer?

## Q3: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each have different estimates for how long their work will take, and there's no historical data from similar projects?

**Answer:** When there's no historical data, the honest starting point is that every estimate is a guess with wide error bars, and the job isn't to produce a single confident number — it's to make the uncertainty visible and structure the work so the biggest unknowns get resolved earliest. I'd start by having each team break their work down into the smallest meaningful units rather than giving one lump estimate, because a single number hides where the risk actually lives. Then I'd classify each unit by confidence: things we've done before and can estimate tightly, things that are similar to prior work but not identical, and things that are genuinely novel or dependent on external parties (test houses, regulators, suppliers). The novel and external items are where the schedule will actually slip, so they deserve explicit risk buffers and early attention rather than being averaged into a smooth timeline.

I'd also map the dependencies between teams, because in medical device development the critical path usually runs through handoffs — firmware can't fully validate until hardware is stable, regulatory submission can't start until design outputs are frozen. Those handoffs are where different estimates collide, and making them explicit turns "we disagree on the total" into "we disagree about this specific interface and when it's ready." For the timeline itself, I'd present ranges rather than points, with the assumptions behind each range written down, and I'd identify which assumptions, if wrong, would move the date most. That gives stakeholders something more useful than a false-precision date: a picture of what we're betting on. Finally, I'd build in explicit checkpoints where estimates get revised as real data arrives, so the plan is a living document rather than a commitment made once under maximum uncertainty.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you communicate a range-based timeline to an executive who wants a single committed date?
- What would you do if the regulatory team's estimate is dramatically longer than hardware and firmware combined, and they won't break it down further?

## Q4: How would you approach handling a situation where a team member consistently delivers high-quality technical work but has a pattern of missing internal deadlines, causing downstream delays for the rest of the team?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd resist is treating this as either "great engineer, ignore the deadlines" or "misses deadlines, performance problem." Both miss the point: the person is delivering real value, and the missed deadlines are causing real harm to others, so the goal is to keep the value while fixing the harm. I'd start by trying to understand the pattern rather than the incidents. Is the person underestimating their own work? Are they gold-plating — spending extra time perfecting something past the point of diminishing returns? Are they absorbing unplanned work from elsewhere? Are the deadlines themselves unrealistic, so the "miss" is really a planning failure shared by the team? The answer changes the intervention completely.

If it's underestimation or gold-plating, the fix is often about making the cost of lateness visible to the person in concrete terms — not "you missed the date" but "because this slipped, the firmware team lost two days of integration time and had to context-switch." Many strong engineers who work in isolation genuinely don't see the downstream ripple, and once they do, they self-correct. If it's unrealistic deadlines, that's on me as the lead to fix the planning, not on them. I'd also look at whether the person's work style fits the team's cadence: some engineers do their best work in long uninterrupted stretches and struggle with frequent small deadlines, and the answer might be restructuring how their work is sequenced rather than asking them to become someone they're not. Throughout, I'd keep it a one-on-one conversation, be specific about the observed pattern and its impact, and agree on a concrete change we'll both watch — not a vague "please hit your dates." If the pattern persists after that, it becomes a performance conversation, but I'd want to have genuinely tried to understand the cause first.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the person's high-quality work is genuinely worth the delay, and other team members are the ones who need to adjust?
- What would you do if the person becomes defensive and insists the deadlines were never realistic in the first place?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when team morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than systemic issues?

**Answer:** The framing I'd set from the very start is that a post-mortem is about making the system smarter, not about finding who to blame — and that if the outcome is a person's name attached to a failure, we've done it wrong. That's easy to say and hard to hold, especially when morale is low and people are looking for somewhere to put the frustration. So I'd structure the session to make systemic analysis the path of least resistance. I'd start by reconstructing the timeline of what actually happened — decisions made, information available at the time, handoffs, external events — without evaluating anyone yet. Then I'd look for the points where the project went off track and ask, for each, what conditions made that outcome likely: unclear requirements, optimistic estimates, missing review gates, a dependency nobody owned, a risk that was known but not acted on. The question is never "who dropped this" but "what about how we work made this easy to drop."

I'd also explicitly separate the things that were genuinely outside our control from the things that weren't, because a post-mortem that concludes "everything was bad luck" teaches nothing, and one that concludes "everything was our fault" is both inaccurate and demoralizing. I'd want a small number of concrete, owned changes to come out of it — not a long list nobody will act on — and I'd assign owners and follow-up dates so the analysis doesn't evaporate. On morale specifically, I'd acknowledge the difficulty honestly rather than pretending the miss wasn't painful, and I'd point to the fact that the team shipped something real and that the purpose of this exercise is to protect the next project, not to relitigate this one. If I sense that certain individuals are being scapegoated in side conversations, I'd address that directly and privately, because a culture where failures get personalized will quickly stop surfacing problems early — which is exactly the opposite of what a medical device team needs.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the root cause genuinely does trace back to one person's repeated poor judgment, not a systemic issue?
- What would you do if leadership above you wants a post-mortem that identifies individuals to hold accountable?
