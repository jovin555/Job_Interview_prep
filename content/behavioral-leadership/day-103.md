# behavioral-leadership — Day 103

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two problems: the review is not converging, and the team has lost confidence in it. Those need different fixes, and the second is more dangerous because once people route around a gate, you lose both the review's value and the traceability that a regulated environment depends on. I'd start by diagnosing why the objections keep coming. There are usually a few root causes: the review scope was never bounded, so the reviewer is legitimately finding new issues each time because the package keeps growing; the reviewer is raising concerns that should have been captured earlier and is effectively doing upstream work in a downstream forum; or the objections are a symptom of an unresolved disagreement that nobody has named. I'd look at the objection log across sessions — if the new objections are in areas the package didn't cover, that's a scoping problem; if they're re-litigating the same decision in different words, that's an unresolved decision that needs to be taken off the review and resolved directly. Then I'd restructure the review itself: a fixed agenda with a defined scope per session, a rule that new out-of-scope concerns get logged as action items rather than debated in the room, and a clear entry/exit criterion for what "reviewed" means. I'd also talk to the reviewer privately — often the behavior is driven by a real fear that a concern will be dropped if they don't keep raising it, and giving them a reliable mechanism to log and track concerns removes the incentive to re-raise. On the routing-around problem, I'd be direct that bypassing the review is not acceptable in a regulated context, but I'd also acknowledge that the team did it because the process was failing them, and that fixing the process is my job, not theirs. The goal is a review that converges because it has a bounded scope and a trustworthy action-tracking mechanism, not one that converges because people gave up.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the persistent reviewer's concerns turned out to be legitimate and the team had been dismissing them? What would you do if the reviewer's manager saw the reviewer as "doing their job" and resisted any change to their involvement?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?
**Answer:** I'd structure the presentation in layers rather than trying to find a single level of detail that suits everyone, because there isn't one. The top layer is the decision itself and its consequences in terms each stakeholder cares about: for the product manager, schedule and cost impact; for the quality manager, risk and regulatory implications; for the engineers, the technical mechanism. I'd lead with a one-slide summary that states the trade-off, the options, the recommendation, and what each option costs in schedule, risk, and performance — no equations, no component part numbers. Then I'd have the technical detail available as backup material that I can go into on request, rather than front-loading it. The key discipline is being explicit about which claims are load-bearing for the decision and which are supporting detail. If the decision hinges on, say, a noise margin calculation, that calculation needs to be visible enough that the quality manager can see it was done and the engineers can check it — but it doesn't need to be walked through line by line unless someone asks. I'd also be careful not to hide uncertainty behind false precision: if a number is an estimate, I'd say so, because a quality manager who later discovers an estimate was presented as a measurement will stop trusting the whole presentation. And I'd make sure each stakeholder leaves knowing what they're being asked to decide or endorse, because a mixed audience often leaves a meeting with different understandings of what was agreed. The failure mode I'd most want to avoid is burying the decision in technical depth so that the non-technical stakeholders disengage and the decision gets made by default by whoever is loudest.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the product manager pushed for a level of certainty the engineering data couldn't support? What would you do if an engineer in the room started going deep into detail that was confusing the non-technical stakeholders?

## Q3: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?
**Answer:** The pattern here is usually not a capability gap but a confidence and ownership gap — the engineer has learned that deferring is safe, and has never had a low-stakes opportunity to be wrong and recover. So the mentoring approach needs to create those opportunities deliberately. I'd start by giving them ownership of a decision with a genuinely bounded scope — something where the consequences of a suboptimal choice are recoverable and the decision doesn't sit on a safety-critical path — and then step back from it. The hard part for me as a mentor is not jumping in when they're heading toward a choice I wouldn't make, as long as it's defensible. If it's clearly wrong, I'd ask questions that surface the gap rather than supplying the answer, so they build the reasoning muscle. I'd also work on the "most senior in the room" dynamic specifically: in design reviews, I'd explicitly ask for their view before offering mine, and I'd name the behavior when it happens — "you had a position on this earlier, what was it?" — so they get practice stating a view in front of seniority. I'd also help them build the habit of writing down the decision and its rationale, because owning a decision end-to-end includes being able to defend it later, and writing forces the reasoning to be explicit. Over time I'd increase the scope and the stakes, and I'd be explicit with them about the progression so they know it's intentional rather than random. The measure of success isn't that they start making decisions I'd make — it's that they start making decisions and standing behind them, including when a senior person disagrees.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's deference was actually well-founded because they genuinely lacked the technical depth for the decision? How would you handle it if a senior engineer on the team kept overriding the mentee's decisions, undermining the whole effort?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?
**Answer:** The pressure to ship a fix quickly is exactly the condition under which teams skip the investigation and ship a guess, so the first job is to separate containment from correction and be explicit about which one we're doing. Containment is legitimate and can be fast: if there's a field risk, you contain it — a workaround, a usage restriction, a monitoring escalation — while the investigation proceeds properly. What you must not do is call a containment measure a fix and close the investigation, because an intermittent failure that isn't understood will come back. For the investigation itself, the core problem is that you can't reproduce on demand, so the strategy is to maximize information per occurrence rather than trying to force reproduction. That means instrumenting the system so that when the failure does occur, you capture the state that distinguishes it from normal operation — logging around the suspect subsystem, capturing environmental and timing context, recording the sequence of events leading up to it. I'd also work the problem from the other direction: characterize the failure's signature statistically. When does it happen — after how long, under what conditions, at what rate? Even an intermittent failure has a distribution, and the distribution constrains the hypothesis space. I'd build a fishbone across the plausible categories — timing, power, thermal, environmental, software state, interaction between subsystems — and then design targeted experiments that try to shift the failure rate rather than reproduce the failure outright: change one variable and see if the rate moves. A rate that responds to a variable is strong evidence even without a clean reproduction. Throughout, I'd keep the investigation structured and documented — 8D or equivalent — so that the reasoning is visible and the eventual fix can be verified against the actual root cause rather than against "it hasn't happened again yet." And I'd be honest with stakeholders that an intermittent failure carries schedule uncertainty that a known failure doesn't, rather than promising a date the investigation can't support.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify a fix for an intermittent failure when you can't reproduce the original failure to test against? How would you decide when the evidence is strong enough to declare a root cause versus continuing to investigate?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?
**Answer:** When a technical disagreement turns personal, the technical content has usually stopped being the actual driver — each side is now defending its standing, and the argument has become about who is right rather than what is right. The first move is to de-escalate the framing without dismissing the substance: name that both options are viable, that the team's job is to choose on stated criteria rather than to win, and that a decision either way is better than an unresolved split. Then I'd try to convert the disagreement into something testable. In hardware and firmware, a lot of architecture disputes can be resolved by defining the criteria explicitly and, where feasible, prototyping or benchmarking against them — even a rough prototype or a paper analysis against agreed criteria moves the conversation from assertion to evidence. If a prototype isn't feasible in the time available, I'd have each side write down its case against the same criteria: what does each option cost in schedule, risk, performance, and maintainability, and what would have to be true for each to be the wrong choice. That often reveals that the two sides are optimizing different criteria, which is a much more tractable problem than "they're wrong." I'd also be alert to whether the personal dimension is being driven by something outside the technical question — a past conflict, a perceived slight, a fear about ownership of the outcome — and address that privately if so, because no amount of technical analysis resolves a relationship problem. As the decision-maker, I'd set a decision date and make the call if the team can't converge, and I'd document the rationale and the dissenting view so that the losing side's concerns are on record and can be revisited if the chosen path runs into trouble. The goal isn't a unanimous team — it's a team that can disagree, decide, and commit.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the losing side continued to undermine the chosen architecture after the decision was made? How would you handle it if you personally believed one side was technically right but the other side had the stronger relationship case?
