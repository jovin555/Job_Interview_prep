# behavioral-leadership — Day 123

## Q1: How would you approach leading a team through a situation where a key supplier discontinues a component mid-project, and the replacement requires both a hardware redesign and a firmware change, with the two teams already behind schedule?

**Answer:** The first move is to establish the actual constraint rather than let it become a blame exercise. I'd pull together a short cross-functional session — hardware, firmware, purchasing, and whoever owns the schedule — and frame the problem as a shared one: the part is gone, the question is what the fastest *safe* path to a qualified replacement looks like. I'd want three things on the table quickly: the full list of affected assemblies and firmware touchpoints, a shortlist of candidate replacements with their differences from the original (electrical, mechanical, timing, toolchain support), and a rough effort estimate for each path. The key leadership decision is sequencing — whether hardware and firmware can work in parallel against an agreed interface contract, or whether one has to lead. I'd push for parallel work with a written interface definition so neither team is blocked waiting on the other, and I'd make the trade-offs explicit: a drop-in-ish part that needs less redesign but may carry its own qualification burden, versus a cleaner part that costs more schedule. I'd also flag early whether the change triggers re-verification or regulatory impact, because that often dominates the schedule more than the engineering itself. Throughout, I'd keep the two teams reporting against the same milestone dates and surface slippage the moment it appears rather than at the next gate.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to absorb the schedule slip or escalate it to program management? What would you do if the firmware team argues the hardware change is unnecessary and the old part can be sourced gray-market?

## Q2: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is a respected senior engineer, the technical content is genuinely strong, but the review has quietly become a formality — reviewers defer to the presenter and no one is actually probing the risky parts?

**Answer:** The problem here isn't the presenter's competence, it's the social dynamic — deference has replaced scrutiny, and a review that doesn't probe is worse than no review because it creates false confidence. I'd change the structure rather than lecture people about speaking up. Concretely: assign specific reviewers to specific risk areas ahead of time, so "someone should look at the thermal margin" becomes "you own the thermal margin and I need your verdict." I'd circulate the package in advance with a short list of the two or three decisions I most want challenged, and I'd ask the presenter — privately, respectfully — to explicitly flag the areas where they're least certain, which gives everyone else permission to dig in. During the review I'd model the behavior by asking genuine questions about the highest-risk items first, and I'd redirect vague approval ("looks good") into a specific question. If a reviewer raises something real, I'd make sure it gets captured as an action item with an owner, so raising a concern visibly leads to something happening rather than evaporating. Over time the goal is to make critical engagement the norm and deference the exception.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the senior engineer takes the added scrutiny as a sign you don't trust their work? How do you keep this from turning into performative questioning for its own sake?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between a hardware engineer and a firmware engineer about where a safety function should live — in hardware or in software — should be resolved by the two of them, escalated, or decided by you as lead?

**Answer:** I'd first separate the *technical* question from the *process* question. Technically, the right frame is usually a trade-off table: response latency, determinism, independence from the main processor, testability, verification burden, and regulatory precedent. A safety function implemented purely in software on the same processor that runs the application has an independence problem that a hardware path or a separate supervisory device doesn't — that's often the crux, and it's decidable with evidence rather than opinion. So my first step is to ask both engineers to bring their case against the same criteria, and if the data is genuinely ambiguous, propose a small prototype or bench measurement to settle it. On process: if the two of them can converge once the criteria are shared, it stays with them — that's the healthy outcome. I'd escalate or decide myself only if the disagreement has become about something other than the technical merits (ownership, ego, history), or if the decision has schedule or regulatory consequences that exceed their authority to commit. If I do decide, I'd document the rationale and the dissenting view so the decision is traceable and revisitable if new evidence appears. The worst outcome is letting it drift unresolved while the timeline quietly absorbs the cost.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer whose approach you didn't choose becomes disengaged? What documentation would you want so this decision survives an audit?

## Q4: How would you approach building a culture where engineers proactively flag risks early, on a team that has learned from experience that raising a concern early tends to get the messenger handed extra work or blamed when things go wrong?

**Answer:** That learned behavior is rational given the team's history, so I wouldn't try to talk them out of it — I'd change the incentives until the evidence contradicts the belief. The most important lever is what happens *after* someone raises a concern: if flagging a risk reliably results in the flagger being volunteered to fix it alone, or being associated with the problem in later retrospectives, the behavior will stop no matter what I say. So I'd deliberately separate "raising" from "owning" — a concern can be logged and triaged without automatically becoming that person's problem, and I'd make sure credit for early detection is visible and explicit. I'd also make the channel low-friction and low-stakes: a running risk log where anyone can add an item without it being a formal escalation, reviewed on a regular cadence, so surfacing something early is normal housekeeping rather than a dramatic act. When a risk turns out to be real, I'd publicly note that it was caught early and what that saved — reinforcing the behavior with outcomes, not slogans. And I'd watch my own reactions: if I visibly sigh or get tense when someone raises a problem, that teaches the team faster than any process. The test of whether it's working is whether concerns start arriving earlier and smaller over time.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do the first time someone raises a concern that turns out to be a false alarm — how do you avoid punishing that? How do you distinguish healthy risk-surfacing from a team that escalates everything to avoid ownership?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when morale is low and the room is inclined to blame one or two individual contributors rather than examine how the project was set up?

**Answer:** I'd set the frame before the meeting, because a post-mortem that turns into a trial produces defensiveness and no learning. The opening statement matters: we're here to understand the system that produced this outcome, not to assign personal fault, and the goal is changes we can actually make next time. I'd structure it around a timeline of decisions and conditions rather than a list of people — when were estimates made, what information was available then, what pressures existed, where did assumptions go unchallenged. Blame tends to collapse into "so-and-so was slow," and the useful question underneath that is usually about workload, unclear requirements, missing review gates, or a schedule that was never realistic. I'd use a structured method — a timeline plus a "five whys" or fishbone pass on the biggest slips — to keep it systematic. I'd also make sure the individual contributors most likely to be scapegoated get to speak early and are heard, because if they shut down the whole exercise loses its most valuable data. Output is a small set of concrete, owned, systemic actions with dates, plus an explicit note of what we're *not* changing. And I'd follow up on those actions, because a post-mortem whose recommendations quietly die teaches the team that the exercise was theater.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if senior leadership wants a name attached to the failure? What would you do if the same systemic issue shows up in the post-mortem that was flagged — and ignored — a year earlier?
