# behavioral-leadership — Day 54

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where a senior engineer's proposed architecture is technically sound, but the documentation package is too thin for reviewers to evaluate the safety-critical portions — and the engineer is likely to take a request for more detail as a personal criticism?
**Answer:** The first move is to separate the technical quality of the design from the completeness of the evidence package, and to say so explicitly and early. A useful framing is that the review's job is not to judge the engineer but to confirm that a reviewer who wasn't in the room could reach the same conclusions — and that's a documentation problem, not a competence problem. I'd open by acknowledging the strengths of the architecture so the engineer knows the substance is respected, then reframe the gap as a risk to the project rather than a criticism of the person: if the safety-critical rationale isn't written down, the design can't be defended in a regulatory audit, and the engineer's own reasoning gets lost if they're ever unavailable. Concretely, I'd avoid vague requests like "add more detail" — that reads as a judgment. Instead I'd name the specific artifacts needed: the fault-tree or risk-control rationale for each safety function, the derating and worst-case calculations, the interface assumptions the design depends on. Turning it into a checklist makes it objective and finite, which lowers the emotional temperature. I'd also offer to help draft the structure or pair on the first section, so the ask feels collaborative rather than punitive. If the engineer still resists, I'd keep the focus on the deliverable and the standard, not the person, and let the review record show the action items and owners. The goal is a reviewable package, and the fastest path there is usually to make the request specific, bounded, and clearly about the design's defensibility rather than the designer's worth.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer agrees the documentation is thin but argues there's no time to fix it before the review? What would you do if other reviewers start piling on and the session turns into a pile-on rather than a focused gap list?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?
**Answer:** I'd treat escalation as a tool with a cost, not a default and not a failure. The first question is whether the disagreement is actually about facts or about values and priorities. If it's factual — two different predictions about thermal behavior, timing margin, or failure rate — it's usually resolvable inside the team with data: a bench measurement, a simulation, a prototype, or a targeted test. Escalating a factual question before doing that work just moves the argument up a level without resolving it. If it's about priorities — cost versus schedule versus risk tolerance — that's often genuinely above the team's pay grade, because it requires authority over resources or risk acceptance that the team doesn't hold. That's a legitimate escalation. I'd also weigh reversibility and blast radius: a decision that's cheap to change later can be made by the team and revisited; a decision that locks in tooling, a safety architecture, or a long-lead component probably warrants a broader owner. Before escalating, I'd make sure the disagreement is crisply framed — what each option costs, what each assumes, what evidence would change each engineer's mind — so the manager is deciding between two understood options rather than refereeing a personality clash. I'd also set a time box: if the team can't converge within a defined window, escalate with a clear recommendation rather than a bare question. And I'd tell both engineers the plan in advance, so escalation doesn't feel like going behind anyone's back. The test I use: can the team make this call and own the consequences, or does it need someone with authority the team doesn't have? If the former, resolve it; if the latter, escalate early and with a recommendation attached.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if you escalate and your manager simply picks the option you disagreed with? How would you keep the two engineers working productively together after a decision one of them strongly opposed?

## Q3: How would you approach handling a situation where a team member consistently delivers high-quality technical work but has a pattern of missing internal deadlines, causing downstream delays for the rest of the team?
**Answer:** I'd start by getting curious about the mechanism rather than the symptom, because "misses deadlines" is a description, not a diagnosis. The likely causes are quite different and call for different responses: the person may be underestimating tasks, may be over-polishing work that's already good enough, may be silently absorbing blockers, or may be deprioritizing internal dates because they don't see them as real. I'd have a private, non-accusatory conversation framed around the pattern and its downstream effects, and I'd bring specific examples rather than a general complaint — vague feedback is easy to deflect. A key question is whether the estimates are the problem or the execution is. If it's estimation, the fix is structural: break work into smaller increments, add explicit checkpoints, and have the person flag slippage early rather than at the deadline. If it's perfectionism, the fix is agreeing on a definition of "done" for internal milestones so good-enough work can actually ship. If it's hidden blockers, the fix is a standing short check-in so problems surface in days, not weeks. I'd also make the dependency visible: show the person the downstream impact of a late handoff, because engineers often don't see how their internal date feeds someone else's critical path. Throughout, I'd keep the tone that the technical quality is valued and the goal is predictability, not punishment. Then I'd follow up on a defined cadence to see whether the pattern changes, and adjust. If it doesn't improve after clear, supported feedback, that becomes a performance-management conversation — but that's the last step, not the first.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between someone who's genuinely overloaded and someone who's chronically underestimating? What would you do if the person is a key technical contributor and the team can't afford to lose them?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than systemic issues?
**Answer:** The single most important thing is to set the frame before the meeting starts: a post-mortem is about making the system more reliable, not about finding who to blame. If that frame isn't established explicitly, the session will default to finger-pointing and people will withhold the very information that makes the analysis useful. I'd structure it around the timeline and the decisions, not the people — reconstruct what was known, when it was known, and what options were visible at each point. That reframing turns "who dropped the ball" into "what made the ball easy to drop," which is a systemic question. I'd use a structured method — a timeline, then contributing factors grouped into categories like estimation, requirements churn, dependencies, resourcing, and decision latency — so the discussion is organized rather than free-floating grievance. I'd explicitly separate the trigger (the immediate cause of the slip) from the underlying conditions that allowed it, because teams often stop at the trigger. I'd also model the behavior I want: name my own contribution to the outcome first, and treat every factor as a hypothesis to be examined rather than a verdict. On morale, I'd acknowledge the disappointment honestly rather than pretending it's fine, and I'd make sure the output is a small set of concrete, owned, systemic changes rather than a long list of vague lessons. Finally, I'd follow up on those actions in later reviews, because a post-mortem whose recommendations quietly die reinforces the cynicism that made the session hard in the first place.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a senior leader insists on naming an individual as the cause? How would you keep the post-mortem from becoming a venue for unrelated grievances?

## Q5: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each give different estimates and there's no historical data from similar projects?
**Answer:** With no historical data, the honest starting point is that every estimate is a guess, and the goal isn't a precise date — it's a shared understanding of the dependencies, the unknowns, and the range. I'd begin by mapping the critical path and the handoffs rather than the durations: hardware bring-up feeding firmware integration, design freeze feeding verification, verification feeding the regulatory submission. Most schedule pain in medical device work lives in the interfaces between functions, not inside any one function's estimate. Then I'd decompose each function's work into smaller pieces and separate the well-understood work from the genuinely uncertain work — the latter is where I'd apply ranges rather than single points, and where I'd look for the earliest cheap test that could collapse the uncertainty. I'd make the assumptions explicit and visible: what each team is assuming about the others' deliverables, and what happens to the schedule if those assumptions are wrong. Given the absence of history, I'd build in explicit buffers at the integration points rather than padding every task, and I'd treat the first real integration milestone as a calibration event that updates the rest of the plan. I'd also communicate the timeline as a range with named risks rather than a single confident date, because overpromising on a guess is how credibility gets spent. Finally, I'd set a cadence to revisit the plan as real data arrives, so the schedule evolves deliberately instead of being quietly abandoned.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you respond if leadership demands a single committed date despite the uncertainty? How would you decide where to place buffers without the buffers becoming hidden padding that everyone learns to game?
