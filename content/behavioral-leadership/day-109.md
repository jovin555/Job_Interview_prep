# behavioral-leadership — Day 109

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically strong, but the safety-critical rationale is scattered across appendices and side documents — and reviewers are likely to approve without ever engaging with it?
**Answer:** The core problem is a mismatch between where the risk lives and where the reviewers' attention goes, so I'd fix the structure before the meeting rather than scold people for skimming. First, I'd ask the presenter to add a short "safety-critical decisions" summary at the front of the package — one line per decision, the chosen option, the rejected alternative, and a pointer to the supporting calculation. That converts buried content into a checklist reviewers can walk through in the first ten minutes. Second, I'd change the review protocol: instead of a free-form read-through, I'd assign each safety-critical item to a named reviewer in advance and ask them to state, out loud, whether they're satisfied and why. Approval becomes an active act rather than a default. Third, I'd capture in the minutes that each item was explicitly reviewed, so the record shows engagement rather than mere attendance. If the pattern recurs across presenters, that's a signal the review template itself is wrong — the fix is to make the safety summary a required first section, not an optional appendix. The underlying principle is that review effectiveness is a design property of the review process, not a function of how diligent individuals happen to be on the day.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the presenter pushed back, arguing the summary duplicates content already in the appendices? How would you handle a reviewer who says "I trust the presenter, I don't need to re-derive the calculation"?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is technically competent but they consistently optimize for the wrong objective — chasing measurement precision in an area where robustness and manufacturability matter far more?
**Answer:** I'd start by checking whether this is a misunderstanding of priorities or a genuine values mismatch, because the interventions differ. Often it's the former: the engineer has never been told explicitly what "good" looks like for this subsystem, so they've defaulted to the metric they find most intellectually satisfying. The fix is to make the real objective concrete and visible — for example, stating that a design must survive a defined tolerance stack-up and assembly variation, and that exceeding the precision target buys nothing if it costs adjustability or adds a trim step in production. I'd walk through the trade-off with them on their own design, using the actual cost and yield implications rather than abstract advice, because engineers respond to evidence. I'd also give them a role in the constraint-setting: ask them to propose the acceptance criteria for their next block, then review those criteria together. That builds the habit of asking "what does this design actually need to do?" before "how precisely can I make it do it?" If the pattern persists after that, I'd make it an explicit expectation in their goals and follow up in one-to-ones, framing it as a growth area rather than a fault. What I'd avoid is silently reworking their designs, which teaches nothing and breeds resentment.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you tell the difference between over-engineering and a legitimate concern you're dismissing too quickly? What if the engineer argues that robustness and precision aren't actually in tension here?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether to absorb a small schedule slip quietly within the team or escalate it, when you suspect the slip is the leading edge of a larger problem?
**Answer:** My default is to escalate early but calibrate the message to the uncertainty, because a small slip that's genuinely contained is cheap to absorb, while one that's the visible tip of an underestimated workstream is very expensive to discover late. The deciding question isn't the size of the slip — it's whether I can explain *why* it happened and what it implies for the remaining work. If the cause is understood and bounded (a vendor lead time, a single re-spin), absorbing it with a note in the risk log is reasonable. If I can't articulate the cause, or if the same category of task has slipped before, that's evidence the estimate was wrong at the root, and I'd escalate with that framing: not "we're three days late" but "this slip suggests our estimate for this class of work is optimistic, and here's the range of impact if that holds across the remaining items." I'd bring a proposed response — re-plan, de-scope, or add resource — rather than just the problem. The failure mode I want to avoid is the quiet absorption that compounds: three small slips absorbed silently become one large surprise that destroys credibility when it finally surfaces. Escalating with a clear "here's what I know, here's what I don't, here's what I recommend" preserves trust far better than a clean-looking status report that turns out to be wrong.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you escalate a suspected systemic slip without alarming stakeholders if it turns out to be contained after all? What signals would tell you a slip is definitely not the leading edge of something larger?

## Q4: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across hardware, firmware, and verification teams whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?
**Answer:** The divergence usually isn't laziness — each team has a locally rational definition that optimizes for its own handoff, and the gaps only appear at the seams. So I'd make the seams explicit early. Concretely, I'd run a short workshop at project kickoff where each team states what it needs from the others to consider its own work complete, and we write those as a shared exit-criteria list per phase — for example, what "hardware design complete" must include before firmware can rely on it (frozen pinout, documented register map, known errata), and what "firmware feature complete" must include before verification can test it (defined interfaces, logging, a way to inject faults). I'd attach each criterion to a phase gate so it's checked at the gate rather than discovered at integration. I'd also insist on a joint integration milestone early — even a crude one — because a shared definition of done is much easier to agree on when there's a concrete artifact everyone has already touched. Finally, I'd revisit the criteria at each gate, since "done" legitimately evolves; the goal isn't a frozen document but a shared, current understanding. The measure of success is that disagreements about completeness happen in a scheduled review with a checklist, not in a late-night integration session.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a team that agrees to the criteria in the workshop but quietly doesn't follow them? What's the minimum viable version of this if the project is already underway and the divergence has started?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when two senior engineers disagree on a hardware-versus-software implementation of the same safety function — say, a watchdog — and the disagreement is blocking the timeline?
**Answer:** First I'd reframe the disagreement from "which engineer is right" to "what does the safety function actually need to guarantee," because hardware-versus-software arguments usually conflate several requirements — independence, diagnosability, response time, and failure modes — that the two options satisfy differently. I'd get both engineers to write down, briefly, the failure modes their approach protects against and the ones it doesn't, plus the assumptions each makes about the other (a software watchdog assumes the clock and the supervisory path are healthy; a hardware supervisor assumes its own supply and reset logic are sound). That usually reveals the disagreement is narrower than it appeared, and sometimes that the two approaches are complementary rather than exclusive. Then I'd anchor the decision in the risk analysis: which failure modes are highest severity, and which option covers them with the least residual risk and the least verification burden. If it's still genuinely balanced, I'd time-box a small prototype or a fault-injection test to generate data rather than continue arguing in the abstract. I'd make the decision explicit, document the rationale and the rejected option in the design record, and — importantly — make sure both engineers see their concerns reflected in the record, so the losing side isn't demoralized. The timeline unblocks because the decision has a defensible basis, not because I picked a side.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the losing engineer remained convinced the decision was wrong and kept raising it? How would you decide if the two approaches should both be implemented for redundancy rather than choosing one?
