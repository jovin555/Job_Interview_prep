# behavioral-leadership — Day 11

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design review when you know that a senior engineer on the team has a strong personal attachment to a particular architecture choice, and the review is likely to become contentious rather than objective?

**Answer:** I'd start by setting the frame for the review explicitly — the goal is to evaluate the design against requirements, risk, and manufacturability, not to validate anyone's preferred approach. Before the meeting, I'd talk to the senior engineer privately to understand their rationale and acknowledge the work they've put in, so they don't feel ambushed. During the review, I'd structure the discussion around decision criteria rather than personalities: what are the requirements, what are the trade-offs, what evidence do we have for each option? If the conversation starts to become adversarial, I'd pause and redirect to the data — for example, asking "what would we need to see to be confident that option B is better?" rather than letting it become a debate about who's right. I'd also make sure action items are captured with owners and due dates, and that the decision rationale is documented, so the outcome doesn't depend on who argued most forcefully. If the disagreement is genuinely close, I'd suggest a small, time-boxed prototyping effort to generate data rather than trying to settle it by discussion alone.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the senior engineer refuses to accept the group's decision after the review? What if the disagreement is between two senior engineers with equal authority and the rest of the team is deferring to them?

## Q2: How would you approach building a technical mentoring relationship with a junior engineer who is technically capable but lacks confidence in their design decisions, causing them to seek approval for every minor choice?

**Answer:** The goal is to shift them from seeking permission to making sound decisions with appropriate escalation. I'd start by understanding what's driving the lack of confidence — is it fear of making a mistake, lack of familiarity with the design standards, or uncertainty about what level of decision requires review? I'd then create a structured framework for decision-making: for example, a simple triage of "decide and inform," "decide and confirm," and "escalate before deciding," based on risk and reversibility. I'd walk through real examples from the current project and categorize them together, so they can see that many decisions don't need approval. I'd also make a point of asking for their recommendation before giving mine, so they practice forming a position. When they do make a decision independently, I'd give specific feedback — not just "that's fine," but "I think that was a good call because you considered X and Y; next time you might also check Z." Over time, I'd gradually increase the complexity of decisions they handle on their own, and I'd be explicit that making a wrong call on a low-risk decision is a learning opportunity, not a failure.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the junior engineer makes a decision independently that turns out to be wrong and causes rework? How would you adapt your approach if the engineer's lack of confidence is actually masking a knowledge gap in a specific area?

## Q3: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a firmware timing bug or a hardware noise problem, and the two teams are already pointing fingers at each other?

**Answer:** The first priority is to contain the situation — ensure patient safety and stop any further field exposure while the investigation is underway. Then I'd frame the investigation as a joint effort, not a blame-finding exercise. I'd bring the firmware and hardware leads together and establish ground rules: we're looking for root cause, not assigning fault, and we'll use data to drive the investigation. I'd start by gathering all available evidence — field reports, logs, waveforms, environmental conditions — and build a timeline of when the issue occurs. Rather than asking "is it firmware or hardware?" I'd ask "what conditions are present when the fault occurs, and what changes in those conditions?" That reframing often breaks the deadlock. I'd then design experiments that can discriminate between the hypotheses — for example, adding instrumentation to monitor the bus lines while running the firmware in a loop, or varying the firmware timing while holding the hardware constant. If the teams still disagree, I'd bring in a neutral third party with relevant expertise to review the evidence. Throughout, I'd document everything, because in a medical device context, the investigation itself becomes part of the design history file and may be needed for regulatory review.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the evidence initially points to one team's area, but that team is defensive and resists accepting the finding? How would you decide when to escalate the investigation to a formal 8D process versus handling it informally?

## Q4: How would you approach communicating a technical schedule risk to non-technical stakeholders, such as product management or executives, when the risk is based on engineering uncertainty rather than a specific known problem?

**Answer:** The key is to translate engineering uncertainty into business language without either overstating or understating it. I'd start by framing the risk in terms of outcomes, not activities — for example, "there's a chance the sensor calibration will take longer than planned because the accuracy requirement is tighter than what we've validated before," rather than "we might have issues with the ADC." I'd also give a range, not a single point: "the most likely outcome is a two-week delay, but it could be as much as six weeks if we need to redesign the analog front end." I'd be explicit about what's driving the uncertainty — is it a new technology, a new vendor, a requirement that changed late? — and what we're doing to reduce it, such as running a feasibility prototype or ordering long-lead parts early. I'd also propose mitigation options with their costs, so stakeholders can make an informed trade-off rather than just receiving bad news. Finally, I'd commit to a specific date for when we'll have more certainty, and I'd update them proactively rather than waiting for the next scheduled status meeting.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a stakeholder pushes back and demands a firm commitment despite the uncertainty? What if the risk is in an area where you have limited personal expertise, such as regulatory approval timelines?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture of structured root-cause analysis on a team that currently treats investigations as a quick fix-and-move-on exercise, without documenting the analysis or verifying that the fix actually addresses the root cause?

**Answer:** I'd start by making the value visible — not by mandating process, but by picking a recent recurring issue and walking the team through a structured analysis, showing how it reveals contributing factors that the quick-fix approach missed. I'd use a simple framework like 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram, and I'd keep it lightweight so it doesn't feel like bureaucracy. The key is to show that the structured approach actually saves time in the long run, because it prevents the same issue from coming back. I'd also introduce a simple template for documenting investigations — problem statement, timeline, root cause analysis, corrective action, verification of effectiveness — and make it part of the existing workflow rather than an additional step. I'd pair this with a review of past incidents where the quick fix didn't hold, to demonstrate the cost of skipping the analysis. Over time, I'd recognize and highlight examples where the structured approach caught something that would have been missed. I'd also be careful not to make it punitive — the goal is learning, not blame, and that has to be modeled consistently by leadership.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a team member resists the structured approach because they believe they already know the root cause and the process feels like overhead? How would you measure whether the culture change is actually taking hold?
