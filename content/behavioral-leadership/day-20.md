## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a sensor hardware failure or a firmware configuration error, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd start by establishing a structured investigation framework before any conclusions are drawn, because the risk with dual-cause scenarios is that teams anchor on their preferred hypothesis early. The first step would be containment — ensuring patient safety isn't compromised while the investigation proceeds, which might mean a field notice or usage restriction depending on severity. Then I'd gather all available data systematically: device logs, firmware version history, sensor calibration records, environmental conditions at time of failure, and any error codes or flags the device recorded. Rather than debating which cause is more likely, I'd focus on what evidence would definitively distinguish between them. For a sensor hardware failure, I'd expect to see consistent failure patterns across different firmware versions or a correlation with physical stress indicators. For a firmware configuration error, I'd expect the issue to track with specific software versions or configuration parameters. I'd also look at the failure distribution — if it's isolated to specific units, that points toward hardware; if it's widespread across many units with the same firmware, that points toward configuration. I'd design experiments to test both hypotheses in parallel where possible, such as bench-testing returned units while also reviewing the firmware configuration logic. The key is documenting the investigation trail so that whatever conclusion emerges, the rationale is transparent and defensible. Once the root cause is identified, I'd implement the corrective action and, critically, verify its effectiveness over a defined period before closing the investigation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation if the evidence is genuinely ambiguous and you can't definitively rule out either cause? What level of evidence would you require before recommending a field corrective action?

## Q2: How would you approach building a technical leadership roadmap for a team that is transitioning from a single-product medical device focus to a multi-project portfolio, where engineers are used to deep specialization rather than cross-project flexibility?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a capability-building exercise rather than just a resource allocation problem. The first step would be assessing the current skill distribution across the team — who has depth in which areas, where the overlaps are, and where the critical single points of failure exist. In a single-product environment, engineers often develop deep but narrow expertise, so the transition to multi-project requires deliberately broadening capabilities. I'd start by mapping the technical requirements of the upcoming projects against the team's current skills to identify gaps and overlaps. Then I'd create a structured cross-training plan that pairs engineers with complementary strengths — for example, having a power design specialist work alongside a firmware engineer on a project that requires both, so each gains exposure to the other's domain. I'd also establish shared design guidelines and reusable intellectual property blocks that reduce the need for every engineer to be an expert in everything. For example, standardizing common circuit blocks or firmware modules across projects means engineers can work outside their specialty without starting from scratch. I'd also introduce a lightweight peer-review system where engineers from different projects review each other's work, which builds cross-project awareness and spreads knowledge organically. The roadmap would include milestones for when each engineer should be able to work independently on projects outside their original specialty, and I'd track progress through regular check-ins rather than formal assessments. The cultural piece is equally important — I'd need to shift the team's identity from "I own this subsystem" to "I contribute to multiple products," which requires recognizing and rewarding flexibility alongside depth.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle resistance from senior engineers who feel that being asked to work across multiple projects dilutes their expertise? What would you do if a critical project requires deep expertise that no one on the team currently has?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** I'd approach this by treating the test strategy as a risk mitigation exercise that addresses both teams' legitimate concerns rather than picking a side. The firmware team's goal of extended battery life is valuable, and the hardware team's concern about analog stability during wake-up is a real technical risk. The test strategy needs to characterize the wake-up transition behavior comprehensively. I'd start by defining the specific parameters that matter: settling time for the analog front-end after wake-up, voltage rail stability during the transition, reference voltage drift, and sensor reading accuracy during the first few milliseconds after wake-up. The test plan would include both bench-level characterization and automated repeated cycling tests — thousands of sleep-wake cycles to catch intermittent issues that might not appear in a few dozen cycles. I'd also design tests that stress the system under realistic conditions: varying battery voltage as it discharges, temperature extremes, and different sensor input ranges. The key is to establish acceptance criteria before running the tests, so there's no ambiguity about what constitutes acceptable performance. If the tests reveal issues, the strategy should include a structured path for resolution — whether that's adjusting the firmware wake-up sequence, adding hardware settling time, or modifying the power supply decoupling. I'd also recommend a phased approach: first characterize the behavior on the bench, then run extended cycling tests, and only then proceed to formal verification. This way, the test strategy itself becomes a tool for building consensus between the teams — it provides data that either validates the firmware approach or identifies specific changes needed.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific test equipment and measurement techniques would you use to characterize the wake-up transition? How would you determine the acceptable settling time threshold?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a technical review of a proposed architecture for a new medical device where the system architect has chosen a modular design with multiple microcontrollers communicating over CAN-FD, but several senior engineers believe a single high-performance processor would be simpler and more reliable?

**Answer:** I'd approach this by ensuring the review focuses on objective criteria rather than personal preferences or past experiences. Both architectures have legitimate trade-offs, and the decision should be driven by the specific requirements of the device, not by what the team is most comfortable with. I'd start by establishing a clear set of evaluation criteria based on the product requirements: reliability targets, safety certification requirements, power consumption, development timeline, manufacturing cost, and long-term maintainability. Then I'd structure the review so each architecture is evaluated against these criteria systematically. For the modular multi-MCU approach, I'd want to examine the CAN-FD communication reliability, the added complexity of inter-processor synchronization, and the potential for communication failures to impact safety functions. For the single-processor approach, I'd want to examine whether the processor can meet all the real-time requirements without resource contention, whether the thermal and power characteristics are acceptable, and whether the certification effort is actually simpler. I'd also consider the team's experience — if they have deep expertise in one architecture, that's a legitimate factor, but it shouldn't override technical requirements. I'd encourage the engineers who disagree to articulate their concerns as specific technical risks rather than general preferences. If the disagreement persists, I might suggest a prototyping exercise to validate the highest-risk assumptions of each approach. The decision should be documented with the rationale, including the alternatives considered and why they were rejected, which is particularly important for medical device development where design decisions need to be traceable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation if the prototyping exercise shows that both architectures are technically viable, but the team remains split? What criteria would you use to make the final decision?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers feel comfortable raising concerns about a design decision early, even when the decision was made by a senior person or a consensus of the team, and even when the concern is based on a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated technical argument?

**Answer:** I'd approach this by recognizing that the cultural problem is fundamentally about psychological safety, not about the technical validity of concerns. Engineers won't raise early concerns if they fear being dismissed, embarrassed, or penalized for questioning a decision. The first step is modeling the behavior I want to see — as a leader, I need to explicitly invite challenge to my own decisions and respond to it constructively, even when the concern turns out to be unfounded. I'd also establish a norm that "gut feelings" are legitimate starting points for investigation, not final arguments. When someone raises a concern based on intuition, the response should be to help them articulate what's driving that feeling — often there's a legitimate technical insight behind it that hasn't been fully formed yet. I'd create structured opportunities for early input, such as design reviews that explicitly include a "what concerns do you have?" segment, and I'd make it clear that raising a concern early is valued even if it doesn't change the decision. I'd also be careful about how concerns are handled when they're raised — if someone raises a concern and it's dismissed without genuine consideration, that sends a strong signal that discourages future input. When a concern does lead to a design change, I'd make sure the person who raised it gets recognition. When it doesn't, I'd make sure they understand why the decision was maintained, so they see that their input was genuinely considered rather than ignored. Over time, this builds a culture where raising concerns early becomes normal and expected, rather than risky.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where an engineer repeatedly raises concerns that turn out to be unfounded, and the team starts to view them as a nuisance? How would you balance encouraging open input with maintaining decision-making efficiency?
