# behavioral-leadership — Day 28

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a sensor hardware failure or a firmware filtering algorithm error, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd start by framing the investigation around evidence rather than hypothesis ownership. The first step is containment — ensuring patient safety isn't at risk while the investigation proceeds, which might mean a field advisory or usage restriction depending on severity. Then I'd structure the investigation using a formal methodology like 8D or a fishbone diagram, but critically, I'd separate the two candidate root causes into parallel workstreams rather than letting the team argue about which is more likely.

For the sensor hardware path, I'd want to examine returned units, look at failure patterns across serial numbers and manufacturing lots, and check whether the failures correlate with environmental factors like temperature or mechanical stress. For the firmware path, I'd review the filtering algorithm's behavior at boundary conditions — what happens with rapid signal changes, what the settling time is, whether there are any edge cases in the ADC sampling sequence. The key is to identify discriminating tests: what data would definitively point to one cause over the other? For example, if the sensor hardware is at fault, you'd expect to see raw ADC values that are physically implausible before filtering. If the firmware is at fault, the raw data might look perfectly reasonable but the processed output is wrong.

I'd also make sure both teams contribute to a shared fault tree rather than defending their own area. The goal isn't to assign blame but to find the actual root cause, and sometimes the answer is that both contribute — a marginal sensor signal combined with a filter that doesn't handle that particular waveform. Once the root cause is confirmed, the corrective action needs to address the systemic issue, not just the immediate symptom, and I'd want to verify the fix under conditions that reproduce the original failure.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the two teams disagree on what constitutes sufficient evidence to rule out their respective causes? What if the failure is intermittent and you can't reproduce it in the lab?

## Q2: How would you approach building a technical leadership roadmap for a team that is transitioning from a single-product medical device focus to a multi-project portfolio, where engineers are used to deep specialization rather than cross-project flexibility?

**Answer:** The core challenge here is shifting from a "one product, one team" mindset to a model where engineers need to contribute across multiple projects without losing the depth that made them effective in the first place. I'd start by mapping the current skill set against the anticipated project portfolio — not just technical skills but also familiarity with different regulatory pathways, communication protocols, and design constraints. Some engineers will naturally adapt to breadth, while others will remain deep specialists who are shared across projects as consultants.

I'd structure the roadmap around three layers. First, a technical foundation layer: establishing common design standards, reusable building blocks, and documentation templates so that moving between projects doesn't mean relearning everything from scratch. Second, a project allocation strategy: rather than assigning engineers to projects for their entire duration, I'd look at phased involvement — someone might be deep in one project during its design phase, then shift to a lighter advisory role while picking up another project in its early definition phase. Third, a knowledge-sharing mechanism: regular technical forums where engineers present lessons learned from their current project, so institutional knowledge doesn't stay siloed.

The leadership aspect is managing the transition period where engineers may feel anxious about losing their specialization. I'd be transparent about the fact that depth isn't being abandoned — it's being leveraged differently. I'd also watch for the risk of overloading people during the transition; there's a natural tendency to ask the most capable people to do everything, which leads to burnout. The roadmap should include explicit capacity planning and a phased ramp-up rather than expecting everyone to be fully cross-functional overnight.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you identify which engineers are best suited for breadth versus depth? How would you handle an engineer who resists the transition and wants to stay focused on a single product?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** This is a classic case where both teams are raising legitimate concerns, and the test strategy needs to address both the opportunity and the risk. I'd start by defining what success looks like for each side: the firmware team wants to demonstrate that the sleep mode achieves the battery life target without compromising functionality, and the hardware team wants assurance that sensor readings are accurate and stable after wake-up. The test strategy should be designed to answer both questions with data.

I'd structure the testing in phases. First, a characterization phase on the bench: measure the actual current draw in sleep mode, the wake-up time, and the settling behavior of the analog front-end. This isn't pass/fail testing — it's gathering data to understand the system's real behavior. Key parameters would include the time from wake-up trigger to stable sensor readings, any transient glitches on the power rails during the transition, and whether the sensor's reference voltage or bias circuitry settles within the required accuracy window.

Second, a stress testing phase that exercises the worst-case scenarios: frequent wake-sleep cycles, wake-ups during active sensor sampling, and operation across the temperature range. The concern about analog stability often only appears under specific conditions — for example, if the sleep mode disables the sensor's bias circuitry, the wake-up transient might cause a settling error that's acceptable for some measurements but not for others. I'd want to map the envelope of conditions where the sleep mode is safe to use.

Third, I'd design the verification tests to include realistic usage patterns, not just worst-case scenarios. If the device wakes up every few minutes to take a measurement, the test should reflect that pattern. I'd also want to see a clear specification for what "stable" means — is it a percentage of full-scale accuracy, a settling time, or both? Without that agreement upfront, the test results will be subject to interpretation. Finally, I'd make sure the test strategy includes a fallback position: if the sleep mode proves problematic, what's the alternative? Having a plan B prevents the project from stalling while the teams debate.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the bench characterization shows that the sensor takes longer to stabilize than the firmware's wake-up sequence allows? What if the battery life target can only be met with the sleep mode, but the stability issue can't be fully resolved?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a technical review of a proposed architecture for a new medical device where the system architect has chosen a modular design with multiple microcontrollers communicating over CAN-FD, but several senior engineers believe a single high-performance processor would be simpler and more reliable?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the technical debate from the interpersonal dynamics. The architect has proposed a specific solution, and the senior engineers have concerns — both perspectives need to be heard on their merits. I'd structure the review so that each side presents their case with specific criteria: reliability, development effort, testability, regulatory risk, power consumption, and long-term maintainability. The goal isn't to declare a winner but to understand the trade-offs clearly.

For the modular multi-MCU approach, the arguments typically center on fault isolation, independent development streams, and the ability to use smaller, lower-power processors where appropriate. The concerns usually involve inter-processor communication reliability, synchronization complexity, and the increased difficulty of system-level testing. For the single-processor approach, the arguments center on simplicity, fewer points of failure, and easier debugging — but the trade-offs include higher power consumption, more complex software partitioning, and the risk that a single fault affects everything.

I'd push the discussion toward data. What are the actual requirements for processing throughput, latency, and power? Are there hard real-time constraints that would be difficult to meet on a single processor? What's the team's experience with each architecture? If the team has deep experience with single-processor designs but limited CAN-FD experience, that's a real risk factor that should be weighed. I'd also ask for a prototype or proof-of-concept to resolve the most contentious unknowns — for example, if the concern is CAN-FD timing under load, a small bench test could answer that question definitively.

The decision should be documented with the rationale, including the alternatives considered and why they were rejected. If the team remains split after the technical discussion, I'd look for a way to make the decision based on the strongest evidence rather than the loudest voice. Sometimes that means escalating to a higher-level technical authority or running a more detailed feasibility study. The key is to ensure the decision is made on technical merit, not on who argues most persuasively.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the architect is senior to the engineers raising concerns, and the discussion becomes about authority rather than technical merit? What if the prototype shows that both approaches are viable, and the decision comes down to team preference?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers feel comfortable raising concerns about a design decision early, even when the decision was made by a senior person or a consensus of the team, and even when the concern is based on a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated technical argument?

**Answer:** This is fundamentally about psychological safety and the norms around how technical decisions are made and revisited. I'd start by modeling the behavior myself — explicitly inviting challenge on my own decisions, thanking people who raise concerns even when they turn out to be unfounded, and making it clear that raising a concern early is always better than suppressing it until it becomes a problem.

The "gut feeling" aspect is important to address directly. A gut feeling in an experienced engineer is usually pattern recognition — they've seen something similar before and their brain is flagging it, even if they can't articulate the specific mechanism yet. I'd encourage engineers to voice these concerns with the framing "something about this doesn't sit right, and I want to figure out why" rather than requiring them to have a fully formed technical argument. The expectation isn't that the concern is automatically acted upon — it's that it gets investigated. Sometimes the investigation confirms the concern, sometimes it resolves it, but either way the process builds trust.

I'd also look at the structural incentives. If raising a concern leads to extra work, delays, or social friction, people will stop doing it. So I'd make sure that concerns are logged, investigated, and closed out with a clear outcome — even if the outcome is "we investigated and the design is sound." That documentation also serves the regulatory side, because it shows the design history includes consideration of alternatives and risks. Over time, the culture shifts from "don't question the decision" to "questioning is part of good engineering."

One practical mechanism is to build a "concerns register" as part of the design review process — a formal place where anyone can raise a concern, and it gets tracked to closure. This removes the burden of the person raising the concern having to drive the investigation themselves. It also creates a record that's valuable for audits and for future design reviews.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a junior engineer raises a concern that turns out to be based on a misunderstanding, and the senior engineer who made the decision responds dismissively? How would you balance the need for timely decisions with the desire to encourage thorough questioning?
