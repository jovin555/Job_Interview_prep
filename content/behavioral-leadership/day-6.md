# behavioral-leadership — Day 6

## Q1: How would you approach convincing a hardware team to adopt a new design-for-manufacturing (DFM) checklist when they see it as unnecessary overhead that will slow down their existing workflow?

**Answer:** I would start by understanding the team's resistance rather than pushing the checklist as a mandate. The concern about overhead is valid — any new process step takes time, and if the team doesn't see the value, they'll treat it as a checkbox exercise. I'd begin by reviewing recent manufacturing issues from the last few projects — assembly yield problems, component clearance violations, panelization issues — and quantify how much rework time those caused. Then I'd frame the checklist not as extra work, but as a way to *reduce* total work by catching problems before they reach manufacturing. I'd propose a lightweight pilot: take the next PCB layout and run it through the checklist as a parallel activity, timing how long it takes and comparing it to the time spent on a typical respin cycle. If the pilot shows that 15 minutes of checklist review saves days of layout revision and fab delays, that's a concrete argument. I'd also involve the team in building the checklist — ask them what recurring DFM issues they've seen and what checks they already do informally. When people contribute to creating a tool, they're more likely to use it. Finally, I'd make the checklist a living document that gets updated after each project, so it stays relevant and doesn't become stale paperwork.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a senior engineer who refuses to use the checklist even after the pilot shows positive results? What would you do if the manufacturing team reports that the checklist is missing critical items specific to their fab's capabilities?

## Q2: How would you approach leading a technical decision when two equally experienced engineers disagree on whether to use a hardware timer interrupt or a dedicated PWM peripheral for motor speed control in a medical device, and both approaches have valid trade-offs?

**Answer:** I'd first ensure both engineers have a structured forum to present their cases — not a debate, but a technical review where each explains their approach with specific reasoning about timing accuracy, CPU load, power consumption, failure modes, and code complexity. I'd ask them to focus on the *risk profile* of each approach for this specific application: a medical device with motor speed control. For example, a hardware timer interrupt might offer more flexibility but introduces jitter if other interrupts preempt it, while a dedicated PWM peripheral gives deterministic output but may have limited resolution or frequency range. I'd then look at what data we already have — datasheet specs for the microcontroller, any previous project experience with similar peripherals, and the motor's requirements for speed regulation accuracy. If the decision still isn't clear, I'd propose a small proof-of-concept: each engineer implements their approach on a development board, and we measure actual performance against requirements — timing accuracy under worst-case interrupt load, power draw, and code size. The data from that experiment becomes the decision basis. I'd document both approaches and the rationale for the chosen one in the design history file, including the test results. This way, even the engineer whose approach wasn't selected understands that the decision was evidence-based, not political.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the proof-of-concept shows both approaches meet requirements equally well — how do you break the tie? How would you handle the losing engineer feeling that their approach was unfairly dismissed?

## Q3: How would you approach managing a situation where a regulatory audit is scheduled in two weeks, and you discover that several key design verification test reports are missing signatures or have incomplete data that cannot be re-run in time?

**Answer:** First, I'd assess the scope of the problem — exactly which test reports are affected, what's missing (signatures, raw data, test conditions, pass/fail criteria), and whether any of the missing information can be reconstructed from lab notebooks, email approvals, or meeting minutes. I'd prioritize the reports that cover the most critical safety functions or the highest-risk failure modes from the risk management file. For each affected report, I'd determine the best path forward: some gaps might be fixable with a formal memo from the test engineer documenting what was done and why the original signature is missing; others might require a deviation or non-conformance report if the data is genuinely incomplete. I'd then communicate transparently with the regulatory team — not hiding the issue, but presenting it with a clear remediation plan and timeline. I'd also prepare a briefing for the audit team explaining what happened, what corrective actions we're taking, and how we'll prevent recurrence. During the audit itself, if the inspector asks about a specific report, I'd be honest about the gap and show the remediation documentation. Auditors generally respect transparency and a proactive corrective plan more than attempts to hide or downplay issues. After the audit, I'd implement a process change — such as a pre-audit checklist that verifies all documentation is complete before scheduling, and a sign-off gate at the end of each verification activity.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the missing data is for a test that showed a borderline pass, and you suspect the engineer might have omitted it intentionally? How would you handle an auditor who insists on seeing the original raw data even though it was lost in a server migration?

## Q4: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each have different estimates for how long their work will take, and there's no historical data from similar projects?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that without historical data, all estimates are uncertain — and that's okay as long as we're transparent about the uncertainty. I'd bring the leads from each team into a structured planning session where we break down the work into smaller, concrete deliverables rather than high-level phases. For example, instead of "hardware design — 4 months," we'd break it into schematic capture, PCB layout, prototype assembly, bring-up testing, and verification testing, each with its own estimate. For each sub-task, I'd ask the team to give three numbers: an optimistic estimate (everything goes perfectly), a realistic estimate (normal hiccups), and a pessimistic estimate (major issues). This gives us a range, not a single point. I'd then look for dependencies between teams — what does firmware need from hardware before they can start, and vice versa? Where can work happen in parallel? I'd build the timeline showing the critical path and identify where schedule risk is highest. I'd also build in buffer time at key integration points, not at the end of the project. When presenting the timeline to stakeholders, I'd show the range of possible completion dates based on the three-point estimates, not just the "realistic" number. This sets expectations that the timeline is a forecast, not a promise, and that we'll update it as we get real data from the first few milestones. I'd schedule regular check-ins to compare actual progress against estimates and adjust the timeline accordingly.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a product manager who insists on a single fixed delivery date despite your uncertainty ranges? What if the regulatory team's pessimistic estimate pushes the project timeline beyond what the business can accept?

## Q5: How would you approach handling a situation where a team member consistently delivers high-quality technical work but has a pattern of missing internal deadlines, causing downstream delays for the rest of the team?

**Answer:** I'd start with a private conversation to understand the root cause, not to assign blame. There are several possible reasons for missing deadlines: the engineer might be overcommitting because they're optimistic about their own speed, they might be spending too much time perfecting details that aren't critical, they might have unclear task boundaries, or there could be external blockers I'm not aware of. I'd ask open-ended questions like "What's been getting in the way of hitting the timeline on the last few tasks?" and "Is there anything I can do to help remove obstacles?" If the pattern is perfectionism — delivering excellent work but always late — I'd work with them to identify which aspects of their work are truly critical and which can be "good enough" for the internal milestone, with refinements added later. I'd also help them break tasks into smaller chunks with intermediate deadlines, so we can track progress earlier and adjust if they're falling behind. If the issue is overcommitment, I'd coach them on how to estimate more realistically and encourage them to push back when asked to take on too much. I'd also check whether the deadlines themselves are realistic — if everyone on the team is struggling with the same timeline pressure, the problem might be systemic, not individual. Finally, I'd set up a lightweight check-in process: a brief weekly sync where we review progress against the next milestone, so small delays get caught early rather than compounding into major downstream impacts. The goal is to support the engineer in developing better time management habits while protecting the team from cascading delays.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer acknowledges the problem but doesn't change their behavior after several coaching conversations? How would you balance supporting this engineer against the frustration of other team members who are consistently impacted by the delays?
