# behavioral-leadership — Day 108

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically sound, but the safety-critical calculations are embedded in a long appendix rather than surfaced in the main body — and reviewers are likely to approve without engaging with them?

**Answer:** The core problem is a review-structure problem, not a competence problem, so I'd treat it that way rather than turning it into a criticism of the presenter. Before the meeting, I'd ask the presenter to restructure the package so that the safety-critical decisions and their justifying calculations appear in the main body, with a short "decisions requiring reviewer scrutiny" section up front. The appendix can hold supporting derivations, but the load-bearing numbers need to be where reviewers will actually look. If the meeting is already scheduled and the package is fixed, I'd open the review by explicitly walking the room through the safety-critical items first, asking targeted questions ("what's the margin on this trace width, and where's the calculation?"), and recording each as an action item with an owner. The goal is to make silent approval uncomfortable — reviewers should have to affirmatively state they've seen and agree with the calculation, not just fail to object. Longer term, I'd push for a standard review-package template that requires a "safety-critical decisions" summary section, so this becomes structural rather than dependent on the presenter remembering. The key leadership move is framing it as "how do we make it easy for reviewers to do their job" rather than "you buried the important stuff."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the presenter pushed back, arguing that the calculations were always there and reviewers should have read the appendix? What would you do if a reviewer had already signed off on the package before the meeting?

## Q2: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across a hardware, firmware, and verification team whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?

**Answer:** The divergence usually isn't laziness — each discipline has a genuinely different notion of completeness, and the mismatch only surfaces when one team's "done" hands off to another team's "not started." I'd start by making the implicit definitions explicit: run a short workshop where each discipline writes down what "done" means for a given deliverable at each phase gate, then compare. The gaps are usually predictable — hardware considers a board "done" when it powers up and passes functional checks, firmware considers a module "done" when it runs on a bench setup, verification considers it "done" when it's been exercised against the requirements with documented evidence. Those are all reasonable and all different. From there I'd build a shared phase-gate checklist that names the exit criteria per discipline and, critically, the handoff artifacts — what evidence one team must produce before the next team can start. I'd anchor it to the requirements and traceability rather than to opinions, so "done" is defined against the spec, not against whoever's most senior. I'd also build in a lightweight mid-phase check rather than waiting for the gate, because the whole point is to catch the mismatch early. The leadership piece is getting each team to see the others' definition as legitimate rather than as an obstacle, and then committing to a single written standard everyone signs up to.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where verification insists on a stricter definition of done than the schedule can absorb? What would you do if the shared checklist started being treated as a box-ticking exercise rather than a real gate?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether to absorb a small schedule slip quietly within the team or escalate it, when you suspect the slip is the leading edge of a larger problem?

**Answer:** My default is to escalate early and frame it honestly, because a small slip that I suspect is symptomatic is exactly the kind of thing that becomes a large slip if it stays quiet. But "escalate" doesn't mean "raise alarm" — it means communicating the fact of the slip, the evidence that it might be symptomatic, and a concrete plan to determine whether it is. I'd want to do a quick diagnostic first so I'm not escalating a vague worry: is the slip caused by a one-off (someone was out sick, a part arrived late) or by something structural (an estimate that was always wrong, a dependency nobody mapped, a hidden integration problem)? If I can characterize it in a day or two, I'd bring that characterization to my manager rather than just the raw slip. The framing matters: "we're two days behind on X, and I think it's because Y, here's how I'm going to confirm that and what the range of outcomes looks like" is far more useful than either silent absorption or a bare "we're slipping." Absorbing quietly is only defensible if I'm confident it's genuinely a one-off and the buffer can take it — and even then I'd note it so the pattern is visible if it recurs. The thing I'd avoid is the trap where absorbing one small slip sets a precedent that makes the next one easier to absorb, until the aggregate is a problem nobody saw coming.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you communicate the slip to a non-technical stakeholder who's likely to hear "two days" and stop listening? What would you do if your manager told you to absorb it quietly because escalation would reflect badly on the team?

## Q4: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is technically competent but they consistently optimize for the wrong thing — chasing precision or elegance in an area where robustness and manufacturability matter far more?

**Answer:** This is usually a values mismatch rather than a skill gap, so the fix is about making the actual priorities visible and concrete rather than just telling them to care about different things. I'd start by getting specific about where the misalignment is showing up — not "you over-engineer," but "this regulator selection optimizes for output precision when the real constraint is that this part has to survive a five-year manufacturing life and a wide temperature range." Then I'd connect the optimization target back to the actual requirements and the actual failure modes: what does the product need to do, what does it need to survive, what does the manufacturing line need to be able to build repeatably. Precision and elegance are real virtues — they're just not the binding constraint here, and the engineer needs to see that the binding constraint is what gets optimized. I'd also try to give them ownership of a manufacturability or robustness problem directly, because the fastest way to internalize a priority is to be accountable for it. In design reviews I'd make the trade-off explicit and ask them to argue for their choice against the stated criteria, which either surfaces a good reason I hadn't considered or helps them see the mismatch themselves. Over time I'd want the team's design guidelines to encode these priorities so it's not a personal correction but a shared standard.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer argued that robustness and precision aren't actually in tension in this case, and they might be right? What would you do if the pattern persisted after several conversations?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when two senior engineers disagree on a hardware-versus-software approach to the same safety function — say, a watchdog implementation — and the disagreement is blocking the project timeline?

**Answer:** First I'd separate the technical disagreement from the schedule pressure, because deciding under a deadline tends to produce a decision that's fast rather than right, and for a safety function that's the wrong trade. I'd get both engineers to write down, independently, the failure modes their approach protects against, the failure modes it doesn't, the assumptions it depends on, and what evidence would change their mind. That usually does two things: it surfaces whether they're actually disagreeing about the same thing (often they're optimizing for different failure modes and both are partly right), and it gives me the material to make a decision on criteria rather than on seniority. Then I'd define the decision criteria explicitly — what failure modes are in scope per the risk analysis, what the regulatory and verification burden looks like for each, what the reliability and testability implications are, what the schedule and cost impact is. If the criteria still don't resolve it, I'd look for a cheap way to get data: a bench prototype, a fault-injection test, a review of field or test data from similar designs. If it genuinely can't be resolved by data in the time available, I'd make the call myself, document the rationale and the dissenting view, and set a checkpoint to revisit if new evidence appears. The important leadership behaviors are: don't let it become personal, don't let the deadline force a bad decision, make the criteria explicit so the decision is defensible, and make sure the engineer whose approach wasn't chosen knows their input was heard and recorded.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer whose approach you didn't choose remained visibly disengaged from implementing the chosen one? How would you document a decision like this so it holds up under a regulatory audit?
