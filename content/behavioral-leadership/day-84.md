# behavioral-leadership — Day 84

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct to either cancel the review or wave it through are both wrong — one stalls the milestone, the other defeats the purpose of the gate. I'd separate the review into two parts. First, run the review on what *is* reviewable: architecture, interfaces, component selection, layout intent, and anything not dependent on the missing calculations. Capture those decisions and action items so the meeting produces real value. Second, for the safety-critical item specifically, I would not let it pass on verbal assurance. I'd ask the presenter to walk through the reasoning live — what the calculation is meant to bound, what inputs it uses, what margin they believe they have — and record that as a provisional finding, explicitly flagged as "pending documented calculation." The milestone gate can then be marked conditionally passed with a hard, dated action item: the calculation must be submitted and independently checked before the next gate. That keeps the schedule honest without pretending the gap doesn't exist. I'd also follow up privately with the presenter afterward, because a late package with missing safety calculations usually signals either an overloaded engineer or a process problem, not laziness — and the fix for that is upstream of this meeting.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the milestone owner pushes back and says a conditional pass isn't acceptable, how would you handle that?
- How would you decide whether the missing calculation is a documentation gap or a sign the design itself is unverified?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures are the hardest case because the pressure to "just fix it" is highest exactly when the evidence is thinnest. My first move is to resist the urge to change anything until I've captured what the failure looks like — because the moment you start swapping parts or tweaking firmware, you destroy the state that might explain it. I'd treat it as a data-collection problem before a diagnosis problem: instrument the system to log as much as possible (supply rails, bus traffic, temperature, timing, firmware state at the moment of failure), and set up a way to run the failing condition repeatedly in the background while other work continues. In parallel, I'd build a fault tree — what are all the plausible mechanisms that could produce this symptom, and which ones are consistent with the observed conditions (temperature, load, time-in-service, batch)? That narrows the search space even without a reproduction. For containment, if the device is in the field or in test, I'd apply the most conservative safe action available — not a fix, but a guard — and document it as containment, not corrective action. Only once I have a mechanism I can explain and a way to reproduce it (even probabilistically) would I propose a corrective action, and I'd define up front what "verified effective" means: a specific test, run enough times to be statistically meaningful, that would have caught the original failure.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide when you've collected enough data to stop investigating and start fixing?
- What would you do if the intermittent failure rate is so low that you can't get statistical confidence before the ship date?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode on both sides is the same: the firmware team ends up either ignoring a real constraint or over-engineering around a phantom one. The way to avoid both is to translate the constraint into the *decision* it affects, not the physics behind it. So instead of saying "the ADC is 12-bit and the front end has X noise," I'd say: "at the sample rates you're planning, the effective resolution you can rely on is N bits, which means your filter needs to reject anything below this threshold, and you cannot distinguish changes smaller than this." That's a statement the firmware engineer can design against. I'd also give them the *why* at a level they can reason about — where the noise comes from, whether it's broadband or at a specific frequency, whether it changes with temperature or supply — because if they understand the mechanism, they can make better trade-offs than if I just hand them a number. And I'd make the constraint testable: agree on a measurement they can run on the bench to confirm their firmware is actually achieving the resolution they think it is. The goal isn't to make them analog experts; it's to give them enough of the model that they can tell when their assumptions break.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filter would add latency that the application can't tolerate?
- What would you do if, after your explanation, the firmware team still designs as if the full ADC resolution were available?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This is usually not a confidence problem — it's a habit problem, and habits change through practice, not encouragement. The engineer has learned that deferring is safe: someone else owns the outcome, so they can't be wrong. To break that, I'd give them ownership of something small but genuinely theirs — a subsystem, a trade-off study, a design decision with a bounded blast radius — and then *not* answer when they ask me what I'd do. Instead I'd ask them what they'd do, and why, and what would change their mind. That's uncomfortable at first, and I'd say so explicitly so they know it's intentional. I'd also make the reasoning visible: when they bring me a decision, I'd ask them to write down the options they considered, the criteria they used, and the trade-off they accepted. That document becomes both a learning artifact and a safety net — if the decision turns out wrong, we can look at the reasoning rather than the person. Over time, I'd widen the scope and reduce the check-ins. The measure of success isn't that they stop asking me questions; it's that they start asking themselves the questions I would have asked.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if the engineer's first independent decision goes badly — how do you keep them from retreating back to deferring?
- How would you handle it if the engineer's manager expects them to keep deferring to you because you're the senior person?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a disagreement turns personal, the technical merits are no longer the bottleneck — the process is. The first thing I'd do is stop trying to resolve it in the room. I'd separate the two sides, talk to each person individually, and try to surface what each is actually worried about. Often the personal edge is covering a real concern that hasn't been articulated: one side may be worried about schedule, the other about long-term maintainability, and neither has said so because the argument has been framed as "your architecture is wrong." Once I understand the underlying concerns, I'd reframe the decision around explicit criteria — cost, schedule, risk, testability, regulatory burden, team familiarity — and get both sides to agree on the criteria *before* scoring the options. That's the key move: it's much harder to argue that someone has bad motives when you've both agreed on what "good" means. If the criteria still don't produce a clear winner, I'd propose a bounded prototype or a paper study to generate data, with a deadline. And if it's still deadlocked after that, I'd make the call myself, document the rationale and the dissenting view, and be explicit that the decision is mine to own — not a verdict that one side was right and the other wrong. The goal isn't to make everyone agree; it's to get the team back to disagreeing about the work rather than about each other.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if one of the engineers continues to undermine the decision after it's made?
- At what point would you escalate this to your manager, and how would you frame it so it doesn't look like you couldn't manage the team?
