# behavioral-leadership — Day 67

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design review where the review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two problems, because they have different fixes. The stalling is a process problem; the routing-around is a trust problem, and the second one is more dangerous because it means the review has lost legitimacy in the team's eyes.

On the stalling itself, I'd look at whether the objections are genuinely new technical concerns or whether they're being generated by the format. A common cause is a review with no defined scope or exit criteria — if "done" is never defined, a determined reviewer can always find one more thing. So I'd tighten the review: state up front what decision this session is meant to produce, what's in scope and out of scope, and what evidence would close each open item. Objections that fall outside scope get logged as separate follow-ups rather than blocking the current decision. That's not silencing the reviewer — it's giving their concern a real home instead of letting it hold the whole review hostage.

I'd also talk to the reviewer one-on-one, outside the room. Sometimes the pattern is a genuine unresolved technical worry that they don't feel has been heard, and once it's acknowledged and assigned an owner, the stream of new objections dries up. Sometimes it's a communication style issue, and a private conversation about impact lands better than a public confrontation. Occasionally it's a real signal that the design isn't ready and the reviewer is the only one saying so — I'd want to rule that out before treating it as a behavior problem.

On the routing-around: I'd name it directly and without accusation. The team isn't being malicious; they're responding to a process that feels like it's blocking them. I'd acknowledge that the review has been inefficient, commit to the structural fixes, and be clear that bypassing review isn't acceptable for safety-relevant decisions — not because process for its own sake matters, but because undocumented decisions are exactly what creates problems later. The way to earn back compliance is to make the review worth attending.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the reviewer's objections turned out to be technically valid and the design genuinely wasn't ready?
- What would you do if the routing-around had already produced a decision that was later found to be wrong?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures under schedule pressure are where teams most often do damage — they ship a plausible fix, the symptom disappears for a while, and everyone assumes it's solved when the actual cause was never found. So the first thing I'd do is resist the pressure to treat "the symptom stopped" as "the problem is fixed."

I'd start with containment, kept explicitly separate from correction. Containment is whatever stops the harm in the field right now — a screening test, a firmware guard, a usage restriction — and it's understood by everyone to be temporary and not a root-cause fix. Naming it as containment matters, because it prevents the team from psychologically closing the issue.

For the investigation itself, when you can't reproduce on demand, the leverage is in instrumentation and data, not in staring harder at the bench. I'd want to capture the failure in the act: add logging, counters, or a capture buffer that records state leading up to the event, and get that instrumentation onto units that are actually failing. If the failure only shows up in the field, then the field is the lab. I'd also widen the net on variables — temperature, supply voltage, timing, specific firmware build, specific hardware revision, specific user sequence — because intermittent failures usually have a triggering condition that's rare but deterministic, not truly random.

I'd apply structured methods rather than free-form guessing: a fishbone to enumerate candidate causes across hardware, firmware, environment, and use, then 5 Whys on the most probable branches, and design of experiments to test hypotheses efficiently rather than one variable at a time. And I'd be honest with stakeholders about the difference between "we have a containment" and "we have a verified root cause," because those carry very different schedule implications.

The verification-of-effectiveness step is the one teams skip under pressure. Once a fix is in, I'd want a defined way to confirm the failure rate actually dropped — not just that nobody's complained recently.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide when you've done enough investigation to justify a corrective action, versus continuing to dig?
- How would you communicate to leadership that you have containment but not yet a root cause, without it sounding like you're stalling?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is real. Oversimplify and you say "the sensor is noisy, just filter it," and the firmware team picks a filter that either doesn't help or adds latency that breaks a control loop. Bury them in the analog details and they disengage, because it's not their domain and they can't tell which numbers matter.

What I aim for is a shared model of the signal chain that's quantitative where it needs to be and abstracted where it doesn't. Concretely, I'd give the firmware team three things: the noise floor and its character, the signal bandwidth they actually need to preserve, and the resolution and timing budget they're working within. The character of the noise matters as much as the magnitude — white noise, 1/f drift, and periodic pickup from a switching supply all call for different firmware responses, and a filter that's right for one is wrong for another.

I'd frame it as a contract rather than a complaint. Something like: "The analog front end delivers the signal with this much noise in this band; anything above this frequency is not real signal, so filtering there is free. Below this frequency you're filtering real information, so there's a trade-off between noise and latency." That gives the firmware engineer the information to make their own trade-off instead of being handed a conclusion.

I'd also make the constraint testable. If the hardware team can provide a captured dataset — real samples from the actual front end, including a known-good reference — the firmware team can develop and validate against it rather than against a description. A shared dataset removes most of the ambiguity in the conversation.

And I'd keep the door open both ways. Sometimes the right answer isn't a firmware filter at all — it's a hardware change, like a better reference, a different front-end topology, or a layout fix. If the firmware team comes back and says the constraint is unworkable, that's useful information, not a rejection, and it should feed back into the hardware decision while there's still time to change it.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filter introduced latency that violated a system-level timing requirement?
- What would you do if the hardware constraint couldn't be met without a board respin, and the schedule didn't allow one?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This is a common and often under-diagnosed pattern. The engineer isn't lacking ability — they execute well — but they've learned that deferring is safe. In most rooms, deferring to the senior person is rewarded, so the behavior persists even though it caps their growth.

The core of the fix is giving them ownership in a way that's real, not nominal. If I hand someone a task and then make the decision myself when it gets hard, I've taught them that deferring was correct. So the ownership has to include the decision, with me available as a resource rather than as the fallback. That means when they bring me a question, I resist answering it directly and instead ask what they think and why — not as a Socratic game, but genuinely, because their reasoning is the thing I'm trying to develop.

I'd also work on the distinction between a decision and a preference. Engineers who defer often haven't internalized that a design decision is a defensible choice among alternatives with stated trade-offs, not a search for the one right answer that a senior person happens to know. So I'd have them practice writing decisions down: the options considered, the criteria, the choice, and the reasoning. Once it's written, it's much harder to defer, because there's a concrete artifact to defend or revise.

I'd scaffold the difficulty. Start with a decision that's genuinely theirs to make and where the stakes are recoverable, and let them own it fully — including the consequences. Then increase scope as they build confidence. And I'd be explicit about the goal, so they understand that deferring isn't being polite, it's leaving a skill undeveloped.

The one thing I'd avoid is framing it as a confidence problem to be fixed by encouragement. Encouragement without a change in the actual ownership structure just produces someone who feels encouraged and still defers.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if they made a decision you disagreed with, and it was within their ownership?
- How would you tell the difference between healthy deference to expertise and a pattern that's limiting their growth?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?

**Answer:** The instinct is to find a middle level of detail that works for everyone, and that's usually the wrong move — it ends up too shallow for the engineers and too deep for the product manager, and nobody gets what they need. A better approach is to structure the presentation in layers, so each audience can engage at the depth that's relevant to their decision.

I'd lead with the decision and its consequences in plain terms: what we're choosing between, what each option costs in schedule, risk, and capability, and what I'm recommending. That top layer has to stand on its own, because the product manager and often the quality manager will make their judgment there. Then I'd provide the technical substantiation underneath — the measurements, the analysis, the trade-off curves — as supporting material that the engineers can interrogate and that anyone can drill into if they want.

The key is being explicit about which parts of the decision are technical and which are judgment calls about priorities. Engineers often present a trade-off as if it's purely technical when it actually contains a value judgment — how much schedule risk is acceptable, how much margin is enough. Surfacing that distinction lets the product manager contribute where their input is actually decisive, instead of feeling like they're being asked to rubber-stamp an engineering conclusion.

For the quality manager specifically, I'd make sure the risk and compliance implications are stated in their terms — what the trade-off means for the design history file, for verification, for the risk analysis — rather than assuming they'll extract it from the technical detail.

And I'd leave room for questions rather than trying to pre-empt every one. A mixed audience will ask at different levels, and the layered structure means I can answer the deep question without losing the room.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the product manager pushed for a decision based on schedule that the engineers felt was technically unsafe?
- What would you do if the quality manager raised a compliance concern that you hadn't anticipated during the presentation?
