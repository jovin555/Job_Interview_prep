# behavioral-leadership — Day 107

## Q1: How would you approach leading a team through the transition from a culture of "test it and see if it works" to a culture of "define what correct looks like before you test"?

**Answer:** The shift is fundamentally about moving from outcome-based confidence to evidence-based confidence, and it fails if it's framed as a process imposition rather than a change in how the team reasons. I'd start by making the cost of the current approach visible without blaming anyone: pick a recent issue where a "test and see" cycle produced a fix that didn't hold, and walk through — as a group, not as an accusation — where the missing definition of correct would have caught it earlier. That creates a shared problem statement the team owns.

From there I'd introduce the practice incrementally and concretely rather than as a policy. For a given work item, the lightweight version is: before testing, write down the pass criteria, the conditions under which the test is valid, and what a failure would look like. That's a few lines, not a document. I'd model it myself in reviews, ask for it in review packages, and — critically — make it safe to say "I don't actually know what correct looks like here yet" so that the gap gets surfaced early instead of being papered over with a test that happens to pass.

I'd also watch for the failure mode where the new practice becomes ritual: pass criteria written after the fact to match whatever the result was. The check is whether the criteria ever cause a test to be judged a failure that would previously have been called a pass. If that never happens, the practice isn't doing anything. Over time I'd want the team to see the payoff in fewer reopened issues and less rework, because that's what makes a culture change stick — the team adopting it because it works, not because it's required.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a senior engineer who agrees with the principle but argues that writing pass criteria up front is unrealistic when the behavior of the system isn't yet understood? What would you do if the practice starts producing pass criteria that are so vague they can't fail?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is technically competent but they consistently optimize for the wrong thing — for example, chasing precision or elegance in an area where robustness and manufacturability matter far more?

**Answer:** First I'd check whether this is a values mismatch or an information gap, because the response differs. If the engineer genuinely doesn't know what the project values — because nobody has said it explicitly — that's on leadership, and the fix is to make the priorities concrete: what "good" means for this design, what the dominant failure modes are, what the manufacturing and use environment will actually do to it. Many engineers default to optimizing what's measurable and intellectually satisfying precisely because the real priorities were never stated in terms they could design against.

If the priorities have been stated and the pattern persists, I'd move from general guidance to specific, recurring feedback tied to consequences. Rather than "be less focused on precision," I'd point at a concrete decision and ask them to walk me through the trade-off they made — what they optimized for, what they gave up, and whether that matches what the project needs. Getting them to articulate the trade-off themselves is usually more effective than telling them the answer, and it builds the habit of asking the question before committing.

I'd also give them a different kind of ownership: put them in a position where they have to defend a design to manufacturing, or to a reviewer whose job is to find the fragile parts. Nothing recalibrates an engineer's sense of what matters faster than being accountable to the people who have to build or maintain the thing. Throughout, I'd keep the feedback about the work and its context, not about their taste — the goal is to widen what they optimize for, not to convince them that craft doesn't matter.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer pushes back and argues that the robustness concerns are overblown? How would you tell the difference between someone who needs more context and someone who is simply not going to change how they work?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether to absorb a schedule slip quietly within the team or escalate it, when the slip is small but you suspect it's the leading edge of a larger problem?

**Answer:** The decision hinges on whether the slip is a variance or a signal, and the honest answer is that you often can't tell yet — so the right move is usually to escalate the *suspicion* in a calibrated way rather than either hiding it or declaring a crisis. Absorbing it quietly is tempting because a small slip looks manageable and escalation has a cost in credibility if it turns out to be nothing. But if it's the leading edge of something structural, the cost of having said nothing compounds: stakeholders lose the chance to make trade-offs early, when options are cheap.

My approach would be to do a short, focused investigation first — enough to characterize the slip rather than just report it. Is it one task, or a pattern across several? Is the estimate wrong, or is the work itself different from what was assumed? Is the same unknown showing up in more than one place? That turns a vague worry into either "this is contained, here's why" or "this looks like a pattern, here's the evidence." Then I'd communicate accordingly: if it's contained, I'd still mention it with the reasoning, because stakeholders dislike surprises more than they dislike small slips. If it looks like a pattern, I'd escalate early with the evidence and a proposed path, rather than waiting for certainty.

The key discipline is separating the size of the current slip from the size of the risk it implies, and being explicit about which one I'm communicating. Overpromising on the "it's fine" side is the failure that damages trust most, because it's the one that gets discovered late.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you frame the escalation so it doesn't read as alarmism when the actual slip is still small? What would make you decide the investigation itself is taking too long and you should escalate before you have the answer?

## Q4: How would you approach a design review where the technical content is strong but the presenter has structured the package so that the riskiest decisions are buried in appendices, and reviewers are likely to approve without engaging with them?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a review-design problem before treating it as a presenter problem, because the structure of a package determines what actually gets scrutinized, and a package that buries its risk will get a shallow review no matter how good the reviewers are. The first move is to redirect the review's attention explicitly: at the start, name the two or three decisions that carry the most risk or the least supporting evidence, and say that those are where I want the review's time spent. That's not an accusation — it's scoping — and it changes the meeting from a walkthrough into a targeted critique.

I'd also ask the presenter to lead with the decisions they're least sure about, rather than the ones they're most proud of. That's a norm worth establishing generally, because it inverts the natural incentive to present the finished-looking parts and hope nobody digs. If the presenter resists or the structure can't be changed in time, I'd pull the buried items out myself during the review and put them on the table, with the reasoning that if a decision is important enough to be in the package, it's important enough to be discussed in the room.

Afterwards, I'd give feedback on the package structure separately from the technical content, framed around what the review is for: a package should make the risky parts easy to find, not easy to skip. And I'd follow up by checking whether the actions from the buried items actually got closed, because a review that surfaces a risk and then loses it is worse than one that never found it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the presenter says the appendices are where the detail lives and reviewers are welcome to read them? What would you do if a reviewer tells you afterward that they didn't feel qualified to challenge the buried decisions?

## Q5: How would you approach building a shared understanding of "done" across a team where hardware, firmware, and verification each have a different working definition, and the mismatch only becomes visible late in a project?

**Answer:** The mismatch is almost always invisible early because each group's definition is internally coherent — it's only when they meet at an integration point that the gaps show. So the approach is to force the definitions to meet early and often, at a level of specificity where disagreement becomes concrete rather than abstract. "Firmware done" and "hardware done" can coexist peacefully as phrases; "the sensor reading is valid within this tolerance across this temperature range, with this power state, at this sample rate" cannot — either everyone agrees or the disagreement surfaces immediately.

Practically, I'd build the shared definition around integration checkpoints rather than around each discipline's internal milestones. For each checkpoint, define what must be demonstrably true, who demonstrates it, and what evidence counts. That gives the three groups a common object to argue about, and arguments about evidence tend to be productive in a way that arguments about process are not. I'd also make the definitions living: revisit them when a checkpoint reveals a gap, and treat the gap as a definition problem to fix rather than a failure to assign.

The cultural piece is making it normal to say "I don't think we mean the same thing by that" without it sounding like an accusation. I'd model that myself, and I'd watch for the pattern where a group quietly narrows its own definition to make its milestone — which is usually a sign that the shared definition was never specific enough to be binding.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if one group consistently meets its own definition but the integration checkpoints keep failing? What would you do if the team agrees on the definitions but then doesn't produce the evidence at the checkpoint?
