# behavioral-leadership — Day 78

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter has done solid technical work, but the review package arrives late and is missing key calculations, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct to either cancel the review or wave it through are both wrong — one wastes the only slot, the other defeats the purpose of the gate. I'd run the meeting but explicitly re-scope it. First, I'd open by naming the situation plainly and without blame: the package is incomplete, so this session cannot be a full approval review, and I'd state what it *can* accomplish — a structured walkthrough of the architecture and the areas where reviewers have enough information to give real feedback. Then I'd separate the review into two buckets: items that can be meaningfully assessed now (topology choices, interface definitions, component selection rationale, obvious risk areas) and items that are blocked by the missing calculations (trace widths, thermal margins, timing budgets). For the blocked items, I'd assign owners and a hard date for a follow-up review, and I'd record in the minutes that the milestone gate is conditionally passed pending those items — not silently passed. The key discipline is that the milestone decision must reflect reality: if the missing calculations are safety-critical, the gate does not close until they're reviewed, even if that means a short schedule slip. I'd also treat the late package as a process signal worth examining afterward — was the presenter overloaded, was the template unclear, was the deadline unrealistic? — because the fix is usually upstream of the individual.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the milestone owner pressures you to sign off anyway because "the work is clearly good," how do you hold the line without becoming the bottleneck?
- How would you decide which missing items are truly blocking versus which can be reviewed asynchronously after the gate?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when the failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures are where teams most often ship a "fix" that suppresses the symptom without addressing the cause, so the first job is to resist that pressure explicitly and get agreement that a containment action and a corrective action are different things. I'd start by defining containment: what can we do *right now* to protect users or the field while we investigate — a firmware guard, a derating, a usage restriction, additional monitoring — clearly labeled as temporary and tracked. Then I'd shift the investigation from "reproduce it" to "capture it." If we can't reproduce on demand, we instrument: add logging, counters, watchdogs, or a diagnostic build that records state at the moment of failure, and deploy it to where the failure occurs. I'd build a structured hypothesis tree — using something like a fishbone or fault tree — covering the plausible categories (timing, power, thermal, EMI, marginal component tolerance, software state) and design a test or measurement that could *falsify* each one, rather than trying to prove a favorite. I'd also look hard at the boundary conditions: intermittent problems often live at temperature extremes, voltage margins, or specific timing windows, so environmental and stress testing is often more productive than repeated nominal runs. Throughout, I'd keep a written log of what was ruled out and why, because with intermittent faults the discipline of elimination is what eventually corners the cause — and it prevents the team from re-litigating the same theories.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you decide when a containment action is good enough to ship while the investigation continues, especially in a regulated device?
- If the failure rate is very low and instrumentation is expensive to deploy, how do you justify the cost of the investigation?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is real: oversimplify and the firmware team makes assumptions that don't hold; over-detail and they disengage. I'd aim for a shared model rather than a data dump. Concretely, I'd characterize the constraint in the terms the firmware actually operates in — effective number of bits at the sampling rate they'll use, the noise floor in LSBs, the frequency content of the noise, and how it behaves over temperature and supply variation. Then I'd translate that into the decisions they own: what filtering is realistic, what averaging window makes sense, what the achievable resolution actually is after their processing, and where the constraint will bite them (for example, near full-scale or at fast sample rates). I'd give them a small set of measured plots or a short characterization table rather than a raw datasheet excerpt, and I'd offer to sit with them to interpret it. The goal is that they can predict, before writing code, whether a given algorithm will meet the requirement — and can come back to me with a specific question rather than a vague "the sensor seems noisy." I'd also make clear which parts of the constraint are fixed by physics and which are design choices still open, so they know where to push back.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team proposes a digital filter that you believe will mask a real hardware problem rather than solve it?
- What would you put in a short written interface document so this knowledge survives beyond the conversation?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This is usually not a capability gap but a confidence-and-exposure gap — they've been rewarded for executing well and never given a safe space to own a decision. I'd start by giving them ownership of something bounded but genuinely theirs: a subsystem, a component selection, a test strategy — with the explicit understanding that they make the call and I review it, not the reverse. Early on I'd ask them to bring me their recommendation *and* their reasoning before I offer any opinion, so I don't accidentally become the senior voice they defer to. In design reviews I'd deliberately ask them first, before the more senior people speak, and I'd redirect questions that get thrown over their head back to them. I'd also normalize the discomfort of owning a decision: walk them through how I structure a trade-off, how I decide when I have enough information, and how I document the rationale so a decision can be revisited later without ego. Over time I'd widen the scope and reduce my involvement, and I'd be explicit about the progression so they can see it's intentional. The measure of success is that they start disagreeing with me on technical grounds — that's when I know the mentoring is working.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if they make a decision you disagree with, and it's not safety-critical?
- What would you do if a more senior engineer keeps overriding them in meetings despite your efforts?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a disagreement turns personal, more debate makes it worse, so the first move is to change the *format*, not to pick a side. I'd pause the argument and reset the frame: state clearly that both options are legitimate, that the goal is to choose the one that best fits the requirements, and that we're going to decide on evidence rather than advocacy. Then I'd do two things. First, get the criteria on the table — the actual requirements the architecture must satisfy (cost, power, schedule, regulatory burden, manufacturability, team familiarity, risk) — and get both sides to agree on the criteria *before* scoring either option, because people argue about weights less when they're not defending a favorite. Second, wherever the disagreement hinges on an empirical question, resolve it with a prototype or a measurement rather than opinion: a bench test, a small proof-of-concept, a timing measurement. Often the personal edge comes from each side being partly right about different risks, and a prototype makes that visible. If after that the options are genuinely close, I'd make the call, document the rationale and the trade-offs accepted, and be clear that the decision is made so the team can commit — while leaving the door open to revisit if the assumptions change. Throughout I'd keep the language about the *problem*, not the people, and I'd avoid letting anyone's seniority substitute for an argument.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if, after the decision is made, one side continues to undermine it in side conversations?
- How do you document an architecture decision so it's defensible later without reopening the personal conflict?
