# behavioral-leadership — Day 106

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review keeps stalling because one reviewer raises a new objection in every session, and the team has started routing around the review process to make progress?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two problems, because they need different fixes. The reviewer's behavior is one issue; the team bypassing the review process is a more serious one, because it means the review has lost legitimacy and people are now making design decisions without scrutiny — which is exactly the failure mode reviews exist to prevent. I'd start by diagnosing why the objections keep coming. Sometimes a reviewer who raises something new every session is genuinely finding real issues but has no way to get them addressed in the current format, so each session surfaces the next one. Other times the reviewer is using the review as a design session — thinking out loud rather than gating — and the fix is structural: require objections to be submitted in writing before the review, with the specific concern and what evidence would resolve it. That converts "new objection every time" into a bounded, pre-circulated list. I'd also introduce a clear disposition for each item — accept, reject with rationale, or defer to a named owner with a date — so items close rather than accumulate. On the routing-around problem, I'd address it directly and without accusation: acknowledge that the process has been painful, state plainly that bypassing it isn't acceptable for safety-relevant decisions, and commit to fixing the format so it stops being a bottleneck. The message is "the process is the problem, not your patience — but we fix the process, we don't abandon it." If the reviewer's objections are legitimate but the volume is the issue, I'd consider splitting the review into a design-intent review early and a detailed verification review later, so the reviewer has a proper venue for the deeper concerns instead of surfacing them one per meeting.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the reviewer's objections turned out to be consistently valid and the real problem was that the design wasn't ready for review in the first place? How would you handle it if the reviewer perceived the pre-submission requirement as an attempt to silence them?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?
**Answer:** I'd structure the presentation in layers rather than trying to find one level of detail that suits everyone — because there isn't one. The top layer is the decision itself and its consequences in terms each stakeholder owns: for the product manager, schedule and cost impact; for the quality manager, risk and regulatory implications; for the engineers, the technical basis. I'd lead with a one-slide summary that states the options, the recommendation, and the two or three criteria that drive it, so anyone in the room can follow the logic even if they don't follow the physics. Then I'd go deeper on the criteria that matter to the decision, not on everything I know. The common failure is presenting the full technical derivation because it's what we're proudest of, which loses the non-engineers and invites the engineers to relitigate details that don't change the outcome. A useful discipline is to ask, for each piece of detail, "if this changed, would the recommendation change?" If not, it belongs in backup, not the main flow. I'd also make the trade-off explicit and quantified where possible — what we give up with each option — because that's the language all three stakeholders can engage with. For the quality manager specifically, I'd surface the risk implications early rather than letting them be inferred, since that's their lens and they'll ask anyway. And I'd leave deliberate space for questions, with the detailed backup ready, so depth is available on demand rather than imposed on everyone.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the product manager and the quality manager drew opposite conclusions from the same trade-off summary? What would you do if an engineer in the room felt the summary oversimplified to the point of being misleading?

## Q3: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?
**Answer:** When a disagreement turns personal, the technical argument has usually stopped being the actual driver — people are defending positions and identities rather than evaluating options, and more debate makes it worse, not better. My first move is to de-escalate the framing: name the pattern explicitly but without blaming anyone, something like "we've got two credible approaches and the discussion has gotten heated; let's reset on what would actually let us choose." Then I'd try to convert the dispute into something empirical. The most effective tool is usually a defined evaluation: agree on the criteria that matter — power, cost, reliability, schedule, regulatory risk, team familiarity — weight them, and score both options against them, ideally with a small prototype or benchmark for the criteria that are genuinely uncertain. This works because it moves people from arguing about whose judgment is better to arguing about evidence, which is a much healthier fight. I'd also deliberately separate the people from the positions: give each side a structured turn to state the strongest version of the other side's case, which often reveals that the disagreement is narrower than it appears. If after a genuine evidence-based evaluation the options are still close, that's a signal the decision may not be as consequential as the heat suggests, and I'd make a call, document the rationale, and commit — including a checkpoint to revisit if specific conditions change. What I would not do is let it drift or split the difference into a hybrid that satisfies neither camp. And if the personal dynamic persists despite all that, I'd address it privately with the individuals, because unresolved interpersonal friction will resurface on the next decision.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if one side refused to engage with the evaluation criteria because they believed the criteria themselves were rigged? How would you handle it if the losing side quietly disengaged from implementing the chosen architecture?

## Q4: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument?
**Answer:** The core insight is that "gut feeling" concerns are usually pattern recognition that hasn't been verbalized yet — an engineer who has seen a similar failure before may sense a problem before they can articulate why. A culture that only accepts fully-formed arguments discards exactly the early warning signal you most want. So I'd work on two fronts. First, make it safe and normal to raise an inchoate concern by giving it a low-cost channel and explicit legitimacy: something like a standing "concerns and unknowns" item in design reviews, or a norm that anyone can say "I can't fully justify this yet, but something about this worries me — can we spend five minutes on it?" The leader's job is to respond to that with curiosity rather than "come back when you have data," because the response to the first tentative concern teaches everyone whether it's worth raising the next one. Second, I'd make it structurally easier to challenge senior decisions by separating the decision from the decider — for example, requiring that significant decisions be documented with their rationale and explicitly inviting dissent on the record before sign-off, so disagreement is a normal part of the process rather than a challenge to a person's authority. I'd also model it myself: when I make a call, I'd state what would change my mind, and visibly thank people who push back, especially when they turn out to be right. Over time the norm becomes that raising a concern early is a contribution, not an act of insubordination. The failure mode to watch for is the "we discussed that already" shutdown, which teaches people to stay quiet.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a "gut feeling" concern turned out to be wrong and the engineer became embarrassed about having raised it? What would you do if a senior engineer consistently treated early concerns from juniors as noise, undermining the culture you're trying to build?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than systemic issues?
**Answer:** The purpose of a post-mortem is to change what happens next time, not to assign blame — and those two goals are in direct tension, because a blame-oriented process guarantees people will defend themselves and withhold information, which destroys the learning. So the first thing I'd do is set the frame explicitly at the start: we're here to understand the system that produced this outcome, not to find the person who caused it, and I'd mean it — including being willing to put my own decisions under the same lens. I'd structure the analysis around the timeline and the decisions, not the people: what did we know, when did we know it, what did we decide, and what information or constraint drove that decision. That framing naturally surfaces systemic causes — optimistic estimates with no historical basis, a dependency that was never made explicit, a review gate that was treated as a formality, a risk that was raised but had no owner. I'd use a structured method like a fishbone or a "five whys" on the major schedule slips to get past the first, superficial cause, because "the firmware was late" is a symptom, not a cause. I'd also deliberately separate containment from corrective action: what do we do about this project now, versus what do we change in how we plan and gate projects going forward. On morale, I'd acknowledge the difficulty honestly and credit the team for what did work, while being clear that the retrospective is how we earn a better outcome next time. The output should be a small number of specific, owned, dated changes — not a document that gets filed and forgotten — and I'd follow up on whether those changes actually took effect.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the retrospective surfaced that a specific individual's performance was genuinely a significant factor, not just a systemic one? How would you make sure the corrective actions from the post-mortem actually get implemented rather than quietly dropped once the next project ramps up?
