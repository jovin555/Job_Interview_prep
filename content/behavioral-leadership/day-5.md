# behavioral-leadership — Day 5

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design history file (DHF) audit readiness review when you discover that several critical design decisions were documented only in email threads and meeting notes, not in the formal DHF?

**Answer:** I would start by assessing the scope of the gap — identifying which design decisions are affected, whether they relate to safety-critical functions, and what stage the project is in. The immediate priority is containment: I'd freeze any further undocumented decisions and establish a temporary process requiring all design decisions to be formally captured in the DHF going forward. Then I'd work backward to reconstruct the missing documentation, pulling from the available sources (emails, meeting minutes, design review slides) and interviewing the engineers involved to confirm the rationale and alternatives considered. For each entry, I'd include a clear statement of the decision, the technical basis, any trade-offs evaluated, and a reference to the source materials used for reconstruction. I'd flag these reconstructed entries clearly so an auditor can see they were captured retrospectively. Finally, I'd implement a process improvement — such as requiring design decision records to be completed within 48 hours of a decision being made, with a checklist tied to the project's phase-gate reviews — to prevent recurrence. The key is balancing audit readiness with honesty about what was captured contemporaneously versus reconstructed.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the engineers who made the original decisions have left the company? What if the missing documentation involves a safety-critical decision that could affect the device's risk management file?

## Q2: How would you approach convincing a team that has never used structured root-cause analysis (like 8D or fishbone diagrams) to adopt it for investigating a recurring technical issue?

**Answer:** I wouldn't start by mandating a specific methodology. Instead, I'd begin by framing the problem: we have a recurring issue that quick fixes haven't resolved, and we're spending more time firefighting than we would on a thorough investigation. I'd propose trying a structured approach on this one issue as an experiment, not a permanent process change. I'd lead the first session myself, walking through the 8D steps collaboratively — starting with containment (what do we do right now to protect the customer?), then moving to problem description (what exactly is happening, under what conditions?), then root cause analysis using a simple fishbone diagram on a whiteboard. I'd keep it practical and time-boxed, showing how the structure actually saves time by preventing rabbit holes. After we identify and implement corrective actions, I'd follow up to demonstrate whether the issue recurred. Once the team sees the method working on a real problem they care about, adoption tends to follow naturally. I'd also offer to pair with a team member on the next investigation so they can lead it themselves with coaching.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if a senior engineer on the team resists because they believe their intuition is faster than any formal method? How would you measure whether the structured approach is actually reducing recurrence rates?

## Q3: How would you approach managing a situation where your team is split on whether to use a known, qualified component that is nearing end-of-life or a newer, unqualified alternative for a medical device that will need to be manufactured for at least five more years?

**Answer:** I'd frame this as a risk trade-off decision that needs data, not opinions. First, I'd gather the facts: what is the manufacturer's last-time-buy date and estimated remaining inventory for the qualified component? What is the lead time and qualification status of the alternative? How long would requalification take for the medical device — including any regulatory implications like filing a 510(k) supplement if the change affects safety or performance? I'd then map out two scenarios with timelines and risk profiles. The known component gives us immediate production stability but carries obsolescence risk; the alternative requires upfront investment in qualification but extends product life. I'd involve supply chain, regulatory, and quality teams to get their input on the risks. If the qualified component has enough inventory to cover production plus a reasonable buffer while we qualify the alternative in parallel, that's often the safest path — we don't disrupt current production, and we have a qualified backup ready before the component goes end-of-life. I'd document the decision with the rationale and risk assessment, and set a timeline for completing the alternative qualification. If inventory is insufficient for parallel qualification, then we'd need to accelerate qualification or find a second qualified alternative, accepting some schedule impact.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the regulatory affairs team telling you that any component change, even to a pin-compatible alternative, requires a new submission? What if the newer component has better performance but introduces a new failure mode that needs additional risk mitigation?

## Q4: How would you approach building a cross-functional project timeline for a medical device when the hardware, firmware, and regulatory teams each have different estimates for how long their work will take, and there's no historical data from similar projects?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging the uncertainty openly rather than trying to force a single estimate. I'd bring the team leads together for a structured estimation session, breaking the project into smaller work packages that each team can estimate independently. For each work package, I'd ask for three estimates: optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic — and I'd ask them to articulate the assumptions behind each. This surfaces hidden dependencies and differing risk tolerances. For example, the firmware team might assume hardware prototypes are available on a certain date, while hardware assumes they can start layout later. I'd then build a timeline that shows the critical path and the range of possible completion dates, not a single date. I'd present this to stakeholders as: "Based on our estimates, the most likely completion is month X, but it could be as early as month Y or as late as month Z, depending on these key risks." I'd also identify which risks have the biggest impact on the timeline and propose mitigation strategies — like ordering long-lead components early, or running regulatory pre-submission meetings in parallel with development. The goal is to replace false precision with honest uncertainty and a plan to reduce that uncertainty over time.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a product manager who insists on a single committed date for the business plan? What if, halfway through the project, it becomes clear that the pessimistic scenario is playing out — how do you communicate that without losing credibility?

## Q5: How would you approach handling a situation where a team member consistently delivers high-quality technical work but has a pattern of missing internal deadlines, causing downstream delays for the rest of the team?

**Answer:** I'd start with a private conversation to understand the root cause before assuming it's a motivation or discipline issue. There are several possible reasons: the person might be overcommitting because they struggle to say no, they might underestimate task complexity, they might have perfectionist tendencies that prevent them from shipping "good enough" work, or there could be external blockers they haven't raised. I'd ask open-ended questions about their workload, their estimation process, and whether there are specific types of tasks where they tend to run late. Once I understand the pattern, I'd work with them on practical adjustments. If they underestimate complexity, we could add a review step early in the task where I help validate their estimate. If they overcommit, we could set a rule that they check with me before taking on additional work. If perfectionism is the issue, I'd clarify what "done" looks like for each deliverable and set intermediate checkpoints to ensure they're on track. I'd also make sure the rest of the team understands that I'm addressing the issue — without throwing the person under the bus — so they know the downstream delays are being managed. If the pattern doesn't improve after coaching and process changes, I'd escalate to a formal performance improvement plan with clear, measurable expectations and a timeline.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the team member is highly respected for their technical expertise and the rest of the team tolerates the delays because the work quality is so good? How would you balance the needs of the individual against the team's overall velocity?
