## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a sensor hardware failure or a firmware filtering algorithm error, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd start by framing the investigation around data collection rather than hypothesis testing, because the risk with a hardware-versus-firmware split is that each team biases what they look at. First, I'd establish a containment action — if the device is in the field, that might mean a software patch to disable or flag the affected sensor channel, or a field notice to collect units — so patient safety is addressed while we investigate. Then I'd gather three categories of evidence: field data (error logs, device telemetry, environmental conditions at time of failure), returned units (physical inspection, re-testing under controlled conditions), and design documentation (sensor datasheet, calibration procedure, filtering algorithm specification, and any prior failure modes from similar devices). I'd use a fishbone diagram to map all plausible causes across categories — sensor drift, solder joint integrity, ADC reference noise, filter coefficient overflow, timing issues in the firmware — rather than letting the two teams anchor on their own domain. The key discriminator is often in the pattern: if failures correlate with temperature or device age, that points to hardware; if they correlate with specific firmware versions or data patterns, that points to software. I'd also look for a way to isolate the sensor from the algorithm — for example, logging raw ADC values before filtering, or running the same firmware on a test bench with a known-good sensor. Once we have a root cause, I'd document it formally, implement the corrective action, and define a verification test that proves the fix addresses the root cause — not just the symptom. If the investigation stalls, I'd escalate to a cross-functional review with quality and regulatory involved, because in a medical device context, the decision affects the design history file and potentially the risk management file.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where the field data is incomplete — for example, the device logs don't capture the raw sensor values needed to discriminate between the two causes?
- What criteria would you use to decide whether to recall devices from the field versus issuing a software patch?

## Q2: How would you approach building a technical leadership roadmap for a team that is transitioning from a single-product medical device focus to a multi-project portfolio, where engineers are used to deep specialization rather than cross-project flexibility?

**Answer:** I'd start by assessing the current skill distribution and the demands of the new portfolio — not just technically, but in terms of how work is structured. In a single-product environment, engineers often develop deep expertise in one subsystem (e.g., analog front-end, power, firmware). In a multi-project portfolio, you need people who can move between projects and contribute at different levels of depth. I'd approach this in phases. First, I'd map the portfolio's technical requirements against the team's current skills to identify gaps — both in terms of missing competencies and in terms of over-specialization that creates bus-factor risk. Second, I'd create a cross-training plan that pairs engineers from different specialties on short, low-risk tasks — for example, having the firmware engineer review a hardware design for testability, or having the analog engineer review a firmware change that affects sensor sampling. The goal isn't to make everyone a generalist, but to build enough shared vocabulary that people can contribute outside their core area and can cover for each other when projects overlap. Third, I'd introduce shared infrastructure — common design guidelines, reusable schematic blocks, standardized firmware drivers — so that moving between projects doesn't mean relearning everything from scratch. Fourth, I'd address the cultural shift: engineers who are used to deep specialization may feel threatened by the expectation to work across projects, so I'd make it clear that specialization is still valued, but that the team needs a broader base of capability. I'd also use project retrospectives to capture lessons learned and feed them back into the shared guidelines. Finally, I'd work with project management to stagger project timelines so that no single engineer is the critical path for multiple projects simultaneously, and I'd use the portfolio planning process to identify which projects can serve as training grounds for cross-functional skills.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a senior engineer who resists cross-training because they believe it dilutes their expertise and reduces the quality of their work?
- How would you measure whether the cross-training effort is actually improving the team's ability to handle the multi-project portfolio?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a risk-based test design problem rather than a simple yes/no decision on the sleep mode. The core question is: what can go wrong during the wake-up transition, and how do we detect it? I'd start by defining the failure modes — for example, the sensor's reference voltage settling time, ADC warm-up drift, or noise coupling from the power supply as it ramps. Then I'd design a test matrix that covers three dimensions: the sleep mode parameters (duration, depth of sleep, which peripherals are powered down), the wake-up conditions (timing, temperature, battery state of charge), and the measurement requirements (accuracy tolerance, settling time, data validity). The key is to test the transition itself, not just the steady-state performance. I'd include tests that measure sensor output continuously across the wake-up boundary — not just sampling at a single point — to capture transient behavior. I'd also test at the extremes: worst-case battery voltage, highest and lowest operating temperature, and the shortest and longest sleep durations. If the hardware team's concern is about analog stability, I'd add a test that measures the sensor's output noise and offset during the first few milliseconds after wake-up, compared to steady-state. I'd also involve the firmware team in defining what "valid data" means — for example, whether the first few samples after wake-up should be discarded or flagged. The test strategy should include both automated bench testing (to characterize the transition) and system-level testing (to verify the device behaves correctly in a realistic use scenario). If the test results show instability, I'd work with both teams to find a mitigation — for example, adding a settling delay in firmware, or changing the power sequencing in hardware. The decision to proceed with the sleep mode should be based on data, not on which team argues more convincingly.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide what settling time is acceptable, given that the sensor's datasheet may specify a worst-case value that is much longer than the typical value?
- How would you handle a situation where the test results show marginal instability that only occurs at extreme temperatures, and the firmware team wants to ship with a workaround while the hardware team wants to redesign the power supply?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a technical review of a proposed architecture for a new medical device where the system architect has chosen a modular design with multiple microcontrollers communicating over CAN-FD, but several senior engineers believe a single high-performance processor would be simpler and more reliable?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating the technical debate from the interpersonal dynamics. The architect has a rationale for the modular design — likely fault isolation, independent development, or scalability — and the senior engineers have a rationale for the single-processor approach — likely simplicity, lower power, or fewer failure modes. My role is to ensure the decision is made on evidence, not on seniority or persuasion. I'd structure the review around a set of explicit evaluation criteria: reliability (failure modes and their impact), safety (how failures are detected and handled), power consumption, development risk, testability, manufacturability, and long-term maintainability. For each criterion, I'd ask the team to provide data or analysis — not opinions. For example, for reliability, I'd ask: what are the single points of failure in each architecture, and how do they map to the device's risk analysis? For power, I'd ask for estimates based on the actual peripherals and operating modes. For development risk, I'd ask about team experience with each approach and the availability of tools and libraries. I'd also ask the architect to articulate the specific design decisions that led to the modular choice — for example, whether the CAN-FD bus is needed for data throughput, or whether it's a preference for decoupling subsystems. If the disagreement persists after the analysis, I'd propose a prototyping phase to resolve the key unknowns — for example, building a small test board to measure CAN-FD latency and power consumption under realistic load, or benchmarking the single-processor's ability to handle the same workload. The decision should be documented with the rationale, including the trade-offs that were considered and why the chosen architecture was selected. If the team still can't reach consensus, I'd escalate to a broader technical review with input from quality and regulatory, because in a medical device, the architecture decision affects the risk management file and the design history file.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where the architect is unwilling to compromise and threatens to leave the project if their design is not chosen?
- What specific metrics or data would you require before making a final decision, and how would you ensure the data is not biased by the team's preferred outcome?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers feel comfortable raising concerns about a design decision early, even when the decision was made by a senior person or a consensus of the team, and even when the concern is based on a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated technical argument?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a psychological safety problem as much as a technical process problem. The first step is to model the behavior myself — I'd explicitly invite challenges to my own decisions, and when someone raises a concern, I'd take it seriously even if it's not fully formed. I'd also make it clear that raising a concern early is not a criticism of the decision-maker, but a contribution to the decision's quality. The second step is to create a structured channel for concerns that lowers the barrier to speaking up. For example, I'd introduce a "technical concern log" where anyone can raise a concern anonymously or with their name, and the team commits to responding within a set timeframe. The response doesn't have to agree with the concern, but it has to acknowledge it and explain the reasoning — either why the concern is valid and will be addressed, or why it's been considered and rejected. This prevents concerns from being dismissed in the moment, which is what discourages people from speaking up. The third step is to normalize the idea that a "gut feeling" is a legitimate starting point for investigation. I'd encourage engineers to phrase it as "I have a concern about X, and I'm not sure why — can we look at it together?" rather than requiring them to have a fully articulated technical argument. The goal is to convert the gut feeling into a testable hypothesis. The fourth step is to recognize and reinforce the behavior — when someone raises a concern that leads to a design improvement, I'd acknowledge it publicly and connect it to the outcome. Finally, I'd address the specific case of a senior person's decision: I'd make it clear that seniority doesn't make a decision immune to challenge, and I'd model this by asking questions about senior engineers' decisions myself. If a senior engineer reacts defensively to a challenge, I'd address that privately — not by punishing the person who raised the concern, but by helping the senior engineer see that the challenge is an opportunity to strengthen the design.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where a junior engineer raises a concern that turns out to be based on a misunderstanding, and the senior engineer uses that as evidence that junior concerns should be ignored?
- How would you measure whether the culture is actually changing, rather than just having the appearance of openness?
