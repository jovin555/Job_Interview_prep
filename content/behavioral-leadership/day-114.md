# behavioral-leadership — Day 114

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a team has quietly stopped escalating problems early — not because anyone decided to, but because past experience taught them that raising an issue early gets the messenger blamed or handed extra work?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a learned behavior, not a character flaw, and start by acknowledging that the team's caution is rational given what they've experienced. The first step is to make the cost of silence visible without punishing anyone retroactively: in a retrospective or one-on-one setting, ask people to describe — in general terms — what happens after someone raises a concern, and listen for the pattern. If the pattern is "the person who flags it owns it forever" or "the last person to raise a problem got questioned about why they didn't catch it sooner," that's a process and leadership problem, not an attitude problem.

Then I'd change the mechanics, not just the message. Concretely: separate "raising a concern" from "owning the fix" so that flagging a risk is a valued contribution even if someone else resolves it; make early risk-raising a normal agenda item in design reviews and standups rather than an exceptional act; and when someone does raise something early, respond visibly well — thank them publicly, act on it proportionately, and don't attach blame to the discovery. I'd also model it myself by surfacing my own uncertainties and near-misses, because a team calibrates what's safe from what leaders do, not what they say. Finally, I'd watch for the failure mode where "psychological safety" becomes an excuse for never delivering bad news firmly — the goal is early, honest escalation with clear ownership, not conflict avoidance.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the person who most discourages early escalation is a senior engineer whose technical judgment the team respects? How would you tell whether the culture has actually changed, given that the absence of raised concerns could mean either safety or silence?

## Q2: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the two strongest engineers on your team have each produced a fully documented, defensible design for the same subsystem, and both are refusing to concede — not because the data is ambiguous but because each has become personally invested in their own proposal?

**Answer:** When the data is genuinely ambiguous, the problem usually isn't technical anymore — it's that "which design wins" has become a proxy for "whose judgment is respected." I'd start by naming that dynamic explicitly but without accusation, so the team can separate the engineering question from the identity question. Then I'd try to convert the disagreement into something testable: define the criteria that actually matter for this subsystem — cost, power, manufacturability, verification effort, schedule risk, regulatory exposure — and score both proposals against them with the two engineers present. Often the exercise itself reveals that one design is clearly better on the criteria that matter and worse on criteria nobody weighted; sometimes it reveals the two designs are close enough that the decision should be made on secondary factors like team familiarity or supply chain.

If it's still genuinely close, I'd make the decision and own it, rather than forcing a false consensus. I'd state the rationale in writing, credit both engineers' work, and — critically — commit to a checkpoint where we revisit if specific evidence emerges. What I would not do is let it drift, or split the difference into a hybrid design that neither engineer has validated. I'd also follow up privately with the engineer whose proposal wasn't chosen, because the goal is to keep both of them engaged and willing to bring strong proposals next time.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the losing engineer's proposal was actually the better one and you only realized it after the decision? How would you prevent this kind of personal investment from building up in the first place?

## Q3: How would you approach building a shared definition of "done" across hardware, firmware, and verification teams whose working definitions only diverge visibly late in a medical device project?

**Answer:** The divergence usually isn't a disagreement about words — it's that each discipline has a different implicit exit criterion. Hardware may consider a board "done" when it powers up and the rails are in spec; firmware may consider a feature "done" when it runs on the bench; verification may consider it "done" only when it's been exercised against the requirement under worst-case conditions and the evidence is captured. Late in a project those three definitions collide, and the collision looks like a schedule problem when it's really a definition problem.

I'd address it by making "done" explicit and shared at the level of the deliverable, not the discipline. Practically: for each major work item, write down what evidence will exist when it's complete — a test report, a captured trace, a signed-off requirement, a reviewed schematic — and who accepts that evidence. That forces the conversation early, when it's cheap. I'd also build the definition into the project plan itself, so that "done" is a gate with named artifacts rather than a feeling. And I'd run a short joint review at each milestone where hardware, firmware, and verification each state what they believe is complete and what they're assuming the others have done — the assumptions are where the gaps hide. The cultural piece is making it normal to say "I don't think that's actually done yet" without it being heard as an attack on a colleague.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if verification's definition of done is stricter than the project can afford, and the schedule was built on the looser definition? What's the smallest intervention that would have caught this divergence early?

## Q4: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically strong, but the safety-critical rationale is scattered across appendices and side documents — and reviewers are likely to approve without ever engaging with it?

**Answer:** My first move is to not run the review as scheduled, or at least not run it as an approval. If the safety-critical rationale is buried, the review will produce a false signal — a room full of people nodding at material they haven't actually evaluated — and that's worse than a delay, because it creates a record of review that doesn't reflect real scrutiny. I'd say so plainly and without blaming the presenter: the package is good work, but the format makes it too easy for reviewers to miss the decisions that matter most.

Then I'd restructure the session around the risk, not the document. I'd ask the presenter to walk through the two or three safety-critical decisions in the main body — what the hazard is, what the design does about it, what the residual risk is, and what evidence supports it — and have reviewers engage with those specifically before anything else is discussed. If the rationale genuinely isn't ready to be presented that way, the honest outcome is to defer approval and reschedule, with a clear list of what needs to be surfaced. I'd also give the presenter concrete guidance on structure for next time: safety-critical decisions belong up front with their justification, not in an appendix, because the review's job is to catch problems, and a package that hides its riskiest content defeats that.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the presenter takes the request to restructure as criticism of their work? What would you do if the milestone gate can't move and the review has to happen today regardless?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding whether to absorb a small schedule slip quietly within the team or escalate it, when you suspect the slip is the leading edge of a larger problem?

**Answer:** The deciding factor isn't the size of the slip — it's whether I understand its cause. A small slip with a known, bounded cause (a component arrived late, a test took longer than estimated) is something a team can absorb and report at the next normal checkpoint. A small slip whose cause I can't explain, or that's the second or third in the same area, is a signal, and signals get escalated even when the number is small. The trap is that absorbing it quietly feels like good citizenship — protecting the team from scrutiny — when it's actually withholding information that the people above me need to make their own decisions.

So my test is: can I state the cause, the remaining uncertainty, and a credible recovery path? If yes, I'd manage it within the team and mention it at the next checkpoint with the recovery plan attached. If no — if I'm guessing, or if the same area keeps slipping — I'd escalate early, framed not as "we're late" but as "here's what I'm seeing, here's what I don't yet understand, here's what I'm doing to find out, and here's when I'll know more." That framing gives stakeholders something to act on rather than a bare bad number, and it protects my credibility for the next time I say a slip is genuinely small. I'd also make sure the team knows why I escalated, so it doesn't read as throwing them under a bus.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if your manager's response to early escalation is to micromanage, which is exactly what the team was trying to avoid? How do you distinguish a slip that's the leading edge of a problem from ordinary estimation noise?
