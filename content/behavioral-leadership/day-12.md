# behavioral-leadership — Day 12

## Q1: How would you approach building a technical leadership roadmap for a team that is transitioning from a single-product medical device focus to a multi-project portfolio, where engineers are used to deep specialization rather than cross-project flexibility?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the current skill inventory against the anticipated project pipeline, identifying both gaps and underutilized strengths. The transition from single-product depth to multi-project breadth is as much a cultural shift as a technical one, so I'd focus on three pillars. First, establish clear technical ownership models — each project needs a designated technical lead with defined decision rights, but those leads should form a peer group that meets regularly to share lessons learned and avoid siloed problem-solving. Second, create reusable design assets and guidelines that reduce the cost of context-switching — for example, common schematic building blocks, validation checklists, and DFM rules that engineers can apply across projects without re-deriving everything from scratch. Third, implement a lightweight resource allocation process that makes trade-offs visible and explicit rather than letting them happen implicitly through whoever shouts loudest. I'd also be deliberate about pairing engineers across projects — having someone from the sensor team review a power design for a different product, for instance — to build cross-pollination without overloading any single individual. The key is to maintain engineering quality and regulatory rigor while increasing flexibility, which means the documentation and design review practices that work for one project need to be scaled thoughtfully rather than duplicated wholesale.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle an engineer who resists working across multiple projects because they feel it dilutes their expertise? What metrics would you use to track whether the transition is succeeding?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a system-level verification problem rather than a firmware-only or hardware-only concern. The first step would be to define the exact requirements: what does "stable" mean for the sensor readings during wake-up, what settling time is acceptable, and what are the worst-case environmental conditions? These need to be quantified, not left as qualitative concerns. Then I'd design a test matrix that exercises the sleep/wake transitions across the full operating envelope — different battery voltages, temperatures, and timing sequences — because these transitions are exactly where subtle interactions between the power rail, the sensor reference, and the firmware state machine tend to surface. I'd also recommend adding diagnostic hooks to the firmware so that test engineers can correlate the timing of wake-up events with analog signal behavior, rather than trying to infer causality from the output data alone. A key part of the strategy would be automated long-duration testing that cycles through thousands of sleep/wake transitions, since intermittent issues often only appear after many cycles. Finally, I'd make sure the test results feed back into a formal risk assessment — if the sleep mode introduces any new failure modes, they need to be documented and mitigated through the design, not just tested around.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific analog parameters would you monitor during the wake-up transition, and how would you set pass/fail criteria? How would you handle a situation where the sleep mode passes all automated tests but a clinician reports occasional anomalous readings in the field?

## Q3: How would you approach leading a technical review of a proposed architecture for a new medical device where the system architect has chosen a modular design with multiple microcontrollers communicating over CAN-FD, but several senior engineers believe a single high-performance processor would be simpler and more reliable?

**Answer:** I'd structure the review around objective criteria rather than allowing it to become a preference debate. The first step is to establish what the system actually needs: the number of concurrent real-time tasks, the isolation requirements between safety-critical and non-critical functions, the communication bandwidth needs, and the fault tolerance expectations. For a medical device, the key question is often whether a single point of failure in one processor is acceptable, or whether the architecture needs to provide some level of redundancy or partitioning. A modular multi-MCU approach can offer better fault isolation — a failure in one subsystem doesn't necessarily compromise the others — but it introduces complexity in inter-processor communication, synchronization, and overall system verification. A single-processor approach is simpler to verify and debug, but it concentrates risk and may create scheduling challenges if there are many concurrent real-time tasks. I'd ask the architect to walk through specific failure scenarios and how each architecture handles them, and I'd ask the skeptics to articulate their concerns as testable hypotheses rather than general unease. If the disagreement persists, I'd propose a focused prototyping effort — perhaps implementing the most challenging communication or timing scenario on both architectures — to generate data that informs the decision. The goal is to make the trade-offs explicit and documented, so that whatever decision is made, the rationale is captured for the design history file.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when a prototyping effort is worth the schedule cost versus making a decision based on analysis alone? What specific failure scenarios would you prioritize in the evaluation?

## Q4: How would you approach handling a situation where you are leading a project and discover that a critical firmware module was developed by an external contractor who is no longer available, the code has no documentation, and the module is now failing intermittently during system integration testing?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a risk management problem with immediate and long-term components. The immediate priority is to stabilize the system — I'd work with the team to characterize the failure precisely: what are the conditions, how often does it occur, and what are the observable symptoms? This data is essential before any code changes are made, because modifying undocumented code without understanding the failure is a recipe for introducing new issues. I'd also assess whether there's a workaround that can keep integration testing moving on other subsystems while the firmware issue is investigated. In parallel, I'd begin a structured reverse-engineering effort: the team would need to map the module's interfaces, trace its state machine, and document its behavior from the outside in. This is where pair debugging is valuable — having one engineer trace the code while another documents findings and tests hypotheses. I'd also check whether the contractor left any artifacts — version control history, build scripts, or even informal notes — that could accelerate understanding. Once the failure is understood and fixed, the long-term corrective action is to establish a standard for external deliverables: documented code, design descriptions, and a knowledge transfer session before the contractor exits. The 8D framework applies here — containment is the workaround or patch, root cause is the code defect, and the corrective action includes both the code fix and the process improvement that prevents undocumented external code from being accepted in the future.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to fix the existing module versus rewriting it from scratch? What level of documentation would you require from external contractors going forward, and how would you enforce it?

## Q5: How would you approach building a culture where engineers feel comfortable raising concerns about a design decision early, even when the decision was made by a senior person or a consensus of the team, and even when the concern is based on a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated technical argument?

**Answer:** I'd focus on creating psychological safety through both structure and behavior. Structurally, I'd make it explicit that raising concerns is part of the engineering role, not an optional extra — design reviews should have a standing agenda item for "concerns and open questions," and the expectation should be that every engineer speaks at some point in the review, even if only to say they have no concerns. I'd also make it safe to raise partially-formed concerns by responding to them seriously — asking clarifying questions, exploring the reasoning, and treating the concern as a hypothesis to be tested rather than dismissing it as vague. Behaviorally, I'd model this myself: when I have a concern, I'd voice it openly, even if I can't fully articulate it, and I'd thank others when they do the same. I'd also be careful about how concerns are resolved — if a concern is raised and the team decides not to act on it, the decision and rationale should be documented, and the person who raised it should be acknowledged for their contribution. Over time, this builds a norm where raising concerns is seen as a sign of engagement and rigor, not a challenge to authority. The key is consistency — one dismissive response can undo months of trust-building, so the response to a concern, especially a vague one, needs to be as careful as the response to a fully-formed technical argument.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where an engineer repeatedly raises concerns that turn out to be unfounded, and the team starts to tune them out? How would you distinguish between a "gut feeling" that deserves investigation and one that is likely just anxiety or resistance to change?
