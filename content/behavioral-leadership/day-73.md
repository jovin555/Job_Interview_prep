# behavioral-leadership — Day 73

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter has done solid technical work, but the review package arrives late and is missing key calculations, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct is to either cancel the review or wave it through, and both are wrong for a milestone gate. I would separate the two problems: the review's *purpose* and the review's *inputs*. The purpose — independent scrutiny before a gate — doesn't go away because the package is late. So I'd run the meeting, but reframe it explicitly at the start: this session is a structured walkthrough of the design intent and the open risks, not a sign-off. I'd ask the presenter to talk through the design decisions and the reasoning behind them, since that knowledge is in their head even if it isn't on paper. Where a calculation is missing, I'd ask them to state the assumption and the method verbally, and I'd capture it as an action item to be written up and circulated. The reviewers who can evaluate the parts that *are* present should do so fully. Then I'd schedule a short follow-up focused only on the missing items, and make clear that the gate decision is provisional until those items are reviewed. The key is not to let "the package is incomplete" become a reason to skip scrutiny, and not to let "we have a gate" become a reason to rubber-stamp. I'd also treat the late package as a process signal worth raising separately — if it's a one-off, it's a one-off; if it's a pattern, the review cadence or the template is probably wrong.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the presenter pushes back and says the missing calculations are "obvious" and don't need documenting?
- What would you change about the review process itself so this doesn't recur?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when the failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures are where teams most often ship a fix that treats a symptom, so the first thing I'd do is resist the pressure to guess. I'd start by separating containment from correction: if the failure has field or safety impact, containment — a workaround, a usage restriction, a monitoring patch — can go out fast, but it must be labeled as containment, not as the fix, and it must not close the investigation. For the investigation itself, the core problem with intermittent failures is observability, so I'd invest early in instrumentation: logging that captures the state leading up to the failure, not just the failure itself; triggering on the anomaly rather than sampling continuously; and recording enough context (timing, temperature, supply state, recent commands) that a pattern can emerge. Then I'd work the 5 Whys and a fishbone across the usual categories — hardware, firmware, environment, user interaction, manufacturing variation — but with the explicit goal of generating *falsifiable* hypotheses rather than a list of plausible causes. Each hypothesis gets a test that could rule it out. I'd also look hard at the boundary conditions: intermittent failures often live at edges — power-up and power-down transitions, temperature extremes, timing races, marginal signal levels — so I'd deliberately stress those. Throughout, I'd keep a written log of what was tested and ruled out, because with intermittent issues the biggest risk is re-testing the same hypothesis twice. And I'd be honest with stakeholders that the timeline is uncertain until we can reproduce it, rather than promising a date we can't support.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide when you've done enough investigation to be confident the fix addresses the root cause rather than a symptom?
- What would you do if the failure rate is so low that you can't gather enough data to find a pattern?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is real: oversimplify and the firmware team makes assumptions that don't hold; over-detail and they disengage. I'd aim for a middle layer that gives them what they need to make decisions without requiring them to become analog engineers. Concretely, I'd characterize the constraint in terms of *what it means for their code*: the effective number of bits they can actually trust, the noise floor and its spectral character, the settling time after a channel switch, the sample rate that's realistic given the front end, and any known artifacts — drift with temperature, crosstalk between channels, behavior near supply rails. I'd express these as numbers with conditions attached ("at this sample rate, expect this much noise; below this signal level, the reading is dominated by noise"), because firmware decisions are usually about thresholds, averaging windows, and filtering. I'd also be explicit about what the hardware *cannot* fix and what firmware *shouldn't* try to fix — for example, if the noise is low-frequency drift, a fast digital filter won't help and may make it worse. Where it's useful, I'd provide a simple model or a reference measurement they can compare against, so they can validate their filtering against real data rather than a spec sheet. And I'd keep a short written interface document — the analog front end's behavior as seen by firmware — so the knowledge doesn't live only in a conversation. The goal is that the firmware team can reason about trade-offs themselves, not that they have to come back to me for every decision.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team proposes a filtering approach that you believe will mask a real hardware problem rather than solve it?
- What would you do if the hardware characterization isn't complete yet and firmware needs to start?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This is usually not a capability gap — it's a confidence and ownership gap, and it's often reinforced by the environment. If every design review has a senior person who answers first, the engineer learns that deferring is the safe move. So I'd work on both the person and the setting. For the person, I'd start by giving them ownership of a decision with real but bounded stakes — something where the consequences are contained and the trade-offs are genuine, so they have to form and defend a position. I'd be explicit that I want their recommendation *first*, before I offer mine, and that I'll hold back even when I disagree, so they get the experience of reasoning it through. When they do commit to a position, I'd push on the reasoning — "what would change your mind?", "what's the strongest argument against this?" — to build the habit of defending a decision rather than just stating one. I'd also make the invisible work visible: walk them through how I actually make a call under uncertainty, including the parts that don't show up in a review — what I check, what I'm willing to accept as unknown, when I'd prototype versus decide. For the setting, I'd look at whether the review culture itself is the problem: if the most senior voice always speaks first, that's a structural issue, and I'd change the format so the owner presents and defends before others weigh in. Over time the goal is that they stop treating "most senior in the room" as the decision criterion and start treating evidence and trade-offs as the criterion.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if they make a decision you disagree with, and it turns out to be wrong?
- How would you tell the difference between healthy deference and a genuine lack of confidence?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a disagreement turns personal, the technical merits stop being the deciding factor — people are defending their judgment and their standing, not the architecture. So the first move is to de-escalate the *frame*, not to pick a side. I'd acknowledge openly that both positions are credible, because if one were obviously wrong this wouldn't have gotten personal. Then I'd try to convert the dispute back into something testable. Often the two sides are actually disagreeing about *criteria* rather than about the architectures: one is optimizing for development risk, the other for field reliability, or one for cost, the other for schedule. Making those criteria explicit and getting agreement on how to weight them frequently dissolves the personal dimension, because it turns "you're wrong" into "we're optimizing for different things." Where the disagreement is genuinely empirical — will this meet the timing budget, will this fit the power envelope — I'd propose a bounded prototype or a focused analysis to settle it, with a clear definition of what result would favor which option. If a decision still can't be reached, I'd make the call myself, document the rationale and the dissenting view, and be clear that the decision is mine to own. What I would not do is let it drift, because a stalled architecture decision blocks everyone and the personal conflict gets worse the longer it runs. I'd also follow up individually with the people on the losing side, because a decision they disagree with is much easier to execute if they feel heard and understand the reasoning.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the prototype or analysis is inconclusive?
- What would you do if one of the engineers continues to undermine the decision after it's made?
- How would you document the decision so the rationale survives beyond the people in the room?
