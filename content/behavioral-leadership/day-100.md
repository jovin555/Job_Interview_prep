# behavioral-leadership — Day 100

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but consistently becomes defensive when their work is questioned, and the review needs to be genuinely critical to be useful?

**Answer:** The first move is to separate the person from the artifact before the meeting even starts. I'd have a short pre-brief with the presenter, framing the review explicitly as "the design versus the risks," not "the designer versus the reviewers" — and I'd ask them to walk me through the two or three areas they're least sure about, so they've already surfaced uncertainty in a low-stakes setting. That primes them to hear critique as expected input rather than ambush.

In the meeting itself, I'd change the mechanics of how critique is delivered. Instead of open-floor questioning, I'd structure the review around a checklist of risk categories (safety, thermal, EMC, manufacturability, testability) and have reviewers phrase findings as questions tied to a category — "Under the thermal category, what's the junction temperature margin at max ambient?" That depersonalizes the challenge because the reviewer is applying a shared standard, not judging the engineer. I'd also model the behavior myself: state a concern about my own past designs, and explicitly credit good decisions in the package so the review isn't purely negative.

If defensiveness still surfaces in the moment, I'd avoid arguing in front of the group. I'd acknowledge the point, park it as an action item to resolve with data, and follow up one-on-one afterward. The key is that the review's output is a documented set of actions and rationale, not a verdict on the engineer — and I'd reinforce that by tracking action items visibly so the process feels like forward progress rather than criticism.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if the defensive behavior persists across multiple reviews despite your coaching?
- How would you handle it if other reviewers start softening their feedback to avoid the confrontation, undermining the review's value?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** The pressure to ship fast is exactly the condition that makes a disciplined structure most valuable, because an unstructured "try things until it stops" approach usually produces a fix that masks the symptom and a false sense of closure. I'd start by resisting the urge to change anything until I've captured the failure signature: what conditions correlate with occurrence (temperature, load, timing, specific units, specific firmware state), how often it occurs, and what the observable symptom actually is. For an intermittent issue, instrumentation is the first deliverable — logging, counters, or a test harness that runs long enough to catch the event — because without a capture you're debugging from memory and anecdote.

Then I'd apply a structured method: an 8D-style flow with containment separated from root cause. Containment is what buys time under schedule pressure — for example, tightening screening, adding a watchdog or recovery path, or limiting the affected configuration — and it's explicitly labeled as temporary, not a fix. In parallel, the root-cause track uses a fishbone to enumerate candidate causes across hardware, firmware, environment, and manufacturing, then 5-whys on the most probable branches, with each hypothesis tied to a discriminating test. The discipline is that every hypothesis must be falsifiable by an experiment; if two hypotheses predict the same observation, the test isn't discriminating enough.

Verification of effectiveness is the step teams skip under pressure: after a candidate fix, I'd define in advance what evidence would prove the failure rate dropped — a stress test at the correlated conditions, a statistically meaningful run count, or a monitor left in place through field use — rather than declaring victory because it "hasn't happened since." And I'd document the reasoning trail so that if it recurs, the next investigator isn't starting from zero.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide when containment is good enough to ship while root cause is still open?
- What would you do if the failure rate is so low that proving effectiveness statistically would take months?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The goal is to hand the firmware team a model they can reason with, not a datasheet dump and not a hand-wave. I'd start from what the firmware actually needs to decide: how much of the measured signal is real, how much is noise, what the effective resolution is at the point of use, and what the timing and filtering assumptions are. So instead of saying "the ADC is 12-bit," I'd characterize the analog front end end-to-end — input-referred noise, effective number of bits under realistic conditions, the frequency content of the noise, and any drift or offset behavior — and express it in units the firmware cares about, like "at this gain, one LSB corresponds to X physical units, and the noise floor is roughly N LSBs RMS in this bandwidth."

The second piece is being explicit about the boundary of responsibility. Some noise is best handled in hardware (layout, filtering, shielding, reference stability) and some in firmware (averaging, digital filtering, oversampling). I'd document which is which and why, so the firmware team isn't trying to filter out a problem that's really a grounding issue, and the hardware team isn't assuming firmware will clean up a marginal design. A short interface control document or a shared characterization note — with measured plots, not just numbers — works better than a verbal briefing because it survives personnel changes and gets referenced during integration.

Finally, I'd keep the abstraction level matched to the audience: a one-page summary of "here's the signal quality you can expect and the assumptions behind it" for the firmware lead, with the detailed measurements available as backup. And I'd invite the firmware team to challenge the assumptions early, because they'll often spot where the model breaks down — for example, a filtering approach that assumes stationary noise when the real noise is bursty.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team proposes a digital filter that would mask a hardware problem you think should be fixed at the source?
- What would you do if the characterization data you have was taken on a bench setup that doesn't match the real operating environment?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** The default should be to resolve it within the team, because escalation that happens too readily erodes the team's ownership and turns every hard call into a management decision. But "resolve within the team" doesn't mean forcing consensus — it means running a process that produces a defensible decision. So my first question is whether the disagreement is actually about facts or about values/priorities. If it's factual — "will this approach meet the timing margin?" — the answer is usually to get data: a prototype, a calculation, a bench measurement, a simulation. Many senior-engineer disagreements dissolve once someone runs the experiment, and framing it that way depersonalizes it because both parties agreed to be bound by evidence.

If the disagreement is about priorities or risk tolerance — say, schedule versus robustness — that's a judgment call that may legitimately belong above the team, because it involves trade-offs the team doesn't own. In that case I'd still not just hand it upward raw; I'd frame the decision for my manager with the options, the trade-offs, the team's recommendation, and what's needed from them. That's escalation as a structured request for a decision, not as "these two can't agree, please fix it."

The triggers for escalating regardless of my preference: the decision has cross-team or regulatory implications beyond my scope, it's blocking a milestone and the team is deadlocked after a genuine attempt to resolve it, or the disagreement has become personal and is affecting the team's ability to work together. In those cases delaying escalation is the bigger risk. I'd also document the decision and its rationale either way, so the outcome is traceable and the losing side understands they were heard.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if your manager's decision goes against the recommendation you and the team made?
- What would you do if one of the engineers keeps re-litigating a decision after it's been made?

## Q5: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** The pattern here is usually not a capability gap but a confidence and habit gap — the engineer has learned that deferring is safe, and has never had a low-stakes opportunity to be wrong and recover. So the mentoring approach is to create graduated ownership with a safety net that's invisible to the rest of the team. I'd start by giving them a scoped decision to own outright — something with real consequences but bounded blast radius, like selecting a component within a defined spec envelope or owning a subsystem's design rationale — and make it explicit in front of the team that this decision is theirs to make and defend. Publicly assigning ownership changes the social dynamics; deferring stops being the path of least resistance.

The second element is how I show up in reviews and discussions. If I answer first, they'll never speak; so I'd deliberately hold my opinion until they've stated theirs, and when they do defer ("whatever you think"), I'd redirect with "I want your recommendation first, and then I'll tell you where I disagree." Over time that builds the muscle of forming and articulating a position. I'd also coach on the mechanics of defending a decision — how to structure the rationale, how to distinguish "I'm confident because of X" from "I'm guessing," and how to say "I don't know yet, here's how I'd find out" without losing credibility.

Finally, I'd debrief after decisions land, good or bad, focusing on the reasoning process rather than the outcome — because a good decision can have a bad outcome and vice versa, and the engineer needs to learn that ownership isn't the same as being right every time. The measure of success is when they start pushing back on me in a design discussion with a well-reasoned argument, and I can genuinely change my mind.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if a decision they owned goes badly and they retreat further into deference?
- How would you balance giving them ownership with the reality that some decisions genuinely need senior sign-off for regulatory or safety reasons?
