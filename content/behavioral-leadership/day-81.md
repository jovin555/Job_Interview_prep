# behavioral-leadership — Day 81

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct to either cancel the review or wave it through are both wrong — one stalls the milestone, the other defeats the purpose of the gate. I'd split the review into what can be decided now and what genuinely cannot. The parts of the package that are complete get reviewed normally. For the missing calculations, I'd ask the presenter to walk the reviewers through their reasoning verbally and capture it live — the goal is to surface whether the reasoning is sound, not to punish a documentation gap. If the reasoning holds up, the review can conditionally approve with a hard action item: the calculations must be written up and signed off by a named reviewer before the milestone gate closes, not after. If the reasoning is shaky or the presenter can't reconstruct it, that's a different signal — it means the decision may not have been fully reasoned in the first place, and the gate should not close on that item. I'd also treat the late package as a process problem to fix separately, not something to litigate in the meeting itself. The review is for the design; the process issue gets its own conversation.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the presenter pushes back on the conditional approval, arguing that the verbal walkthrough should be sufficient?
- What would you change in the review process so that a late, incomplete package doesn't put the milestone at risk in the first place?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures are where teams most often ship a fix that makes the symptom disappear without addressing the cause, so the first discipline is to resist that. I'd start by separating containment from correction: if the failure has field or safety impact, contain it — add a workaround, tighten a test, gate the release — while making clear that containment is not a fix. Then I'd focus on making the failure observable rather than trying to force reproduction. That usually means instrumenting the system: adding logging, capturing state at the moment of failure, widening the conditions under which the failure has been seen so the team can characterize the envelope rather than chase a single repro. I'd build a timeline of what is known versus assumed, and use a structured method — fishbone or 5 Whys — to keep the investigation from collapsing into whoever has the loudest theory. Critically, I'd define in advance what evidence would confirm or rule out each candidate cause, so the team isn't just accumulating anecdotes. Under time pressure, the temptation is to skip verification of effectiveness; I'd insist that whatever fix is shipped has a defined way to confirm it actually eliminated the failure, not just that it hasn't recurred yet.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide when to stop investigating and ship a containment fix, versus holding the release until the root cause is confirmed?
- What would you do if the instrumentation you added doesn't capture the failure because it happens too rarely to observe in a reasonable timeframe?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is real: oversimplify and the firmware team designs around an assumption that isn't true; over-detail and they disengage because it reads as a hardware problem they can't do anything about. I'd frame the constraint in terms of what it means for their code, not in terms of the hardware mechanism. So instead of explaining the noise floor of the analog front end, I'd say: at this sample rate, expect the reading to vary by roughly this much, and here's what that means for your filtering and threshold logic. I'd give them a concrete number or range they can design against, plus the conditions under which it gets worse — temperature, supply variation, nearby switching — so they know when to be more conservative. I'd also make clear which parts are fixed and which are negotiable: if the firmware team needs better resolution, is that a hardware change, a calibration step, or something they can compensate for in software? That last question is often where the real solution lives, and it only comes up if the constraint is presented as a shared problem rather than a hardware verdict. I'd follow up with a short written note so the numbers don't drift in retelling.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the firmware team proposes a software compensation that you believe masks a hardware problem rather than solving it?
- What would you do if the constraint turns out to be worse than you initially characterized, after the firmware team has already designed around your first estimate?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This pattern usually isn't a capability gap — it's a confidence and ownership gap, and it gets reinforced every time someone more senior answers for them. The mentoring move is to create low-stakes opportunities where they have to own the decision, and to resist the urge to rescue them when they hesitate. Concretely, I'd give them a scoped decision — a component selection, a trade-off between two viable approaches — with a clear expectation that they bring a recommendation and a rationale, not a question. In reviews, I'd deliberately redirect questions to them rather than answering on their behalf, and I'd be explicit about why: "This is your call to make and defend." I'd also normalize the discomfort of owning a decision by sharing how I structure my own reasoning when I'm uncertain, so they see that ownership doesn't require certainty — it requires a defensible process. Over time, the goal is for them to stop treating seniority as the source of truth and start treating evidence and reasoning as the source of truth. I'd check in periodically on how it feels, because for someone who's used to deferring, being pushed to own decisions can feel like being set up to fail unless the framing is clear.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if they make a decision you disagree with, and the stakes are high enough that you're tempted to override it?
- What signs would tell you the mentoring is working versus the engineer just learning to perform confidence without actually owning the reasoning?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** Escalation isn't a failure of leadership, but escalating too early trains the team to route every disagreement upward and erodes their ability to resolve things themselves. My default is to try to resolve it within the team first, but with a clear structure so it doesn't just become a longer argument. I'd separate the technical question from the interpersonal one: what evidence would distinguish the two positions, and can we get that evidence — a prototype, a measurement, a test — rather than continuing to debate from opinion? If the disagreement is genuinely resolvable with data, that's the path, and it usually dissolves the conflict because both engineers are arguing from the same facts. If it's not resolvable with data in the time available, or if it's really about scope, risk tolerance, or resourcing that's above the team's authority, that's when escalation is appropriate — and I'd escalate with a clear framing: here are the two positions, here's the trade-off, here's what decision is needed and by when. I'd also escalate if the disagreement has become personal and is affecting the team's ability to work, because at that point it's no longer a technical question. What I'd avoid is escalating in a way that makes one engineer look like the problem — the escalation should be about the decision, not about who's winning.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if your manager's decision goes against the position you personally thought was stronger?
- What would you do if the two engineers continue to relitigate the decision after it's been made?
