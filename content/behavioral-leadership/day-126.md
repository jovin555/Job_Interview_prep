# behavioral-leadership — Day 126

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where an engineer on your team is technically excellent but has become the de facto gatekeeper for a subsystem — every change to it routes through them, they review everything, and the team has quietly stopped proposing improvements to that area because "it's not worth the friction"?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two things that are tangled together here: the engineer's genuine expertise, which is an asset, and the bottleneck they've become, which is a risk. I'd want to understand how this happened before acting. Often it's not ego — it's that the engineer was the only person who understood the subsystem at some point, the team learned to defer, and the pattern calcified. That's a knowledge-distribution problem wearing the costume of a personality problem, and it needs a different fix.

I'd start by making the dependency visible without making it an accusation. A simple map of "who can review/approve changes in each area" usually shows the concentration starkly, and it's hard to argue with a diagram. Then I'd talk to the engineer directly, framing it around risk rather than around them: if they're on vacation, ill, or leave, what happens to that subsystem? Most strong engineers respond well to that framing because it's a real engineering concern, not a criticism.

The structural fix is deliberate redundancy. I'd pair a second engineer with them on the next few changes — not as a shadow reviewer but as a genuine co-owner, with the explicit goal that within a defined period the second person can review independently. I'd also change the review norm so that the gatekeeper reviews *design intent* and a peer reviews *implementation*, which spreads the load and forces the knowledge out. And I'd make it safe for others to propose changes by having the gatekeeper's role shift from "approve/reject" to "consult," with disagreements escalated to me rather than settled by attrition.

Throughout, I'd keep the engineer's status intact. The goal is not to demote their influence but to convert it from a single point of failure into mentorship. If they're the person who trains the next two owners of that subsystem, their expertise scales instead of bottlenecking.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer experiences the pairing arrangement as a loss of status and starts disengaging? How would you measure whether the knowledge transfer actually worked, beyond "the second engineer says they're comfortable"?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a technical problem the team is stuck on warrants bringing in outside expertise — a consultant, a specialist from another group, or a vendor's field application engineer — versus continuing to work it internally?

**Answer:** I'd frame it as a buy-versus-build decision on knowledge, and the currency is time and risk, not just money. The first question is whether the problem is on the critical path and whether the team has a credible path to solving it in the time available. If the answer is "we might get there, eventually," that's usually a signal to bring help in — the cost of a stuck critical-path problem compounds daily, and the team's morale erodes while they grind.

The second question is whether the knowledge, once obtained, needs to live inside the team afterward. If it's a one-time answer — "is this component's thermal behavior within spec at our worst-case ambient" — a consultant is fine and cheap. If it's a capability the team will need repeatedly, then bringing someone in should be structured as a transfer, not a transaction: they solve it *with* the team, document the method, and leave behind a repeatable approach. Otherwise you've rented a solution and you'll rent it again.

I'd also be honest about why the team is stuck. Sometimes it's a genuine knowledge gap; sometimes it's that the team has been circling because the problem is framed wrong, and an outside perspective breaks the frame in an hour. Those are different interventions — the first needs depth, the second needs distance. I'd try to diagnose which one I'm looking at before choosing who to bring in.

The failure mode I'd guard against is the opposite: bringing in an expert too early, before the team has done the work to understand the problem, so the expert's answer lands on unprepared ground and nobody can evaluate it. A team that has genuinely wrestled with a problem can absorb expert input; a team that hasn't will just defer to it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation where the outside expert's recommendation conflicts with the team's own analysis and the team is convinced the expert is wrong? What would you do if budget for outside help simply isn't available and the team has to solve it internally regardless?

## Q3: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's technical work is genuinely strong, but the review has drifted into a series of small stylistic preferences — naming, formatting, minor refactors — and the safety-critical and architectural decisions in the package are getting almost no scrutiny?

**Answer:** I'd intervene on the process, not on the individuals, because what's happening is a predictable failure mode: reviewers gravitate to what's easy to comment on. Naming and formatting are legible to everyone and carry no social risk to critique; "I'm not convinced this protection circuit responds fast enough under a worst-case fault" is harder to raise and harder to defend. So the review naturally fills with the easy stuff unless the structure forces otherwise.

My first move would be to reset the agenda around risk. Before the meeting, I'd ask the presenter to mark the two or three decisions in the package that are highest-consequence — the ones where being wrong is expensive or dangerous — and I'd open the review with those, with a fixed time box, before any line-level comments are allowed. If the high-risk items get the first forty minutes and the stylistic items get the last ten, the attention follows the agenda.

I'd also change the *form* of the critique. Asking "what would have to be true for this decision to be wrong?" is a much more productive prompt than "any comments on section 4?" — it gives reviewers a structured way to probe without feeling like they're attacking the presenter. And I'd explicitly legitimize raising an inarticulate concern: "I can't fully justify it yet, but this part makes me uneasy" should be a valid review comment that gets logged and followed up, not dismissed.

Finally, I'd separate the two kinds of feedback into different channels. Stylistic and naming comments can go in written form, asynchronously, and don't need meeting time at all. That preserves the meeting for the things that benefit from real-time discussion — the decisions where you need to hear someone think.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the presenter themselves prefers the review to focus on line-level detail and resists restructuring the agenda? How would you handle a reviewer who consistently uses the "what would have to be true for this to be wrong" prompt to relitigate decisions that were already settled?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a team through a period where a key architectural decision the team made collectively — and that you supported — is turning out to be wrong, and the evidence is accumulating gradually rather than arriving as one clear failure?

**Answer:** Gradual wrongness is harder than sudden wrongness, because there's no single moment that forces the issue. The team can absorb a slow drip of small workarounds and anomalies for a long time without anyone naming what's happening. My job in that situation is to name it early and to make it legitimate to say "we were wrong" — because the alternative is that the team quietly patches around the decision for months and nobody ever revisits it.

I'd start by making the accumulating evidence explicit. A running list of the anomalies, workarounds, and near-misses that all trace back to the same decision is usually more persuasive than any single argument, and it converts a vague unease into something the team can evaluate. I'd want the team to see the pattern themselves rather than hear it from me — that's the difference between a decision being revisited and a decision being overturned by the person with the most authority.

Then I'd be direct about my own role. I supported the decision; if it's wrong, that's partly on me, and saying so out loud is what gives everyone else permission to change their minds without it being a status loss. The team watches how the lead handles being wrong far more closely than they listen to what the lead says about being wrong.

On the substance, I'd want a real comparison rather than a vibe: what would it cost to change course now versus what it costs to keep patching, including the cost of the workarounds already accumulated and the ones still coming. Sometimes the honest answer is that the wrong decision is still the cheaper one to live with, and I'd want the team to reach that conclusion with data if it's true — that's a legitimate outcome, and it's different from never having asked.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation where part of the team is heavily invested in the original decision and reads the re-evaluation as a personal criticism of their earlier work? What would you do if the evidence is genuinely ambiguous — enough to worry, not enough to justify the cost of changing course?

## Q5: How would you approach a situation where you need to give critical feedback to a senior engineer — someone with more tenure and deeper domain expertise than you in a specific area — about how they're working with the rest of the team, and you expect them to receive it poorly?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd get clear on is what specifically I'm asking them to change, and why it matters. Feedback that's really a feeling — "you're difficult to work with" — will land badly no matter how it's delivered, and with a senior person it will land as an authority contest. So I'd want concrete instances: a specific review where a specific person's question was shut down, a specific decision that was made without the people it affected. Specifics are harder to dismiss and they make the conversation about behavior rather than about character.

I'd also go in genuinely curious about their side, because with a senior engineer there's often a reason I don't have full visibility into. Maybe they're under pressure from somewhere I can't see, or they've been burned by a process that wasted their time, or they genuinely believe the team moves faster when decisions don't get relitigated. I don't have to agree with the reasoning to take it seriously, and taking it seriously is what makes the conversation a conversation rather than a correction.

I'd frame the feedback around impact and around what I need, not around what's wrong with them. "When questions get shut down in review, people stop asking, and we lose the check that catches problems early — I need the review to stay genuinely open" is a statement about the team's needs that a senior engineer can act on. It doesn't require them to concede they're a difficult person.

I'd also be realistic that one conversation may not change much, and that the follow-through matters more than the conversation. I'd watch specifically for the behavior in the next few reviews and reinforce it when it improves — a quiet "that was a good catch, thanks for pushing on it" costs nothing and does more than another feedback session. And if the behavior doesn't shift and it's genuinely damaging the team, I'd need to be willing to escalate, having been clear from the start that that's where it goes. Being unwilling to escalate is what makes the first conversation hollow.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's response is that the junior people's questions were genuinely low-quality and the review is more efficient when they're filtered out? How would you handle it if the behavior improves in your presence but continues when you're not in the room?
