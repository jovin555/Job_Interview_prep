# behavioral-leadership — Day 104

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** I'd separate the two problems — the missing justification and the milestone — and handle them in that order. First, I'd open the meeting by acknowledging the package is late and that the safety-critical calculations aren't in it, so everyone is working from the same facts rather than discovering the gap mid-discussion. Then I'd ask the presenter to walk through the decision verbally and state the assumptions behind it: what margin was targeted, what worst-case conditions were considered, and what the calculation would show if it were written out. That lets the reviewers assess the reasoning even without the document, and it usually surfaces whether the gap is a documentation lapse or a genuine analytical one.

For the review itself, I'd split the outcome into two tracks. Anything that doesn't depend on the missing calculations gets reviewed and dispositioned now, so the meeting isn't wasted. The safety-critical item gets a conditional disposition: the design direction may be accepted in principle, but the gate is not cleared until the calculations are documented and independently checked. I'd name an owner and a specific date for that, and record it as an open action with a verification step — not a verbal "we'll get to it." If the milestone gate genuinely cannot move, I'd escalate that trade-off explicitly to whoever owns the gate, framing it as "we can proceed at risk with the calculation outstanding, or we hold the gate" — that's a decision for the gate owner, not something the review quietly absorbs.

The key discipline is not letting a late package become a reason to either rubber-stamp or cancel. The review's job is to produce a defensible disposition, and a conditional one with a tracked action is defensible; an unconditional pass on undocumented safety reasoning is not.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the presenter pushes back and says the calculation is straightforward and doesn't need writing up, how do you respond without turning it into a confrontation? What would you do if the milestone gate owner overrules you and clears the gate anyway?

## Q2: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument?

**Answer:** The instinct to raise a concern has to be made safe before it can be made routine. Two things kill it: concerns that get dismissed visibly, and concerns that get raised once and then quietly ignored. So I'd focus on making the response to a concern predictable and non-punitive, regardless of who raised it or how well-formed it is.

Practically, I'd do a few things. First, explicitly legitimize the half-formed concern in review norms — a standing invitation like "does anyone have an unease they can't yet articulate?" and then treating that unease as a legitimate action item ("investigate X") rather than a debate to be won on the spot. A gut feeling is often pattern recognition that hasn't been verbalized yet; the right response is to convert it into a cheap check, not to demand a proof. Second, when a concern is raised against a senior person's decision, the senior person's reaction in that moment sets the culture more than any policy — so I'd coach senior engineers specifically on receiving concerns well, and model it myself by naming my own decisions as provisional and inviting challenge. Third, I'd make it normal to record dissenting views in the decision record with rationale, so raising a concern doesn't require winning — it just requires being heard and logged. That lowers the cost of speaking up enormously.

I'd also watch for the failure mode where "raise concerns early" becomes "relitigate everything forever." The norm needs a companion: once a decision is made with the concerns documented, the team commits to it and the concern becomes a watch item, not an ongoing objection. That keeps the channel credible for the next person.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a junior engineer raises a concern and a senior engineer dismisses it in the meeting — what do you do in the room versus afterward? How do you tell the difference between a genuine gut-feeling signal worth investigating and noise that would waste the team's time?

## Q3: How would you approach leading a post-mortem after a medical device project missed its delivery deadline by several months, when morale is low and there's a tendency to blame individual contributors rather than systemic issues?

**Answer:** The first job is to set the frame before anyone speaks, because a blame-oriented post-mortem is very hard to redirect once it's underway. I'd open by stating the purpose explicitly: we're here to understand how the system allowed this outcome, not to identify who to punish — and I'd mean it, which means I don't let the discussion settle on "engineer X missed their dates" as a root cause. An individual missing a date is a data point; the question is what made the schedule depend on that one person's date with no buffer or early warning.

I'd structure it in layers. Start with a factual timeline reconstructed from records rather than memory — milestone dates, when risks were first raised, when they were escalated, what decisions were made and on what information. Then work backward: for each slip, ask what signal existed earlier and why it didn't translate into action. That naturally surfaces systemic causes — estimates with no unknowns identified, risk that was known but not communicated upward, a gate that was passed on optimism, a dependency on a single person. I'd use a structured method (5 whys or a fishbone across people/process/tooling/requirements) to keep it from becoming a free-form grievance session.

On morale: I'd acknowledge the frustration genuinely and early, and I'd be explicit that the output is a set of corrective actions with owners — not a verdict. People accept a hard post-mortem far better when it produces changes they can see. I'd also make sure the actions target the system (estimation practice, risk review cadence, escalation norms) rather than singling out individuals, and I'd follow up on those actions publicly so the exercise doesn't feel like theater.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the factual timeline shows that a specific engineer did in fact conceal a slipping date — how do you handle that without turning the post-mortem into a tribunal? How do you make sure the corrective actions from a post-mortem actually get implemented rather than filed away?

## Q4: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The goal is to hand the firmware team a model they can reason with, not a datasheet dump and not a hand-wave. I'd frame it as three things: what the constraint is in physical terms, what it means for the numbers they'll see in software, and what design freedom they still have.

Concretely, for something like a noisy analog front end, I'd give them the signal chain budget in units they care about — the effective resolution at the point of conversion after noise, not just the ADC's nominal bits — plus the noise character (is it broadband, is it correlated with a switching supply, does it drift with temperature). Then I'd translate that into firmware-relevant consequences: what averaging or filtering can and cannot recover, what the realistic noise floor is per sample, and where the constraint interacts with their timing (for example, if a low-power sleep mode changes settling behavior on wake-up, that's a firmware-visible effect of a hardware reality). I'd also state explicitly what the hardware team owns versus what firmware owns, so nobody assumes the other side is absorbing the error.

The "don't bury them" part is about layering: a one-page summary of the constraint and its consequences up front, with the detailed measurements and derivations available as backup for whoever wants to verify. And I'd invite them to push back — if firmware proposes a filtering approach, I want to check it against the actual noise model rather than let a plausible-sounding filter get built on a wrong assumption. The measure of success is that firmware can make a design decision without coming back to ask me what the hardware "really" does.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team's proposed filtering approach would work on paper but interacts badly with a hardware behavior they don't know about? How do you keep that shared model current when the hardware design changes during development?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding whether a technical disagreement between two senior engineers should be escalated to your manager, versus resolved within the team?

**Answer:** My default is to resolve it within the team, because escalation that could have been avoided erodes the team's ownership and trains people to route around each other. But "resolve within the team" doesn't mean "keep talking until consensus" — it means I take responsibility for driving to a decision, and escalation is one of the tools available if the team genuinely can't.

I'd first diagnose why it's stuck. Often a senior-vs-senior disagreement is not actually about the technical merits but about missing data — neither side can prove their case, so it becomes a matter of opinion and status. In that case the right move is to get data: a prototype, a bench measurement, a calculation, a targeted test. A disagreement that can be settled by evidence should be, and that's squarely within the team's authority. If the disagreement is genuinely a judgment call under uncertainty — both options defensible, trade-offs real — then I'd structure the decision: state the criteria explicitly (safety, schedule, cost, risk), have each side argue against their own position briefly, and then make or assign the decision with the rationale documented. That's still a team-level resolution.

I'd escalate when the decision exceeds the team's authority — it commits budget, changes a regulatory posture, or crosses into another team's ownership — or when the disagreement has become personal enough that a neutral party is needed to break the dynamic, or when it's blocking a milestone and the team has demonstrably exhausted its own options. When I do escalate, I'd escalate the decision and the trade-offs, not "these two people can't agree" — I'd bring my manager a clear framing of the options and my recommendation, so the escalation is a decision request rather than a complaint.

**Possible follow-ups:** If you escalate and your manager makes a call you disagree with, how do you handle that with the team? How would you tell the difference between a disagreement that needs more data and one that's genuinely a judgment call that no amount of data will settle?
