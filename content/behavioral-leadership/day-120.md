# behavioral-leadership — Day 120

## Q1: How would you approach leading a design review where the presenter's technical work is solid, but the review package is organized so that the highest-risk decisions are buried in appendices — and reviewers are likely to approve without engaging with them?

**Answer:** The first move is to recognize that this is a review-process problem, not a presenter-competence problem, and to fix it before the meeting rather than during it. When I receive a package, I scan it against a simple test: can a reviewer who reads only the main body identify the top three riskiest decisions and the evidence behind each? If not, I ask the presenter to restructure before the review — surface the risk register and the safety-critical decisions into the main body, with the supporting analysis in appendices referenced from there. Framing it as "reviewers won't find your good work if it's buried" tends to land better than "you've hidden the risky stuff."

In the meeting itself, I steer the agenda by risk rather than by document order. I open with the decisions that carry the most consequence — safety functions, worst-case tolerance analysis, anything with a regulatory hook — and explicitly ask the room to engage with those first. I also model the behavior I want: asking "what would have to be true for this to be wrong?" rather than "does this look OK?" A review that only asks for approval will get approval; a review that asks for disconfirmation gets engagement.

Afterward, I'd treat it as a process improvement: agree a lightweight package template with a mandatory "key decisions and risks" section up front, so this doesn't depend on me personally catching it every time. The goal is that the review's structure forces attention onto risk, independent of who's presenting.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the presenter pushes back and says the appendices are where the detail belongs, how do you handle that without turning it into a documentation-style argument?
- How would you tell whether the review actually engaged with the risk, versus just moved the risk section to the front and approved it anyway?

## Q2: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is consistently high quality, but they have a habit of quietly redefining the scope of their own tasks — delivering something different from what was agreed — and the drift only becomes visible when it disrupts someone downstream?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating two things that often get conflated: the quality of the work and the reliability of the commitment. The person is delivering good engineering, but they're delivering against a definition only they hold, and that's a coordination failure that will keep causing downstream pain no matter how good the individual output is. I'd want to understand the cause before deciding the response — sometimes this is genuine over-engineering, sometimes it's someone spotting a real problem mid-task and fixing it without realizing the fix changes an interface someone else depends on, and sometimes it's discomfort with raising a scope question because they expect it to be refused.

The conversation would be specific and non-accusatory: here's what was agreed, here's what was delivered, here's the downstream effect. Then I'd ask what prompted the change — because if there was a legitimate reason, the process failure is that it wasn't surfaced, not that the change was made. The fix is usually a lightweight checkpoint: for tasks with downstream dependencies, a short "interface/scope confirmation" before work goes deep, and an explicit norm that changing scope is fine but changing it silently is not. I'd also make it easy to raise scope questions early by being clear that "this is bigger than we thought, here are the options" is a welcome message, not a failure.

I'd watch whether the pattern is about this individual or about the team's working agreements — if several people are drifting, the definition of "done" and the interface contracts are probably too vague, and that's my problem to fix, not theirs.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the person's redefinitions were actually improvements, and the downstream disruption was because the original agreement was wrong?
- What would you do if the behavior continued after the conversation, and the work quality remained high?

## Q3: How would you approach leading a technical decision when two senior engineers have each produced a fully documented, defensible design for the same subsystem, and both are refusing to concede — not because the data is ambiguous, but because each has become personally invested in their own proposal?

**Answer:** When two designs are both defensible, the disagreement has usually stopped being about the designs and started being about identity — each engineer now has something to lose by conceding. My job is to change the question from "whose design wins" to "what does the decision actually depend on," and to make the criteria explicit and shared before anyone argues their case again.

Concretely, I'd get both engineers to agree, in writing, on the decision criteria and their weights: safety and regulatory risk first, then reliability and manufacturability, then cost, schedule, and team familiarity. That reframes the conversation around scoring against agreed criteria rather than defending a position. Where the criteria don't settle it — and with two genuinely defensible designs they often won't — I'd look for a cheap way to generate evidence: a prototype, a bench measurement, a design review by someone outside the dispute, or a targeted analysis of the specific failure modes each design handles differently. Data doesn't resolve personal investment, but it does give both engineers a face-saving path to converge on something other than "I was wrong."

If it still doesn't resolve, I'd make the call myself, document the rationale and the dissenting view, and be explicit that the decision is mine to own — which lets both engineers stop defending their proposals without either feeling they lost to the other. I'd also follow up individually afterward, because the risk in these situations is that the "loser" disengages from the chosen design, and I need their full engagement on execution regardless of whose proposal was picked.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How do you make sure the criteria you set aren't perceived as rigged toward one engineer's design?
- What would you do if the engineer whose design wasn't chosen continued to raise objections to the chosen design during implementation?

## Q4: How would you approach building a culture where engineers raise concerns about a design decision early — even when the decision came from a senior person or a team consensus — and even when the concern is only a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated argument?

**Answer:** The instinct to suppress a half-formed concern is rational: people learn quickly that an unarticulated worry gets dismissed, or worse, that raising it costs credibility. So the culture change has to make early, imprecise concerns cheap to raise and genuinely useful to the person who raised them — otherwise no amount of encouragement will overcome the learned cost.

I'd start by modeling it from the top. When I have a gut feeling about a design, I'd say so explicitly — "I can't fully justify this yet, but something about the thermal path worries me; can we spend ten minutes on it?" — which demonstrates that an inarticulate concern is a legitimate contribution and shows how to convert it into something checkable. I'd also make it structurally easy: a standing "concerns and unknowns" item in design reviews where anyone can log a worry without needing to defend it, and a norm that a logged concern gets a named owner and a date to either investigate or consciously dismiss it. That last part matters — people stop raising concerns when they watch them disappear into silence.

I'd also protect the messenger explicitly. If a concern turns out to be unfounded, thank the person for raising it and be visible about it; if a concern about a senior person's decision turns out to be right, make sure the credit lands with the person who raised it, not with the decision-maker who "adjusted." And I'd be honest that a gut feeling is a hypothesis, not a verdict — the culture I want is one where gut feelings get investigated cheaply, not one where they automatically override data.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if a particular engineer started raising so many low-confidence concerns that reviews were slowing down?
- What would you do if a senior engineer's reaction to being questioned — even gently — was visibly discouraging to junior team members, despite your stated norms?

## Q5: How would you approach deciding whether to take on a stretch technical assignment yourself — something outside your current depth — versus delegating it to someone on the team with more relevant expertise but less seniority, when the outcome is high-visibility and you're accountable either way?

**Answer:** I'd frame it as a risk-allocation decision rather than a pride decision, and be honest that the pull toward taking it myself is often about visibility and control rather than about what's best for the outcome. The questions I'd ask: what does the assignment actually need — deep expertise in the specific area, or judgment and accountability across the whole? What's the cost of getting it wrong, and is that cost recoverable? And what does the team need from this assignment in terms of capability building?

If the assignment's difficulty is concentrated in a domain where someone on the team is genuinely deeper than me, delegating is usually right — but "delegating" doesn't mean stepping back. I'd stay accountable by defining the decision points where I want to be involved, agreeing how we'll know it's on track, and making sure the person has air cover and access to whatever expertise they need. The high-visibility nature actually argues for delegation with strong support, because it gives a less-senior engineer a genuine growth opportunity with a safety net, and it puts the deepest expertise where the hardest problems are.

If I do take it myself, I'd be explicit about why — usually because the assignment needs cross-team judgment or accountability that can't be delegated, or because the timeline doesn't allow for someone to ramp up — and I'd pair with the deeper expert on the team so I'm not learning in isolation. Either way, I'd tell the team the reasoning, because "the lead took the interesting one" and "the lead delegated the interesting one" both get read as signals about what the organization values, and I want that signal to be deliberate.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If you delegate it and the person struggles, at what point do you step in, and how do you do that without undermining them?
- How would you handle it if the person with the relevant expertise is less senior but also less interested in the visibility and accountability that come with the assignment?
