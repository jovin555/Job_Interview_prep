# behavioral-leadership — Day 34

## Q1: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a sensor hardware failure or a firmware filtering algorithm error, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd start by establishing a structured investigation framework before any conclusions are drawn. The first step is containment — ensuring patient safety isn't at risk while the investigation proceeds, which might mean issuing clinical guidance or restricting use. Then I'd form a small cross-functional team with representation from both hardware and firmware, and we'd jointly define what evidence would definitively distinguish between the two hypotheses. Rather than asking "which team is right," we'd ask "what data would prove or disprove each failure mode?" For the sensor hardware hypothesis, that might mean examining returned units, reviewing manufacturing test data, and checking solder joint quality or component stress. For the firmware hypothesis, it would involve reviewing the filtering algorithm's edge cases, examining ADC sample timing, and looking for conditions where the algorithm could produce the observed symptom. I'd push for a fishbone diagram to map all potential contributing factors systematically, then use 5-whys to drill into the most plausible branches. Critically, I'd insist on collecting field data — actual device logs, environmental conditions, usage patterns — before making any design changes. The temptation is to fix whichever cause seems most likely, but in medical devices, implementing the wrong corrective action can be worse than no action because it creates false confidence. I'd also document every step of the investigation in a format suitable for the design history file, since this type of investigation often becomes part of the regulatory record.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle the situation if one team becomes defensive and resists participating in the joint investigation?
- What specific data would you want to collect from field devices to discriminate between the two hypotheses?

## Q2: How would you approach managing a situation where a senior engineer on your team consistently delivers excellent technical work but has a pattern of making design decisions unilaterally, and this has caused rework and frustration among other team members?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a communication and process issue rather than a technical competence issue. First, I'd have a private conversation with the engineer to understand their perspective — they may not realize the impact of their behavior, or they may have a legitimate reason for moving quickly (such as feeling that design reviews are too slow or that their decisions are routinely accepted anyway). I'd frame the conversation around outcomes rather than blame: "When decisions are made without the team's input, we lose the opportunity to catch issues early, and it creates rework that slows everyone down." I'd then work with them to identify specific situations where unilateral decisions are appropriate versus those that require team input — for example, a minor component substitution might be fine, but a change to the system architecture or a safety-related design choice should go through review. I'd also look at whether the underlying process is enabling this behavior. If design reviews are scheduled too late or don't have clear decision points, the engineer may feel forced to move ahead. I'd consider establishing a lightweight design decision record — a simple template documenting the decision, alternatives considered, and rationale — that makes the process explicit without adding heavy overhead. If the behavior continues, I'd escalate through progressive coaching, but I'd also check whether other team members are raising concerns directly with this person or only with me, because part of the fix is building a culture where peer feedback is normal and expected.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if this engineer is also the most technically knowledgeable person on the team, and other engineers defer to them even when they disagree?
- What would you do if the engineer responds defensively and says the team's input isn't valuable because they've already considered all the alternatives?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a system-level problem that needs a test strategy addressing both concerns simultaneously, rather than letting each team optimize for their own objective. The firmware team's goal is legitimate — extended battery life is a real clinical benefit, especially for a device meant to be worn continuously. The hardware team's concern is also valid — analog sensors often need settling time after power rails stabilize, and a poorly designed wake-up sequence could produce corrupted readings that look like valid data. The test strategy should focus on the wake-up transition as a critical event, not just steady-state operation. I'd propose a three-phase approach. First, characterization testing: instrument the device to measure power rail settling times, sensor output stability, and ADC readings during wake-up under various conditions (different battery voltages, temperatures, and sleep durations). This gives us data on whether the concern is real and how much margin exists. Second, stress testing: deliberately vary the wake-up timing, add noise injection on power rails, and test with marginal batteries to find the failure boundary. Third, long-duration validation: run the device through thousands of sleep/wake cycles while logging sensor data, looking for intermittent anomalies that might not appear in short tests. I'd also want the firmware team to implement a "data validity" flag — a mechanism where the system knows when the sensor has stabilized and can discard or flag readings taken during the transition. The test strategy should verify that this flag works correctly under all conditions. Finally, I'd document the test results in a format that supports the regulatory submission, since this type of power management feature will need evidence of safety and effectiveness.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide how many sleep/wake cycles are sufficient for the long-duration validation?
- What would you do if characterization testing shows that the sensor needs significantly longer settling time than the firmware team's power budget allows?

## Q4: How would you approach building a culture of structured root-cause analysis on a team that currently treats investigations as a quick fix-and-move-on exercise, without documenting the analysis or verifying that the fix actually addresses the root cause?

**Answer:** I'd start by understanding why the current culture exists. In many cases, teams skip structured analysis because they're under pressure to resolve issues quickly, or because they've seen quick fixes work often enough that they've developed a false sense of confidence. The key is to make structured analysis feel like it's helping the team, not adding bureaucracy. I'd begin by introducing a lightweight version of 8D or 5-whys on a real, recent issue — not a hypothetical one — and walk through it with the team, showing how it reveals insights that the quick fix missed. The goal is to demonstrate value, not to mandate process. I'd also focus on the distinction between containment and corrective action. Teams often implement a containment fix (stopping the immediate problem) and call it done, without asking whether the underlying cause is still present. I'd make this distinction explicit in every investigation: "What have we done to stop the bleeding, and what are we doing to prevent recurrence?" Over time, I'd build simple templates and checklists that make the process easy to follow, and I'd recognize team members who do thorough investigations rather than just quick fixes. I'd also connect the documentation to something the team already cares about — if they've ever been frustrated by re-investigating the same issue twice, or by not understanding why a previous fix was made, that's the motivation for documenting root cause analysis properly. Finally, I'd verify effectiveness as a standard step: after a fix is implemented, we check that the problem doesn't recur, and we document that verification. This closes the loop and builds confidence in the process.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where a team member finds the structured process too time-consuming and resists using it?
- How would you measure whether the culture shift is actually working?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a design review for a medical device PCB when you discover, mid-review, that a critical safety-related trace width calculation was never documented, and the original designer is no longer with the company?

**Answer:** I'd stop the review at that point and address the documentation gap directly, because continuing to review other aspects of the design while a safety-critical calculation is missing would undermine the entire review's integrity. The first step is to determine what we actually know: is the trace width physically present on the board? Can we measure it? What current is it expected to carry, and what's the safety margin required? I'd work with the remaining team to reconstruct the calculation from first principles — trace width for a given current is determined by IPC-2221 or similar standards, considering copper thickness, temperature rise, and ambient temperature. We can also look at the design files to see what trace width was actually used, and we can check whether there are any related documents (emails, meeting notes, older revisions) that might contain the original rationale. If we can reconstruct the calculation and it meets the safety requirements, I'd document it properly and note that it was reconstructed post-hoc. If we can't verify it, or if the calculation shows the trace is undersized, that's a design non-conformance that needs to be addressed — either by redesigning the trace or by documenting a risk assessment explaining why the current design is acceptable. I'd also use this as a trigger to review other safety-related calculations in the design to see if there are similar gaps. The broader lesson is that the design history file needs to be self-sufficient — if a designer leaves, their knowledge shouldn't leave with them. I'd recommend a process where critical calculations are reviewed and documented as part of the design phase, not as an afterthought.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you prioritize which other calculations to verify if you suspect there may be more undocumented decisions?
- How would you handle the situation if the reconstructed calculation shows the trace is undersized for the required current?
