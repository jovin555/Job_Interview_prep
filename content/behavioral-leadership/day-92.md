# behavioral-leadership — Day 92

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter's package is technically solid but arrives late and is missing the calculations that justify a safety-critical decision, and the meeting is the only slot before a milestone gate?

**Answer:** The instinct is to either wave it through because the work is good, or cancel the review because the package is incomplete — both are wrong. The milestone gate exists precisely so that safety-critical rationale is reviewed, not assumed. I would open the meeting by naming the situation plainly: the design appears sound, but the package is missing the calculations that let reviewers independently verify a safety-critical choice, and a review that can't verify isn't a review. Then I'd split the meeting into two parts. First, walk the reviewers through what *is* there — architecture, interfaces, the reasoning the presenter can articulate verbally — and capture concerns and questions. Second, explicitly scope what cannot be dispositioned today: the missing calculations become a blocking action item with an owner and a date, and the review is recorded as "conditionally complete pending verification of X." The milestone gate is not passed until that action closes. The key move is separating "the design is probably fine" from "the design has been reviewed," because conflating them is how undocumented safety decisions slip into a design history file. I'd also make clear to the presenter that this isn't a judgment on their engineering — it's the process working as intended — and offer to help them get the calculations into a form reviewers can check quickly, so the follow-up review is short.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the milestone owner pushes back and says the schedule can't absorb a second review, how do you hold the line without becoming the blocker everyone routes around? What would you do if the missing calculation later shows the design was actually marginal?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?

**Answer:** Intermittent failures under schedule pressure are where teams most often skip straight to a plausible fix and declare victory, so the first job is to resist that without stalling progress. I'd structure it in three parallel tracks. Track one is containment: what do we do *right now* to limit exposure — tighten screening, add a monitor, restrict the affected configuration — so the pressure to "just fix it" is partly relieved and the investigation isn't being rushed by fear. Track two is characterization: since we can't reproduce on demand, we instrument. Add logging, capture the conditions when it does occur (temperature, supply state, timing, load), and treat every occurrence as data rather than an annoyance. The goal is to convert an intermittent into a statistically characterized event before proposing a cause. Track three is hypothesis discipline: build a short list of candidate causes from a fishbone-style pass, then design cheap experiments that discriminate between them rather than experiments that confirm a favorite. I'd explicitly separate "we have a fix" from "we have verified the fix addresses the root cause," and require the latter — including a defined observation window — before closing. The honest communication piece is telling stakeholders that an intermittent with no reproduction path has an irreducible uncertainty, and giving them a containment-based interim posture rather than a false "fixed" date.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide when you've characterized the failure enough to stop investigating and ship a containment-only fix? What's your threshold for declaring a fix verified when the failure rate is low enough that absence of recurrence proves little?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?

**Answer:** The failure mode in both directions is the same: the firmware team ends up either not believing the constraint matters, or not knowing what to *do* about it. So I'd translate the constraint into three things they can use. First, the *budget*: not "the ADC is 12-bit" but "here is the effective number of bits you actually get after noise, and here is the resulting resolution in the physical unit you care about, at the sample rates you'll use." That reframes a hardware spec as a firmware-relevant number. Second, the *mechanism*: a short, concrete explanation of where the noise comes from and how it behaves — is it broadband, is it correlated with a switching supply, does it change with temperature — because firmware mitigations (averaging, filtering, sampling away from switching edges, oversampling) depend on the noise's character, not just its magnitude. Third, the *options and their costs*: what firmware can do about it and what each costs in latency, CPU, or power, so the trade-off is theirs to make with full information. I'd back it with a measurement they can reproduce — a capture or a plot — so it's evidence, not assertion. The discipline is to stop at "here's what you need to decide and the data to decide it," rather than prescribing the filter, because the firmware team owns the timing and power consequences.

**Possible follow-ups:** What do you do when the firmware team's proposed mitigation would work but eats latency the system can't afford — who owns that trade-off? How would you handle it if, after all that, the firmware team still treats the constraint as a hardware problem to be fixed on your side?

## Q4: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?

**Answer:** This pattern usually isn't a capability gap — it's that the engineer has never been given a decision with real stakes and no senior backstop, so deferring has always been the safe move. The mentoring approach is to create graduated ownership with explicit authority. I'd start by handing them a decision that is genuinely theirs: define the scope, the constraints, and the criteria for a good outcome, then state clearly that the call is theirs to make and that I will back it even if I'd have chosen differently. The critical part is actually not overriding them — the first time a mentor overrides a delegated decision "just to be safe," the engineer learns the delegation wasn't real. I'd pair that with a structured decision write-up: they document the options, the trade-offs, the choice, and the rationale, which forces end-to-end thinking and gives us something concrete to review together. In meetings, I'd deliberately redirect "what do you think?" questions that come to me back to them, and coach them on how to state a position with its uncertainty rather than waiting for permission. Over time I'd increase the stakes and the visibility of the decisions. The measure of success is that they start disagreeing with me in the room, with reasons — that's when the deference has actually broken.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you handle the case where a delegated decision goes badly — do you protect them publicly, and how do you debrief it without re-teaching them that deferring was safer? How do you tell the difference between healthy deference to experience and a confidence problem that needs a different approach?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical decision when the team is split between two viable architectures and the disagreement has become personal rather than technical, with each side attributing bad motives to the other?

**Answer:** Once a technical disagreement turns personal, more technical argument usually makes it worse, because each side reads new evidence as ammunition rather than information. So the first move is to change the frame, not add data. I'd separate the people from the positions: acknowledge publicly that both architectures are legitimate and that the disagreement is a real trade-off, which removes the implication that one side is being unreasonable. Then I'd try to convert the argument into explicit, agreed criteria — what does "better" mean for this product, weighted how — because most personal disputes are actually disputes about which criteria matter, left unstated. If the two sides can agree on the criteria and still disagree on the answer, that's a genuine trade-off and the decision can be made on the record with the losing side's concerns documented. If they can't agree on criteria, that's the real problem to solve, and it's mine to facilitate. Where feasible, I'd propose a cheap discriminating experiment — a prototype or a focused evaluation — so the decision rests on evidence both sides helped generate rather than on whose argument won. Throughout, I'd keep the disagreement in the technical register, name the dynamic if it persists ("this is starting to sound like we're questioning each other's judgment, let's get back to the criteria"), and make the final decision transparent with its rationale, so the outcome is a documented choice rather than a defeat.

**Possible follow-ups:** If you make the call and the losing side is visibly disengaged afterward, what do you do to bring them back in? At what point does a disagreement that stays personal become a performance or behavior issue rather than a technical one, and how do you handle that transition?
