# behavioral-leadership — Day 128

## Q1: How would you approach a situation where a design review has become a rubber stamp — reviewers defer to a respected senior presenter, and the risky parts of the design are never actually probed?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the *social* problem from the *process* problem, because a review that has gone quiet is usually a symptom of both. Socially, deference to a senior presenter is natural — people don't want to challenge someone with more tenure and domain depth, and they may fear looking uninformed. So I wouldn't frame the fix as "you should push back more"; I'd change the structure so that scrutiny is expected of everyone rather than being a personal act of courage. Concretely, I'd introduce a few mechanisms: assign specific reviewers to specific risk areas ahead of time (so someone owns "the safety-critical calculation" and someone else owns "the failure-mode analysis"), require the presenter to explicitly flag the two or three highest-risk decisions in the package and state what they want challenged, and use a short written pre-review where reviewers submit questions before the meeting so the discussion starts from prepared concerns rather than a cold read. I'd also model the behavior myself — asking genuine questions about the risky parts, and being visibly comfortable when my own work is questioned. The goal is to make probing the norm rather than an exception, and to give quieter reviewers a low-friction channel to raise concerns. Over time, if the review is genuinely adding value, the culture shifts on its own.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the senior presenter themselves resists the new structure — for example, sees assigned scrutiny as a sign of distrust? And how would you tell whether the change is actually working, versus reviewers just going through the motions of asking prepared questions?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a technical problem the team is stuck on warrants bringing in outside expertise — a consultant, a specialist from another group, or a vendor's field application engineer — versus continuing to work it internally?
**Answer:** I'd frame it as a cost-of-delay versus cost-of-humility trade-off, and try to make the decision on evidence rather than pride. The signals that push me toward outside help are: the team has been stuck long enough that the schedule is genuinely at risk; the problem sits in a domain where the team has no depth and no realistic path to build it in the time available; the team has cycled through the same hypotheses without new information; or the problem is one where a vendor or specialist has seen the failure mode many times before and can shortcut the diagnosis. The signals that push me to keep it internal are: the problem is well-scoped and the team is making measurable progress, just slowly; the knowledge would be valuable to retain; or bringing someone in would create a dependency the team can't sustain. I'd also distinguish *diagnosis* from *ownership* — bringing in a specialist to help diagnose doesn't mean handing over the fix or the accountability. If I do bring someone in, I'd scope it tightly (a specific question, a defined time box), prepare them properly with the evidence the team has gathered, and make sure the team is in the room learning rather than watching from outside. The worst outcome is paying for expertise and not absorbing it.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you manage the team's morale if bringing in an outside expert is perceived as a vote of no confidence in their ability? And how would you decide when to stop the engagement if the outside expert isn't making progress either?

## Q3: How would you approach a situation where a team member's work is consistently high quality, but they have a habit of quietly redefining the scope of their own tasks — delivering something different from what was agreed — and the drift only becomes visible when it disrupts someone downstream?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a communication and alignment problem first, and only escalate to a performance conversation if the pattern persists after clear feedback. The key is to understand *why* the drift is happening — sometimes it's genuine over-engineering or perfectionism, sometimes it's a well-intentioned attempt to solve a problem they spotted, and sometimes it's a mismatch between what they thought was agreed and what was actually agreed. Those have different fixes. I'd start with a direct, non-accusatory conversation: describe the specific instances where the delivered work diverged from the agreed scope, describe the downstream impact concretely, and ask what was going on from their side. Then I'd tighten the process around scope changes — not to punish them, but to make the drift visible earlier. That could mean a short written definition of "done" for each task, a lightweight check-in at the midpoint of longer tasks, or an explicit norm that "if you think the scope should change, raise it before you build it, not after." I'd also want to make sure the downstream teams have a clear channel to flag when they receive something unexpected, so the drift surfaces at the point of handoff rather than weeks later. The goal is to preserve the quality of their work while making their output predictable to everyone else.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the team member's redefinitions are actually *improvements* — they're delivering something better than what was agreed, but still disrupting the plan? And how would you distinguish this from a situation where the original scope was simply poorly defined and the drift is a symptom of that?

## Q4: How would you approach leading a technical decision when a proposed design change would improve performance but requires re-qualifying a component already approved in the design history file, and the schedule has no slack?
**Answer:** I'd start by making the trade-off explicit rather than letting it be decided by whoever is most persistent. The first question is whether the performance improvement is *necessary* — does it address a requirement, a safety concern, or a known field issue — or is it a nice-to-have? If it's a nice-to-have, the answer is usually to defer it to the next revision and document the decision, because re-qualification without schedule slack is a real risk to the whole project. If it's necessary, then the conversation shifts to *how* to absorb the re-qualification cost, and that's a cross-functional decision, not a purely technical one. I'd want the regulatory and quality leads in the room early, because they can tell me what re-qualification actually requires — sometimes it's less onerous than the team assumes, sometimes it's more. I'd also look for ways to de-risk: can the change be qualified in parallel with other work, can a partial qualification cover the risk, is there a fallback if the re-qualification fails? And I'd want the decision documented with its rationale, including the option that was rejected, so that if the schedule slips later there's a clear record of why the change was taken on. The worst outcome is making the change informally, failing to re-qualify in time, and having neither the performance improvement nor a clean DHF.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you communicate the schedule risk to a product manager who is already under pressure to hit the original date? And if the re-qualification does fail, what would your contingency plan look like?

## Q5: How would you approach a situation where a team has quietly stopped escalating problems early — not because anyone decided to, but because past experience taught them that raising an issue early gets the messenger blamed or handed extra work?
**Answer:** This is one of the harder cultural problems because it's self-reinforcing — the behavior is rational from each individual's perspective, even though it's damaging collectively. I'd start by acknowledging that the team's caution is a *response to real experience*, not a character flaw, and that telling them to "just speak up" won't work if the underlying incentive hasn't changed. So the first move is to change what happens *after* someone raises a concern: make it visible that the person who flagged the issue is credited, not blamed, and that raising a problem early doesn't automatically mean they own the fix. That might mean explicitly separating "who found it" from "who fixes it," publicly thanking people who surface risks early, and being careful about how I react in the moment — if my first response to bad news is visible frustration, the lesson lands regardless of what I say afterward. I'd also look at the mechanics: is there a low-friction channel to raise a concern without it becoming a formal escalation? Are there forums where risks are reviewed routinely, so raising one isn't an unusual act? And I'd want to check my own behavior — am I modeling the behavior I'm asking for, including raising my own uncertainties early? Culture change here is slow and mostly about repeated evidence that the new behavior is safe, so I'd expect to be consistent about it for a long time before the team's default shifts.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a specific case where someone *did* raise a concern early and it turned out to be a false alarm — how do you respond in a way that doesn't discourage future raising? And how would you measure whether the culture is actually changing, given that the absence of escalations could mean either "no problems" or "problems being hidden"?
