# behavioral-leadership — Day 68

## Q1: How would you approach a design review where the presenter is technically strong but has a history of becoming defensive when their work is questioned, and the review needs to be genuinely critical to be useful?
**Answer:** The core problem is that the review's value depends on the presenter feeling safe enough to hear hard feedback, so I'd treat the format and framing as the intervention rather than trying to "manage" the person's temperament in the moment. Before the review, I'd set expectations explicitly with the presenter: the goal is to find problems now, when they're cheap to fix, not to grade the design or the designer. I'd ask them to walk through the areas they're least confident about first, which reframes vulnerability as professional rigor rather than weakness. During the review, I'd model the behavior I want — asking questions rather than making pronouncements ("what happens to the analog front end if the supply droops during wake-up?" rather than "this is wrong"), and separating the design from the designer in language. I'd also deliberately invite critique of my own past decisions if relevant, so the norm is that everything on the table is fair game. If a reviewer's comment lands badly, I'd redirect to the technical substance: "let's capture that as an action item and look at the data." Afterward, I'd follow up privately to reinforce that the review surfaced real issues and that's a good outcome, and I'd track action items visibly so the presenter sees the process producing value rather than just criticism. Over time, the pattern that tends to work is consistency: if reviews are always conducted this way, defensiveness has less to latch onto.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the defensiveness persists across multiple reviews despite your efforts? How would you handle a situation where the presenter's defensiveness is actually justified because a particular reviewer is consistently harsh or personal?

## Q2: How would you approach structuring a root-cause investigation when a failure is intermittent, cannot be reproduced on demand, and the team is under pressure to ship a fix quickly?
**Answer:** Intermittent failures are the hardest case because the pressure to ship pushes teams toward guessing, and a guess that "seems to work" is worse than no fix at all — it closes the investigation prematurely and leaves the real cause in the field. My first move is to resist the pressure explicitly and reframe the goal: we're not looking for a fix, we're looking for a mechanism. I'd start by characterizing the failure as precisely as possible from whatever evidence exists — field logs, customer reports, environmental conditions, time-to-failure distributions, whether it correlates with temperature, load, specific units, or specific usage patterns. Even without reproduction, patterns often narrow the search space dramatically. In parallel, I'd set up instrumentation to catch the failure when it happens: extended logging, stress testing that pushes toward the suspected conditions, and if feasible, a test rig that runs the suspect scenario continuously. I'd apply structured methods — fishbone to enumerate candidate causes across hardware, firmware, environment, and use, then 5 whys on the most plausible branches — and I'd be explicit about which hypotheses are testable and which aren't. Containment is separate from correction: if the field risk is real, I'd implement a containment action (a conservative workaround, a usage restriction, additional monitoring) while the investigation continues, and I'd be clear with stakeholders that containment is not a root-cause fix. I'd also document the investigation as it goes, because intermittent issues often get "solved" by a change that happens to correlate with the failure stopping, and without documentation you can't tell correlation from causation. Verification of effectiveness matters more here than anywhere: the fix has to be validated against the original failure mode, not just observed to not fail for a while.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when to stop investigating and ship a containment-only fix? What would you do if the failure stops occurring after a change that you can't explain mechanistically?

## Q3: How would you approach translating a hardware constraint — such as limited ADC resolution or a noisy analog front end — into terms the firmware team can act on, without either oversimplifying or burying them in detail?
**Answer:** The failure mode on both sides is familiar: oversimplify and the firmware team makes assumptions that don't hold ("just average the samples"), over-detail and they disengage because it looks like a hardware problem they can't influence. What works is translating the constraint into the firmware team's own vocabulary — what it means for their code, their timing, their filtering, their calibration — while keeping the underlying physics available if they want it. Concretely, I'd start with the observable behavior: "the sensor output has X LSB of noise at this sample rate, and it's not white — it has a low-frequency component that averaging alone won't remove." Then I'd translate to implications: what sample rates are viable, what filtering approaches are likely to help versus hurt, what calibration or offset correction is needed, and what the firmware should not assume (e.g., that consecutive samples are independent). I'd back this with a short characterization document — noise spectrum, effective number of bits at the relevant bandwidth, any temperature or supply dependence — so the firmware team can reason from data rather than from my summary. I'd also offer to sit with them during the first implementation pass, because the translation usually needs a second round once they start writing code and hit the specific questions the summary didn't anticipate. The key discipline is to be honest about what's a hard limit versus what's a trade-off: if the ADC resolution genuinely caps achievable accuracy, say so plainly; if it's a trade-off against sample rate or power, present it as a decision they can participate in rather than a constraint handed down.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the firmware team proposes a filtering approach you believe won't work, but they're confident it will? What would you do if the hardware constraint turns out to be worse than you initially characterized, after firmware work has already started?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding how much technical detail to include when presenting a design trade-off to a mixed audience of engineers, a quality manager, and a product manager in the same room?
**Answer:** The instinct is to find a single level of detail that works for everyone, and that usually produces something too shallow for the engineers and too deep for everyone else. A better approach is to structure the presentation in layers, so each audience can engage at the depth they need without anyone being talked past. I'd lead with the decision itself and its consequences in plain terms: what we're choosing between, what each option costs in schedule, risk, and capability, and what I recommend and why. That's the layer everyone needs. Then I'd have a technical layer ready — the specific trade-offs, the data behind them, the assumptions — and present it only as deep as the room needs, pausing to check whether the engineers want more detail or whether the non-engineers have questions that pull it back up. The quality manager's concerns are usually about risk, traceability, and whether the decision is defensible; the product manager's are about schedule, cost, and user impact. I'd address those explicitly rather than hoping they infer them from the technical content. I'd also be careful not to hide uncertainty behind technical language — if there's a real unknown, say so in plain terms, because non-technical stakeholders can handle uncertainty but not the feeling that they're being managed. The test of a good mixed-audience presentation is whether each person leaves knowing what was decided, why, and what happens next — not whether they all understood the same depth of detail.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the product manager pushes for a decision before the engineers have finished evaluating the options? What would you do if the quality manager raises a concern in the meeting that you hadn't anticipated and can't answer on the spot?

## Q5: How would you approach a mentoring relationship with an engineer who is strong at execution but has never owned a design decision end-to-end, and tends to defer to whoever is most senior in the room?
**Answer:** The pattern usually isn't a capability gap — it's that the engineer has never been in a position where deferring wasn't the safer move, so the behavior is rational given their experience. The mentoring goal is to build the muscle for owning decisions, which means creating situations where they have to make the call and live with the consequences, with support available but not pre-emptively applied. I'd start by giving them a scoped decision to own — something with real consequences but bounded risk — and being explicit that it's theirs to make, not mine to approve. During the process, I'd resist the urge to answer when they ask "what would you do?" and instead ask what they think and why, then probe the reasoning rather than the conclusion. If their reasoning is sound but their conclusion differs from mine, that's a good outcome to let stand, because the point is to develop judgment, not to clone mine. I'd also make the decision-making process visible: how to frame the options, what data matters, how to weigh trade-offs, how to document the rationale, how to know when to escalate versus decide. Over time, I'd increase the scope and the ambiguity, and I'd deliberately not be in the room for some decisions so they have to own them without a senior person to defer to. The hardest part is tolerating their mistakes when they make them — if I swoop in to correct every suboptimal call, I teach them that deferring was right all along. I'd debrief after decisions, good and bad, focusing on the reasoning rather than the outcome, and I'd be explicit about the progress I'm seeing so they know the deferring habit is something we're actively working on together.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if a decision they own goes badly and other team members start questioning whether they should have been given that scope? What would you do if they continue to defer even when explicitly told the decision is theirs?
