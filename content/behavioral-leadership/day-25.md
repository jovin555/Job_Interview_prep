# behavioral-leadership — Day 25

## Q1: How would you approach building a technical leadership roadmap for a team that is transitioning from a single-product medical device focus to a multi-project portfolio, where engineers are used to deep specialization rather than cross-project flexibility?

**Answer:** I'd start by assessing the current skill matrix against the projected portfolio needs — not just technical skills but also the ability to context-switch and manage parallel priorities. The key is to make the transition incremental rather than abrupt. I'd begin by identifying natural overlaps between projects where specialists can contribute without fully abandoning their depth — for example, an analog engineer might support two projects that both use similar sensor front-ends. I'd pair specialists with a "shadow" engineer from another project to cross-train gradually, and I'd introduce lightweight knowledge-sharing mechanisms like shared design guidelines and reusable IP blocks so that expertise isn't locked in one person's head. I'd also be explicit about the career implications — some engineers thrive on depth and may feel threatened by breadth expectations, so I'd create pathways for both deep specialization and cross-project generalists. Finally, I'd establish clear prioritization rules for how resources are allocated when projects compete for the same engineer, so the team doesn't feel like they're being pulled in all directions without a coherent strategy.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle an engineer who strongly resists broadening their scope and wants to remain a single-project specialist? What metrics would you use to track whether the transition is succeeding?

## Q2: How would you approach designing a test strategy for a battery-powered medical sensor device where the firmware team wants to add a new low-power sleep mode that significantly extends battery life, but the hardware team is concerned about the mode's effect on analog sensor stability during wake-up transitions?

**Answer:** I'd frame this as a system-level verification problem rather than a hardware-versus-firmware dispute. The first step would be to define what "stable" means quantitatively — what settling time, voltage ripple, and measurement accuracy are acceptable after wake-up, based on the device's clinical requirements. With that shared specification, both teams can design tests against the same criteria. I'd propose a three-phase test strategy: first, bench-level characterization of the wake-up transient using a scope and data acquisition to capture the analog front-end behavior under controlled conditions; second, a firmware-in-the-loop test where the sleep/wake cycle is exercised repeatedly while monitoring sensor readings for anomalies; and third, a longer-duration soak test that simulates realistic usage patterns to catch intermittent issues that might not appear in short tests. I'd also recommend adding a hardware test point or debug header specifically for monitoring the analog rail during transitions, so the issue can be characterized without guesswork. The key is to convert the disagreement into a data-collection exercise — once both teams see the actual waveforms and measurement data, the design decision becomes evidence-based rather than opinion-based.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific parameters would you monitor during the wake-up transition, and what would trigger you to reject the sleep mode design? How would you handle a situation where the test results show marginal stability that could go either way?

## Q3: How would you approach leading a technical review of a proposed architecture for a new medical device where the system architect has chosen a modular design with multiple microcontrollers communicating over CAN-FD, but several senior engineers believe a single high-performance processor would be simpler and more reliable?

**Answer:** I'd structure the review to focus on decision criteria rather than personal preferences. The first step is to establish what the architecture needs to achieve — not just functionally, but in terms of safety, maintainability, certification effort, and long-term support. I'd ask the architect to present the modular design with explicit rationale for the partitioning: what drove the decision to split functions across multiple MCUs? Is it isolation of safety-critical functions, independent development timelines, power management, or something else? Then I'd ask the opposing engineers to articulate their concerns in the same framework — what specific risks do they see in the multi-MCU approach, and how would the single-processor design mitigate those? I'd push for concrete comparisons: interrupt latency requirements, memory isolation needs, fault containment strategies, and the certification implications of each approach. If the disagreement persists, I'd propose a focused prototyping effort — even a small proof-of-concept that exercises the riskiest aspect of the architecture, such as CAN-FD timing under worst-case load, can resolve the debate with data. I'd also make sure the decision and its rationale are documented in the design history file, because whichever architecture is chosen, the reasoning needs to survive for regulatory review.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific criteria would you use to decide between the two architectures? How would you handle a situation where the prototyping effort confirms both approaches are technically viable, but the team still can't agree?

## Q4: How would you approach building a culture where engineers feel comfortable raising concerns about a design decision early, even when the decision was made by a senior person or a consensus of the team, and even when the concern is based on a "gut feeling" rather than a fully articulated technical argument?

**Answer:** I'd focus on creating psychological safety through structure rather than just encouraging people to speak up. The key is to normalize the idea that early concerns — even vague ones — are valuable data. I'd introduce a formal mechanism like a "concern log" where anyone can raise an issue without needing to have a fully formed technical argument. The rule would be that every concern gets acknowledged and triaged, not dismissed, and the person raising it gets feedback on what was considered and why. I'd also model the behavior myself by openly raising concerns that are partially formed, and by explicitly thanking people who raise issues that turn out to be false alarms — because the cost of a false alarm is low, but the cost of a missed concern can be catastrophic in medical devices. In design reviews, I'd allocate a specific time slot for "open concerns" where the expectation is that people share intuitions and half-formed thoughts, and I'd make it clear that the goal is to surface risks early, not to win arguments. Over time, this shifts the culture from "I need to be sure before I speak" to "I should speak so we can all become more sure."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a junior engineer raises a concern that is clearly based on a misunderstanding, and a senior engineer responds dismissively? How would you distinguish between a useful gut feeling and noise that shouldn't consume the team's time?

## Q5: How would you approach leading a technical investigation when a field-reported issue with a medical device could be caused by either a sensor calibration drift or a firmware filtering algorithm error, and the two possible causes would require very different corrective actions?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a structured root-cause investigation using a framework like 8D or a fishbone diagram, but the critical first step is containment — ensuring patient safety while the investigation proceeds. If the device is still in the field, I'd work with the clinical team to determine whether the issue poses immediate risk or can be monitored while data is collected. Once containment is in place, I'd focus on gathering discriminating evidence rather than arguing about which cause is more likely. The key is to find tests that can distinguish between the two hypotheses. For sensor calibration drift, I'd look at whether the error correlates with time in service, temperature exposure, or other environmental factors that would affect the sensor physically. For a firmware filtering error, I'd examine whether the error correlates with specific input patterns, firmware versions, or operating modes. I'd also pull the raw sensor data if available — if the firmware is logging pre-filtered values, comparing raw versus filtered data can immediately reveal whether the algorithm is introducing the error. I'd involve both the hardware and firmware teams in designing the data collection, so the investigation is collaborative rather than adversarial. Once the evidence points to a root cause, I'd implement a corrective action and — critically — verify its effectiveness over time, not just in a single test. I'd also document the investigation thoroughly, because in a medical device context, the reasoning behind the corrective action needs to survive regulatory scrutiny.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the field data is insufficient to discriminate between the two causes, and you need to make a decision with incomplete information? What would you do if the investigation reveals that both causes contributed to the issue?
