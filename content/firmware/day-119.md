# firmware — Day 119

## Q1: How would you approach designing a firmware module that must handle a peripheral whose interrupt rate is high enough that servicing every interrupt individually would consume an unreasonable share of CPU time?

**Answer:** The first step is to quantify the problem rather than assume it: measure the actual ISR entry/exit overhead, the per-interrupt work, and the resulting CPU utilization at the expected event rate. If servicing every event individually would consume, say, more than a small fraction of the CPU budget, the ISR is the wrong granularity.

The standard remedy is to decouple "the event happened" from "process the event." In the ISR, do the minimum that hardware requires — acknowledge the interrupt, move the data into a buffer (or, better, let DMA do the moving), and signal a deferred context. Then batch the processing: a thread or work-queue handler drains the buffer in chunks rather than one event at a time, amortizing the scheduling and function-call overhead across many events.

Where the peripheral supports it, DMA is the cleanest answer — configure the peripheral to stream into a circular buffer and interrupt only on half-transfer and full-transfer (or on a watermark), so the interrupt rate drops by the buffer granularity. This also removes the per-byte CPU cost entirely. If DMA isn't available, a FIFO in the peripheral plus a threshold interrupt achieves a similar effect.

The trade-offs to weigh: batching adds latency between event arrival and processing, so it's only acceptable if the consumer can tolerate that latency; the buffer must be sized for worst-case burst plus worst-case scheduling delay, or you need an overflow policy (drop-oldest, drop-newest, or backpressure); and you must be careful about cache coherency and memory barriers if DMA is involved. Finally, verify with measurement — instrument the ISR duration and the deferred handler's queue depth under realistic load, because "high interrupt rate" problems are easy to misjudge from the datasheet alone.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the buffer size and the half/full-transfer watermark for the DMA case? What would you do if the deferred handler still can't keep up during a burst — how do you detect and handle overflow without silently losing data?

## Q2: You're bringing up a new peripheral driver and the datasheet's initialization sequence has several steps with "wait at least X µs" and "wait no more than Y ms" constraints. How would you implement and verify that sequence so it's correct on the first hardware revision and stays correct through later ones?

**Answer:** The core discipline is to make every timing constraint explicit in code rather than implicit in the instruction stream. I'd structure the init sequence as a table or a sequence of small steps, each annotated with its constraint (minimum delay, maximum delay, or "poll until ready, with timeout"), so a reviewer can check the code against the datasheet line by line.

For "wait at least X µs," a busy-wait or a hardware-timer-based delay is appropriate for short intervals, but it must be derived from the actual clock configuration, not a magic loop count — loop-count delays break the moment the compiler, optimization level, or clock changes. For "wait no more than Y ms," the correct pattern is a bounded poll: check the status register in a loop with a timeout, and treat timeout expiry as an error rather than spinning forever. For anything longer than a few hundred microseconds, yield to the scheduler rather than blocking the CPU.

Verification matters as much as implementation. On the bench, I'd instrument the sequence with a GPIO toggle or a logic-analyzer capture at each step boundary so the actual timing is visible, and compare against the datasheet limits with margin. I'd also test at the extremes — cold boot, warm reset, and slow-clock conditions — because init sequences often fail at the corners. To keep it correct through revisions, the timing constants should live in one place with references to the datasheet section, and the sequence should be covered by a test that at minimum exercises it on every hardware revision and flags any step that times out.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a step where the datasheet gives a range but the actual device is consistently at one edge of it — do you trust the datasheet or the measurement? What's your approach if a later silicon revision changes one of the timing constraints?

## Q3: You're implementing a bootloader for a device that must support field updates over an unreliable link, and the device has no physical recovery mechanism (no button, no exposed debug header). How would you structure the update and recovery logic so that a failed or interrupted transfer can never leave the device unbootable?

**Answer:** The guiding principle is that the bootloader must always have a known-good image to fall back to, and the decision of what to boot must be made by logic that cannot itself be corrupted by a failed update. That points to a dual-bank (A/B) layout: the running application and the update target are separate regions, and the bootloader — which is never overwritten by a field update — selects which bank to boot.

The update flow: the application receives the new image, writes it to the inactive bank, and verifies it (CRC or cryptographic hash over the whole image, plus a header check) before marking it bootable. Only after verification does it set a flag requesting that the bootloader try the new bank. The bootloader then boots the new bank with a "trial" marker; the new application must reach a self-test / health-check milestone and explicitly confirm itself before the trial marker is cleared. If the device resets before confirmation — for any reason, including a crash in the new image — the bootloader sees the uncleared trial marker and reverts to the previous bank. This "boot count / trial boot" pattern is what makes rollback automatic rather than dependent on the application cooperating.

Because the link is unreliable, the transfer itself must be resumable or restartable: chunked writes with per-chunk CRC, and the ability to discard a partial image and start over without touching the active bank. The metadata (bank status, trial flags, image hashes) should be stored redundantly — two copies with their own CRC, or in a small dedicated region with wear-leveling — so a power loss mid-metadata-write can't leave the boot decision ambiguous. The bootloader should also validate the image header and hash before jumping, and if both banks fail validation, fall into a minimal recovery mode (e.g., stay in the bootloader listening on the comms interface) rather than jumping into garbage.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you protect the bootloader itself from being corrupted — what's your approach to a bootloader update, given there's no recovery button? How do you prevent an attacker or a buggy tool from flashing an image that passes CRC but is the wrong product or an incompatible version?

## Q4: A junior engineer has implemented a circular buffer shared between an ISR that writes samples and a task that reads them. It works in testing, but you notice the writer doesn't check for a full buffer and simply overwrites the oldest data. How would you guide them?

**Answer:** I'd start by making sure we agree on what the buffer is *for*, because "overwrite oldest" is a legitimate policy in some systems (e.g., a display that only cares about the latest samples) and a serious bug in others (e.g., a data-acquisition path where every sample must be accounted for). So the first question is: what's the required behavior when the reader falls behind — drop oldest, drop newest, or block/backpressure? The answer should come from the system requirements, not from what's convenient to code.

If the requirement is "never lose a sample," then overwriting is a correctness bug and the writer must detect full and handle it — either by dropping the *new* sample (and counting the drop so it's visible), or by signaling backpressure to the producer if the hardware supports it. If the requirement genuinely permits dropping oldest, then the overwrite is fine but must be made explicit and safe: the reader must never observe a torn or inconsistent sample, and the overflow should be counted and surfaced as a diagnostic, because silent data loss is the thing that bites you in the field.

The concurrency mechanics matter either way. With a single writer (ISR) and single reader (task), the classic lock-free pattern is a head/tail index pair where the writer only advances head and the reader only advances tail, with the indices sized so they can't wrap ambiguously, and memory barriers (or `volatile` plus compiler barriers, or atomics) ensuring the reader sees the data before the index update. The subtle part is the full/empty distinction — a naive head==tail check is ambiguous, so you either keep a count, use a power-of-two size with a separate full flag, or reserve one slot. I'd walk the junior engineer through why the naive version is racy and have them write a test that hammers the buffer from both contexts to expose it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you test a lock-free circular buffer to gain confidence it's correct, given that race conditions are timing-dependent? If the reader is a task and the writer is an ISR, what happens if the reader is preempted mid-read — how do you guarantee it doesn't see a half-updated sample?

## Q5: You're leading a firmware team where two senior engineers disagree on whether to implement a critical communication protocol using a polling approach or an interrupt-driven approach. One argues that polling is simpler and more predictable for real-time constraints, while the other insists that interrupts are necessary for responsiveness. How would you guide the team to a decision?

**Answer:** I'd reframe the disagreement away from "which is better" and toward "which meets *this* system's requirements with acceptable risk," because both engineers are describing real trade-offs and the right answer depends on the numbers. My job as lead is to get the decision made on evidence rather than preference, and to make sure whoever "loses" still owns the outcome.

Concretely, I'd ask both to state the requirements the protocol must meet: the maximum latency the protocol can tolerate, the expected message rate and burst behavior, the CPU budget available, and the consequences of a missed message. Then I'd have them characterize the two approaches against those numbers — polling's worst-case latency is bounded by the poll period plus the longest task that can delay the poll, while interrupt-driven latency is bounded by interrupt latency plus the handler, but its CPU cost scales with event rate and it introduces concurrency hazards. Often the data settles it: if the message rate is low and latency tolerance is generous, polling is simpler and easier to verify; if events are bursty or latency-critical, interrupts (or DMA) win.

Where the data doesn't settle it, I'd look at the risk profile: which approach is easier to test, easier to reason about under worst-case load, and easier for the team to maintain? I'd also consider a hybrid — interrupt to capture the event, deferred processing in a thread — which often gets the responsiveness without the ISR complexity. Then I'd make the call, document the rationale and the assumptions it rests on, and set a checkpoint to revisit if the assumptions change. The point is that the team sees a decision made on stated criteria, not on seniority, which keeps the disagreement from becoming personal.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the decision so the engineer whose approach wasn't chosen stays engaged rather than disengaging? What would you do if, after the decision, the chosen approach starts showing problems in integration — how do you decide whether to revisit or push through?
