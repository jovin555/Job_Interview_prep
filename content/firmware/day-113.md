# firmware — Day 113

## Q1: How would you approach configuring and validating a windowed watchdog in a system where the firmware's main loop timing varies with workload, so that a "too early" kick is as much a fault as a "too late" one?

**Answer:** A windowed watchdog is attractive precisely because it catches both failure modes: a hung task that never kicks, and a runaway task that kicks in a tight loop (which a plain timeout watchdog would happily accept). The design problem is that the window is a fixed hardware constraint, while loop timing is workload-dependent, so the two have to be reconciled deliberately rather than by widening the window until it always passes.

My approach would be to first characterize the actual kick interval distribution — not just the nominal period, but the worst-case jitter introduced by the slowest legitimate path through the loop (flash writes, long protocol transactions, blocking waits). That gives a real upper bound and a real lower bound. Then I'd set the window with margin on both sides: the lower bound must be comfortably above the fastest possible legitimate iteration, and the upper bound comfortably above the worst-case legitimate iteration, with the margin justified by measurement rather than guesswork.

The structural fix is usually to decouple the kick from the main loop entirely. Instead of kicking inline wherever the code happens to be, I'd have a dedicated supervisory task or a timer-driven kick point whose only job is to confirm that the things that *should* be running are running — checking progress counters or event flags set by the real work — and then kicking. That way the kick cadence is deterministic even when the work cadence isn't, and the watchdog is genuinely testing liveness rather than testing whether the loop happened to be fast that iteration.

I'd also make sure the window is validated under the same conditions as production: temperature extremes, worst-case bus traffic, and the slowest flash operation, because a window that only holds on a lightly loaded bench is worse than no window at all — it will trip in the field and erode trust in the reset.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a legitimate operation that is longer than the entire window, such as a multi-second calibration? What would you log on a windowed-watchdog reset so that the failure is diagnosable after the fact?

## Q2: You're bringing up a new peripheral driver and the datasheet's initialization sequence has several steps with "wait at least X µs" and "wait no more than Y ms" constraints. How would you implement and verify that sequence so it's correct on the first hardware revision and stays correct through later ones?

**Answer:** Timing-bounded init sequences are where drivers quietly break, because the constraints are usually expressed as ranges and the natural instinct is to satisfy them with a generous fixed delay — which passes on the bench and then fails on a faster or slower part, or under a different clock configuration.

I'd start by encoding the sequence as an explicit ordered list of steps, each with its own constraint type: minimum delay, maximum delay, or "poll until ready with a timeout." Minimum delays get a real delay primitive; maximum-delay steps get a bounded poll on the relevant status bit rather than a sleep, because polling exits as soon as the part is ready and naturally respects the upper bound. The only place a fixed sleep is defensible is a genuine minimum with no status signal to poll, and even then I'd document *why* it's a sleep and what the margin is.

Verification is the part people skip. I'd instrument the sequence with timestamps — either GPIO toggles captured on a scope or a cycle-counter log — and measure the actual elapsed time at each step against the datasheet bounds, on the real hardware at the real clock. That turns "I think it's fine" into a measured margin per step. I'd also deliberately test at the extremes: cold boot, warm reset, and any low-power exit path that re-runs init, since init sequences frequently behave differently after a warm reset than after a cold power-up.

For maintainability, I'd keep the sequence in one place with the datasheet bounds as named constants next to each step, so a future revision of the part or a clock change has an obvious place to be re-validated rather than being buried in scattered sleeps.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if a step has a maximum delay but no status bit to poll? How would you structure the driver so the init sequence can be unit-tested off-target?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether a given piece of firmware logic belongs in an interrupt service routine, a deferred context like a work queue or thread, or a low-priority background task — and what criteria would drive that split?

**Answer:** The decision comes down to three properties of the work: how tight its latency requirement is, how long it takes, and whether it can block.

Anything with a hard, short deadline that cannot tolerate scheduling delay has to be in the ISR — but only the *minimum* part of it. The ISR should do the time-critical thing (latch the data, clear the flag, timestamp the event, maybe push to a lock-free queue) and then hand off. Everything else — parsing, validation, logging, state transitions, anything that might block on a mutex or take more than a few microseconds — belongs in a deferred context.

The criteria I'd apply explicitly: if the work can block on anything (a mutex, a bus transaction, a flash write), it cannot be in an ISR, full stop. If the work's duration is unbounded or data-dependent, it cannot be in an ISR, because ISR duration directly becomes interrupt latency for everything else. If the work is latency-tolerant by tens of milliseconds, a low-priority background task is often the right home, because it yields naturally to anything more urgent and keeps the deferred-context queue short.

The split I'd aim for is a thin, fast ISR that captures the event and signals, plus a deferred handler that does the real work at a priority matched to its actual deadline. I'd also budget the ISR latency explicitly — sum the worst-case ISR durations and check that against the tightest deadline in the system — because "it's only a few microseconds" tends to be true per-ISR and false in aggregate.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a case where the deferred handler falls behind and the queue fills? What would change in your answer if the deferred context were a work queue versus a dedicated thread?

## Q4: A junior engineer has implemented a circular buffer shared between an ISR that writes samples and a task that reads them. It works in testing, but you notice the writer doesn't check for a full buffer and simply overwrites the oldest data. How would you guide them?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating the two issues, because they're different in kind: one is a correctness bug, the other is a design decision that may or may not be acceptable depending on the system.

The correctness issue is the missing full check. Overwriting unread data silently means the reader can receive a torn or stale sample with no indication, and in a data-acquisition path that's exactly the kind of failure that's invisible in testing and shows up as a subtle anomaly later. I'd walk them through the standard single-producer/single-consumer ring buffer discipline: head and tail indices, the full condition distinguished from the empty condition (usually by keeping one slot free or by using a separate count), and the memory-ordering requirements so the compiler and CPU don't reorder the index update ahead of the data write. I'd also make sure the indices are the right width and that the ISR-side update is a single atomic store where the architecture requires it.

The design question is what *should* happen on overflow. There are legitimate policies — drop the newest sample, overwrite the oldest, set an overflow flag and let the reader decide — but the policy has to be chosen deliberately and made visible to the consumer. For a medical data path I'd lean toward never silently overwriting: either drop the new sample and count the drop, or overwrite but set a flag the reader must check, so the loss is recorded rather than hidden.

I'd guide rather than dictate: ask them what should happen when the reader falls behind, and let the answer drive the implementation. That tends to land better than a lecture, and it surfaces whether they'd thought about the reader's behavior at all.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify the buffer under worst-case load — what test would actually exercise the full condition? What changes if there are multiple writers?

## Q5: You're asked to add a feature to a firmware module that has no tests, no documentation, and a reputation for being fragile. The change needs to ship on a tight schedule. How would you approach it?

**Answer:** The instinct is to either rewrite it or tiptoe around it, and both are usually wrong on a tight schedule. I'd approach it as a risk-management problem: make the change as small and as observable as possible, and build just enough safety net to know whether I've broken something.

First I'd spend a bounded amount of time reading the module and mapping its actual interfaces — what it reads, what it writes, who calls it, what global state it touches. That map is the real deliverable, because it tells me where the blast radius of my change is. I'd write down what I believe the module's contract is, even if it's inferred, and flag the places where I'm guessing.

Then I'd build a minimal characterization harness around the specific behavior my change touches — not a full test suite, just enough to pin down the current behavior so I can tell if I've altered it. For firmware that often means a bench test with a scripted input sequence and a captured output, or a host-side test of the pure logic if it can be extracted. The point is a before/after comparison, not coverage.

I'd make the change behind the smallest possible seam — ideally a new code path that can be enabled or disabled — so that if it misbehaves in the field the fallback is a configuration change rather than a rollback. And I'd add logging or instrumentation around the changed path so that if something does go wrong, the failure is diagnosable rather than mysterious.

Throughout, I'd be explicit with the team about what I don't know and what I haven't tested. Shipping on a tight schedule is fine; shipping while pretending the risk isn't there is not. The honest framing — "here's what I changed, here's what I verified, here's what I couldn't" — is what lets the people around me make a real decision about whether to ship.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide when the module has accumulated enough risk to justify a rewrite rather than another patch? What would you do if the schedule pressure came with an instruction not to add any instrumentation?
