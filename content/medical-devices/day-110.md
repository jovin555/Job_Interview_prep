# medical-devices — Day 110

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts against the standard's measurement matrix rather than treating "leakage current" as a single test. IEC 60601-1 defines several distinct quantities — earth leakage current, enclosure leakage current, patient leakage current, and patient auxiliary current — and each has its own limit, its own normal-condition and single-fault-condition values, and its own dependence on the applied part classification. BF and CF parts have different patient leakage limits (CF is tighter because it permits a lower patient leakage current), so the first step is to build a table: rows are the applied parts, columns are the measurement types and conditions, and each cell holds the applicable limit and the measurement setup.

For the measurement path itself, the key design decisions are the measurement network and the reference point. The standard specifies an MD (measuring device) network that models the impedance the human body presents, and the measurement is taken between the applied part and earth, or between applied parts, depending on the quantity. I'd design the test setup so the network, the supply configuration (normal vs. reversed polarity, neutral vs. earth reference), and the single-fault conditions are all switchable in a controlled, documented way — because the most common error is measuring the right quantity under the wrong supply condition and getting a number that looks fine but doesn't correspond to the required test.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes from the device's classification and construction: which parts are applied, whether they're BF or CF, whether the device is Class I or Class II, whether it's mains- or battery-powered, and which single-fault conditions are credible. I'd cross-reference the standard's flowchart rather than guess, and I'd document the rationale for each measurement included and each one excluded — that rationale is what a reviewer will ask for. For a device with both BF and CF parts, I'd also check the measurements between applied parts, since a fault path from one part to another can be the limiting case.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where the CF applied part is only ever used with the BF part disconnected — does that change the measurement matrix? What would you do if a measurement is marginally under the limit and you suspect the test setup itself is contributing?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it usually points to a resonance or a specific coupling path rather than a broadband susceptibility problem. My first step is to characterize the failure precisely: sweep the band in fine steps, note the exact frequency and field strength at which the malfunction begins and ends, and record what the device actually does (reset, display corruption, sensor dropout, communication loss). The shape of that response — sharp versus gradual, and whether it's field-strength-dependent — tells me a lot before I touch the hardware.

Next I'd separate the coupling path from the victim circuit. Is the energy entering through a cable (a harness acting as an antenna), through an enclosure seam or vent, or directly onto a PCB trace? I'd test this by repeating the sweep with cables dressed differently, with ferrites temporarily clamped on suspect cables, and with the enclosure partially shielded with copper tape. If a ferrite on one cable moves or eliminates the resonance, that cable is the antenna and the fix is likely common-mode filtering or a different cable route. If shielding a seam fixes it, it's an aperture problem.

On the victim side, I'd look for the circuit that's actually responding: a high-impedance node, an unbypassed reference, a clock or reset line picking up the field, or a firmware path that's sensitive to a glitch. Sometimes the "malfunction" is a firmware watchdog or brownout response to a transient, not a hardware failure per se — so I'd check whether the device recovers on its own and whether the failure is in the analog front-end or the digital core. The fix is then targeted: add or improve common-mode chokes and feedthrough filtering at the cable entry, tighten decoupling on the affected reference, improve the ground return, or add firmware-level filtering/debouncing if the susceptibility is a transient glitch. I'd verify by re-sweeping the full band, not just the original frequency, because a fix that shifts the resonance rather than removing it is not a fix.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a resonance in the cable from a resonance on the PCB itself? If the fix works at the test lab but the device is later used near a different RF source, how would you gain confidence the fix is robust?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file stays useful if it's built as a living traceability structure rather than a binder. I'd start with the risk management plan, which defines scope, the risk acceptability criteria, how risks will be estimated, and who reviews and approves — that plan is what makes every later entry defensible. Then the core is a hazard/hazardous-situation analysis that's organized around the device's functions and use environment, not around a generic checklist, so that each entry connects to a real design decision.

The key structural choice is linking each risk control to the design output that implements it and the verification evidence that proves it works. If a risk control is "add a redundant sensor path," the file should point to the schematic and the test that demonstrates the redundancy, not just assert it. That linkage is what makes the file auditable: a reviewer can follow hazard → risk → control → verification → residual risk in one thread. I'd keep the risk file and the design history file cross-referenced rather than duplicated, so a design change triggers a risk review and the risk review points back to the changed design output.

To keep it alive, I'd build in periodic review at design milestones and after any change that affects safety, and I'd make the risk review part of the design review agenda rather than a separate exercise. The failure mode I'd actively avoid is the "risk file as a retrospective narrative" — where the file is written to match decisions already made. The file should be where the decision gets made, or at least where it gets recorded at the time. I'd also keep the residual-risk acceptance explicit and signed, because that's the part auditors and reviewers focus on.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a risk that's identified late, after the design is largely frozen — where does it enter the file? How do you keep the risk file from becoming so large that nobody reads it?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means a software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation, so the verification has to demonstrate that the safety-related requirements are met with a level of rigor that matches that consequence. I'd start from the software requirements and the architecture: every safety-related requirement needs a traceable verification method, and the architecture needs to show how the safety-related functions are isolated or protected from non-safety functions. The verification plan is then built from that traceability, not from a generic test list.

At the unit level, Class C drives expectations around structural coverage and rigorous unit verification — the standard's framework calls for things like statement and branch coverage, and for the more critical functions, more demanding coverage and static analysis. I'd define the coverage targets in the plan, run the static analysis and unit tests, and record the results against each requirement. At integration and system level, I'd verify the interfaces between software items and the safety-related behavior under fault conditions — for example, what happens when a non-safety task overruns, when a sensor returns invalid data, or when memory is exhausted. Those fault-injection and robustness tests are where Class C verification earns its keep.

The documentation side matters as much as the testing: the software development plan, the requirements and architecture documents, the unit and integration test records, the problem/defect tracking, and the release criteria all need to be consistent and traceable. I'd also make sure the verification is repeatable — the same test suite, the same configuration, the same tool versions — because a reviewer will ask whether the evidence actually corresponds to the released software. The overall posture is: every safety-related requirement has evidence, every piece of evidence is traceable, and the rigor of the method is proportionate to the classification.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you justify a lower coverage target for a particular software item without weakening the Class C claim? What would you do if a defect is found after the verification is complete but before release?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a disagreement about risk estimation and about the acceptability criteria, not as a schedule fight — because if I frame it as "we can't afford it," I've already lost the argument on the merits. My first move is to get the quality manager's reasoning on the table: what hazard, what severity and probability they're assigning, and which part of the risk acceptability criteria they think it fails. Often the disagreement is that engineering is estimating probability from field experience while quality is estimating it from the worst credible case, and those are different questions.

Then I'd work the risk file with them rather than against them. If the hazard is genuinely negligible under the agreed criteria, the file should show that — the estimation rationale, the existing controls, and the residual risk — and that documented rationale is what resolves the disagreement, not my opinion. If the quality manager has information I don't — a complaint history, a regulatory expectation, a clinical concern — then the estimate may need to change, and I'd rather find that out in the review than after submission. I'd also separate the two questions: is the control warranted on risk grounds, and if so, what's the least disruptive way to implement it? Sometimes a design change, a labeling change, or a firmware guard can address the hazard at a fraction of the cost of the control originally proposed.

On schedule, I'd be honest about the impact and bring options rather than a flat no: implement now and accept the delay, implement a partial control now and complete it in a follow-on revision, or document the rationale for not implementing and accept the residual risk with sign-off. The decision belongs to the risk management process and the accountable reviewers, and my job as lead is to make sure the decision is made on evidence and recorded, not to win the argument. If we do proceed without the control, I'd want the acceptance explicit and signed, because that's the defensible position later.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's position is supported by the regulatory reviewer and yours isn't? How would you prevent this kind of disagreement from recurring on the next project?
