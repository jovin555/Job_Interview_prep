# medical-devices — Day 92

## Q1: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted emissions and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a layered problem rather than a single filter design, because conducted and radiated emissions are attacked at different points in the system and the fixes can fight each other if applied blindly.

Start at the source. The cheapest emissions fix is reducing the noise generated in the first place: choosing switching converters with controlled edge rates, keeping high-di/dt loops physically small, and placing decoupling as close to the load as the layout allows. A filter designed around a quiet source is far smaller and cheaper than one compensating for a noisy one.

Then work outward in stages. For conducted emissions on the mains input, a multi-stage EMI filter — common-mode choke plus X and Y capacitors arranged so the filter's impedance mismatch with source and load is deliberate — is the standard approach. The key discipline is that the filter's effectiveness depends on where it sits relative to the enclosure boundary and on the impedance of the return path; a filter placed after a long unshielded trace has already radiated what you're trying to suppress. For radiated emissions, the dominant mechanisms are usually cables acting as antennas and slots/apertures in the enclosure, so cable routing, common-mode chokes on external cables, and enclosure seam bonding matter as much as any component.

The signal-integrity tension is real: a filter that's aggressive enough to kill emissions can also attenuate or distort the signal you care about, add phase shift, or introduce ringing. So I'd define the passband and stopband requirements for each interface explicitly — what signal bandwidth must survive untouched, what frequency range must be suppressed — and verify the filter against both criteria, not just the EMC one. For sensitive analog or high-speed digital interfaces, I'd prefer filtering that's selective (targeted at the offending band) over broadband attenuation, and I'd keep the filter's return path separate from the sensitive signal's reference so the filter doesn't inject noise into the very net it's protecting.

Finally, I'd plan for iteration. Pre-compliance testing early, with the filter designed as a placeable/modifiable block rather than baked into the layout, so that when a specific frequency fails you can adjust component values without a board spin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a failing frequency is a differential-mode or common-mode problem, and how does that change your filter topology? What's your approach when the filter that fixes emissions also degrades a sensor's accuracy, and how would you prove the trade-off is acceptable?

## Q2: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I'd start from the device's intended use and clinical function, not from a keyword search of the standard catalog. The IEC 60601-2 series is organized by device type and clinical application, so the first step is a clear, written statement of what the device does, on what patient population, in what environment, and by what mechanism — because a device that monitors and also delivers therapy may fall under more than one particular standard, and possibly under a collateral standard as well.

The practical process: enumerate each distinct clinical function, map each to its likely particular standard, and then confirm applicability by reading the scope clause of each candidate standard rather than assuming from the title. Scope clauses explicitly state what's in and what's excluded, and a device that combines functions can end up subject to two particular standards simultaneously. I'd document that mapping as part of the regulatory planning, because it drives the test plan and the DHF structure.

On conflicts: the general standard (60601-1) and a particular standard can specify different limits or different test conditions for what looks like the same requirement. The general rule is that the particular standard takes precedence for the specific requirement it addresses — it exists precisely because the general requirement isn't adequate or appropriate for that device type. But "takes precedence" doesn't mean "ignore the general standard"; it means the particular standard's clause governs where they overlap, and the general standard still applies everywhere the particular standard is silent. Where two particular standards both apply and appear to conflict, I'd resolve it by going back to the clinical intent behind each requirement, documenting the interpretation, and — critically — confirming that interpretation with the test lab and, if needed, the regulatory body before committing to a design. Ambiguity resolved late is expensive; ambiguity resolved in a written rationale early is just part of the file.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the DHF so that the applicable-standards rationale and any conflict resolutions are traceable to specific test cases? If a test lab interprets a scope clause differently than you did, how would you handle that disagreement?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The failure mode I'd design against is the risk file that gets written retrospectively to match a design that's already frozen — at that point it's a compliance artifact, not a risk management tool, and it usually shows: hazards that map suspiciously neatly to existing design features, no evidence of risk controls being considered and rejected, and no traceability to actual design decisions.

So I'd structure it around the design process rather than around the final document. The backbone is a hazard analysis that's built and revised as the design evolves — starting at the concept/architecture stage when the risk of change is lowest, and updated at each design review. Each hazard gets a risk estimate (severity and probability), a risk control measure, and — this is the part that's often skipped — a verification that the control actually reduces the risk as intended, plus an evaluation of residual risk and whether it's acceptable per the risk acceptability criteria defined up front. Those criteria need to be written before you start estimating risks, otherwise every residual risk conveniently turns out to be acceptable.

For auditability, the file needs bidirectional traceability: from each hazard to the design input it drives, to the design output that implements the control, to the verification evidence, and back. That traceability is what lets an auditor or a new engineer see why a particular design decision exists. I'd also keep the risk file linked to the DHF rather than duplicating it — the risk analysis references design inputs and verification records by identifier, so there's one source of truth.

Practically, I'd keep it maintainable by making risk review a standing agenda item at design reviews, assigning ownership, and using a structured format (a spreadsheet or requirements tool) with consistent IDs so entries can be filtered and traced. The test of a good risk file is whether a reviewer can pick a hazard at random and follow it all the way to evidence that the control works — if they can't, the file isn't doing its job regardless of how thick it is.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a risk control that's implemented in software versus one implemented in hardware, in terms of what verification evidence the file needs? What would you do if, late in the project, a new hazard is identified that would require a design change to control adequately?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The decision hinges on whether the failure can contribute to an unacceptable hazardous situation — and that's a question about the failure's effect on the patient or operator, not about how the software is structured or how likely the failure is to occur. Likelihood affects the risk estimate, not whether the failure is safety-related in the first place.

My approach: start from the hazardous situations identified in the risk analysis, and work backward. For a given software failure, ask what the device does as a result, what the clinical consequence of that behavior is, and whether any independent risk control (hardware interlock, watchdog, a separate monitoring path, a clinician in the loop) would prevent the hazardous situation from reaching the patient. If the failure can lead to patient harm and nothing independent catches it, it's safety-related and needs a formal control and verification. If the failure only degrades usability, causes a nuisance alarm, or is caught by an independent protective measure, it may be a reliability or usability issue — but I'd document that reasoning rather than just asserting it, because "it's only a usability issue" is exactly the kind of judgment that needs a traceable rationale.

Two things I'd be careful about. First, don't let the software safety classification (IEC 62304 Class A/B/C) do this work for you — the classification is an output of the risk analysis, not a substitute for it, and a Class B system can still contain individual functions that are safety-related. Second, be wary of the "it's unlikely" trap: a failure that's rare but severe still needs a control if it can reach the patient, and the risk estimate should reflect severity properly rather than being averaged away.

The outcome of this analysis feeds directly into the software requirements and the test strategy — safety-related failures get requirements, traceability, and verification coverage commensurate with their risk, and the rest get ordinary reliability testing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that's safety-related only in combination with a second, independent failure? How would you document the rationale for classifying a failure as non-safety-related so it survives an audit?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** First, I'd separate the two questions that are getting tangled: is the hazard's risk actually negligible, and is the proposed control the right response if it isn't? Those are different conversations and conflating them is usually why these disagreements escalate.

I'd start by getting the quality manager's reasoning on the table explicitly — what severity and probability they're assigning, and on what basis. Sometimes the disagreement is a genuine difference in risk estimate (they may be considering a failure mode or use environment the engineering team hasn't), and sometimes it's a difference in interpretation of the acceptability criteria. Either way, the productive move is to make the estimate and the criteria explicit and compare them, rather than arguing about whether the hazard "feels" negligible. If the team's estimate was informal, this is the moment to formalize it — and if formalizing it shows the risk is actually higher than assumed, the quality manager is right and I should say so.

If the estimate genuinely supports "negligible" against the agreed criteria, I'd still take the concern seriously rather than dismissing it, because the quality manager is often flagging something real even when the proposed fix is disproportionate. I'd ask whether there's a lower-cost control that addresses the concern — a design margin, a verification test that demonstrates the hazard can't occur, a labeling or usability measure — because "add this control" and "do nothing" are rarely the only two options. Demonstrating through analysis or test that the hazard is adequately controlled can satisfy the concern without the schedule hit.

On the schedule impact: I'd be honest about it and bring it into the decision rather than using it as an argument against the control. If the control is warranted, the schedule absorbs it and we re-plan; if it isn't, the schedule is irrelevant to the decision. What I'd avoid is letting schedule pressure quietly override a legitimate safety concern, because that's exactly the failure mode that shows up badly later. And whatever we decide, I'd document the rationale and the decision in the risk file so it's traceable — including, if we decline the control, why the residual risk is acceptable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality manager has authority to block the design review until the control is added? What would you do if, after adding the control, verification showed it didn't actually reduce the risk as expected?
