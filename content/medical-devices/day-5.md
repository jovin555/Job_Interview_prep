# medical-devices — Day 5

## Q1: During IEC 60601-1 leakage current testing, you measure elevated patient leakage current on a BF-type applied part. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving this?

**Answer:** I'd start by confirming the measurement setup is correct — making sure the measuring device (MD) is properly connected between the applied part and earth, and that the device is operating in its normal and single-fault conditions as specified in the standard. Once I've verified the test setup, I'd systematically isolate the leakage path. The most common contributors are: (1) the power supply's Y-capacitors between primary and secondary sides, which can couple mains-frequency current to the patient circuit; (2) inadequate creepage/clearance distances in the isolation barrier; (3) parasitic capacitance across the isolation transformer or optocouplers; or (4) a damaged or incorrectly rated component in the patient circuit. I'd measure leakage current at different points — first with the patient circuit disconnected from the main board, then with individual sub-circuits reconnected one at a time — to identify which section contributes most. If the Y-capacitors are the culprit, I'd evaluate whether they can be reduced in value or relocated to a different point in the filter topology without compromising EMC performance. If the isolation transformer's inter-winding capacitance is too high, I might need a different transformer construction (e.g., with a Faraday shield). I'd also check that the PCB layout maintains the required creepage distances (typically 4 mm for 250 V working voltage at pollution degree 2 for BF applied parts) and that no solder flux residue or moisture is creating a conductive path. After implementing a fix, I'd repeat the test under all single-fault conditions, including mains voltage variations (+10%) to ensure margin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between leakage current caused by Y-capacitors versus transformer inter-winding capacitance? What would you do if reducing Y-capacitor values causes the device to fail radiated emissions testing?

## Q2: How would you approach creating a clinical evaluation report (CER) for a novel medical device that has no substantially equivalent predicate device on the market?

**Answer:** For a novel device with no predicate, the CER must rely on a combination of clinical investigation data and a thorough appraisal of available scientific literature, rather than equivalence claims. I'd start by defining the device's intended purpose, clinical indications, and target population precisely, as this frames the entire literature search strategy. I'd then conduct a systematic literature review using multiple databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane) with well-defined inclusion/exclusion criteria, searching for studies on the underlying clinical condition, alternative treatment modalities, and any related technologies that share similar operating principles or materials. The search protocol should be documented in advance to ensure reproducibility. For the literature appraisal, I'd use established tools like the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials or QUADAS-2 for diagnostic accuracy studies, and I'd present the evidence in structured tables showing study design, population, outcomes, and limitations. Where the literature alone cannot adequately demonstrate safety and performance, I'd identify the specific gaps that would need to be addressed through a clinical investigation — typically a prospective study under an approved clinical investigation plan (CIP). The CER should clearly state the limitations of the available evidence and justify why the benefit-risk profile remains acceptable. I'd also include a post-market clinical follow-up (PMCF) plan that describes how additional clinical data will be collected after market release to address remaining uncertainties. Throughout the process, I'd work closely with the regulatory affairs team and clinical experts to ensure the CER meets the requirements of MEDDEV 2.7/1 Rev.4 or the applicable EU MDR Annex XIV requirements.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the available literature shows conflicting results on the safety of a similar technology? What level of clinical evidence would you consider sufficient to support a CE marking application for a novel Class IIb device?

## Q3: A field complaint reports that a medical device's patient-contacting silicone sensor pad is causing skin irritation in several patients. How would you approach the investigation and corrective action process?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a potential serious adverse event and initiate a formal complaint investigation immediately. The first priority is patient safety — I'd work with the quality and clinical teams to determine whether a field safety corrective action (FSCA) is needed, such as issuing a clinical warning to stop using the affected lot or implementing enhanced monitoring. Simultaneously, I'd begin a root-cause investigation following a structured methodology like 8D or fishbone analysis. The investigation would have several parallel tracks: (1) Materials analysis — send retained samples and returned complaint units to a lab for biocompatibility testing per ISO 10993, including cytotoxicity, sensitization, and irritation testing. Compare the silicone formulation against the approved material specification and any supplier change notices. (2) Manufacturing review — examine batch records for the affected lots, looking for deviations in curing temperature, dwell time, or post-processing steps that could leave residual monomers or catalysts. (3) Clinical review — gather detailed information from the complainants: duration of wear, skin condition prior to use, concurrent medications, cleaning protocols, and whether the irritation pattern matches the sensor contact area. (4) Design review — check whether the material was originally qualified for the actual duration of patient contact (some silicones are tested for short-term contact but may cause issues with extended wear). If the root cause points to a material change by the supplier that wasn't properly qualified, I'd implement a supplier change control process requiring full re-qualification. If the issue is inherent to the material formulation, I'd initiate a design change to an alternative silicone or add a barrier layer. All findings would be documented in the risk management file, and the FSCA effectiveness would be verified through post-market surveillance data.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether this complaint triggers a reportable event to regulatory authorities? What criteria would you use to decide whether to recall the affected lot versus issuing a field correction?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C is the highest software safety classification under IEC 62304, meaning the software can contribute to a hazardous situation that results in death or serious injury. The verification approach must be correspondingly rigorous. I'd start by confirming that the software safety classification is correct by reviewing the hazard analysis — every software item that can contribute to a Class C hazard must be identified and traced. For verification, I'd structure the effort around the three main pillars of IEC 62304: software unit verification, integration testing, and system testing. At the unit level, every function must have documented verification — typically through code reviews and dynamic analysis (e.g., statement coverage, branch coverage, MC/DC coverage for the most critical modules). I'd use a static analysis tool to enforce coding standards (like MISRA C) and check for common defects. For integration testing, I'd verify that software units interact correctly, particularly at interfaces where data is passed between safety-critical and non-critical modules. This includes testing error handling — what happens when a sensor reading is out of range, a communication link fails, or a watchdog timer expires. At the system level, I'd execute the software test plan against the software requirements specification, with each test case traced to specific requirements and to the risk control measures they verify. For Class C, anomaly resolution is especially strict — all software anomalies must be documented, assessed for safety impact, and resolved before release, with no unresolved Class C anomalies permitted. I'd also ensure that the software development environment is qualified (compiler version, toolchain settings) and that all verification results are captured in the software development file with clear pass/fail criteria. Finally, I'd verify that the software release process includes a configuration audit confirming that only approved, verified code is included in the release build.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where achieving MC/DC coverage on a particular module is impractical due to compiler optimizations? What's your approach to verifying interrupt-driven code in a Class C system?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a medical device project. During a design review, the quality manager insists that a new risk control measure must be added to address a hazard with an estimated risk level that the team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a collaborative problem-solving exercise rather than a confrontation. First, I'd acknowledge the quality manager's concern and ask them to walk through their risk assessment in detail — specifically, how they arrived at the risk estimate and why they believe the current controls are insufficient. It's possible they have information the team doesn't, such as similar incidents from other projects or updated regulatory guidance. I'd then facilitate a structured discussion using the ISO 14971 risk management framework. We'd review the hazard identification, the initial risk estimation, and the existing risk control measures together. If the disagreement is about the severity of the potential harm, we might need to consult clinical experts or review published literature. If it's about the probability of occurrence, we could examine field data from similar devices or run a more detailed FMEA. If, after this analysis, the team still believes the risk is negligible (i.e., the residual risk is acceptable per the company's risk acceptability criteria), I'd document the discussion in the risk management file, including both perspectives and the rationale for the final decision. However, if the quality manager's concern raises a legitimate point that changes the risk estimate, I'd support adding the risk control measure — but I'd also ask the team to brainstorm alternative controls that might achieve the same risk reduction with less schedule impact. For example, could a software-based control be implemented more quickly than a hardware redesign? Could a labeling or training control be used as an interim measure while a hardware fix is developed for the next revision? I'd also work with the project manager to assess the true schedule impact — sometimes a control that seems disruptive can be phased in without delaying the critical path. Ultimately, if the quality manager and I still disagree after a thorough analysis, I'd escalate to the project steering committee or the designated safety officer for a final decision, ensuring that both positions are clearly documented. The key is to maintain a collaborative tone and keep patient safety as the shared objective, while also being transparent about the trade-offs.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the quality manager's concern is based on a regulatory interpretation that you believe is overly conservative? How would you handle a situation where adding the risk control measure would delay the product launch past a critical market window?
