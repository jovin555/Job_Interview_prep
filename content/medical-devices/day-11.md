# medical-devices — Day 11

## Q1: How would you approach designing a test strategy for verifying that a medical device's firmware correctly handles a sensor failure while the device is in continuous patient monitoring mode?

**Answer:** I'd start by identifying the failure modes of the specific sensor — open circuit, short circuit, out-of-range readings, stuck-at-value, and intermittent signal dropout — and then map each to the device's intended behavior per the requirements. The test strategy would be layered: at the unit level, I'd inject simulated sensor data into the firmware's input handling functions to verify each failure mode produces the correct state transition (e.g., entering a safe mode, generating an alarm, or continuing with degraded functionality). At the integration level, I'd use hardware fault injection — physically disconnecting the sensor, shorting pins, or using a signal generator to produce out-of-range waveforms — to verify the firmware correctly detects the fault through the actual analog front-end and communication interface. At the system level, I'd run long-duration soak tests with fault injection at random intervals to verify the device recovers correctly and doesn't latch into an unsafe state. A key consideration is the distinction between detection and response: the firmware must not only detect the fault but also decide whether to continue monitoring with reduced capability, switch to a redundant sensor, or shut down, depending on the risk assessment. I'd also verify that fault handling doesn't interfere with other critical functions — for example, that an alarm triggered by one sensor failure doesn't prevent the device from continuing to log data from other sensors. Finally, I'd trace each test case back to the specific requirement and risk control measure it verifies, so the test evidence directly supports the design verification and risk management files.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between continuing with degraded functionality versus shutting down when a sensor fails? How would you test the firmware's behavior when multiple sensors fail simultaneously?

## Q2: During a design review for a medical device that uses a pressure sensor for respiratory monitoring, the firmware lead proposes using a digital filter to smooth the sensor signal, while the hardware engineer argues that the analog front-end should include a hardware low-pass filter instead. How would you approach this trade-off?

**Answer:** I'd frame the discussion around the system requirements rather than treating it as an either/or decision. The key questions are: what is the frequency content of the physiological signal we're trying to capture, what noise sources are present, and what are the latency and safety implications of each filtering approach? An analog filter has the advantage of removing out-of-band noise before it reaches the ADC, which can prevent aliasing and reduce the dynamic range requirements of the converter. It also provides deterministic behavior that doesn't depend on firmware execution. However, analog filters introduce phase delay, component tolerance issues, and are difficult to modify after the PCB is fabricated. A digital filter offers flexibility — you can adjust coefficients in firmware without hardware changes — and can achieve sharper roll-off characteristics than an equivalent analog filter. The risk is that if the noise is large enough to saturate the analog front-end or cause aliasing before the ADC, no digital filter can recover the signal. My approach would be to use a combination: a minimal analog anti-aliasing filter with a cutoff frequency comfortably above the signal bandwidth, followed by digital filtering in firmware for the specific noise rejection and signal conditioning needed. This is a common architecture in medical devices because it provides robustness against out-of-band interference while maintaining flexibility. I'd also consider the IEC 60601-1-2 immunity implications — if the noise is coming from radiated RF, the analog filter's performance under interference should be verified, not just its nominal frequency response. Finally, I'd ensure the filtering strategy is documented in the design inputs and that the verification plan includes testing with realistic noise profiles, not just clean signals, to confirm the combined analog/digital approach meets the accuracy requirements.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify that the combined analog and digital filtering doesn't introduce unacceptable latency in the monitoring path? What if the noise source is intermittent rather than continuous — how would that change your approach?

## Q3: How would you approach developing a post-market surveillance plan for a Class II medical device that has been on the market for two years with no reported serious incidents?

**Answer:** I'd start by recognizing that the absence of reported incidents doesn't necessarily mean the device is performing as intended — it could mean that issues aren't being recognized, reported, or properly categorized. The plan would have several complementary components. First, I'd establish a systematic process for collecting and analyzing complaint data, including not just the complaints themselves but also the context: usage environment, patient population, operator training, and device configuration. This data should be trended over time, not just reviewed case-by-case, because emerging patterns often only become visible in aggregate. Second, I'd include proactive surveillance activities beyond passive complaint handling — for example, periodic literature reviews for similar devices, monitoring of regulatory databases for recalls or safety alerts on comparable products, and engagement with clinical users to understand how the device is actually being used in practice versus how it was intended to be used. Third, I'd define clear triggers for deeper investigation: a change in complaint frequency, a cluster of similar issues, or any report that suggests a potential safety concern even if it doesn't meet the regulatory definition of a serious incident. The plan should also specify how post-market data feeds back into the risk management file — specifically, whether the original risk estimates remain valid given real-world usage patterns. For example, if the device was designed assuming daily cleaning but post-market data shows that some users clean it less frequently, that's a discrepancy between intended use and actual use that needs to be addressed. Finally, I'd ensure the plan includes periodic reporting to regulatory bodies as required, and that the surveillance activities are documented so they can be audited. The key principle is that post-market surveillance is not just about reacting to problems — it's about actively confirming that the device continues to meet its safety and performance requirements throughout its lifecycle.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific data sources would you monitor beyond your own complaint database? How would you determine whether a trend in complaints warrants a field safety corrective action versus continued monitoring?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's real-time clock (RTC) maintains accurate time across power cycles, given that the device logs time-stamped physiological data that may be used in clinical decision-making?

**Answer:** I'd approach this from two angles: the hardware design considerations and the verification strategy. From a hardware perspective, the RTC needs a backup power source — typically a coin cell battery or a supercapacitor — to maintain time when the main power is off. The design needs to handle the transition between main power and backup power without glitches, and the RTC's timekeeping accuracy depends on the crystal oscillator, which is sensitive to temperature and load capacitance. I'd verify that the crystal's specified accuracy is sufficient for the clinical requirements — for example, if the data needs to be correlated with other devices or with medication administration times, the required accuracy might be tighter than if the timestamps are only used for relative ordering. The verification strategy would include several test cases: power cycling at various points in the RTC's update cycle to ensure no corruption of the time registers, extended power-off periods to verify the backup source holds the time within specification, and temperature variation to confirm the crystal's accuracy across the device's operating range. I'd also test the behavior when the backup source is depleted — the device should detect this condition and either prompt for time setting or flag the data as having uncertain timestamps. A subtle but important issue is the transition between main power and backup power: if the RTC is being written at the moment power is lost, there's a risk of partial writes that corrupt the time. I'd verify that the firmware handles this by using a checksum or by writing time registers in a specific order, and I'd test this by cutting power at random points during a write cycle. Finally, I'd consider whether the device needs to synchronize its clock with an external time source — for example, over a network or from a clinician's action — and verify that the synchronization logic handles edge cases like time zone changes or daylight saving transitions without corrupting the time-stamped data.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine the acceptable time drift for the device's clinical use case? What would you do if the RTC's accuracy under temperature variation doesn't meet the requirement?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a medical device project. During a design review, the marketing team presents customer feedback indicating that clinicians want the device to provide audible alerts in addition to the visual alerts currently implemented. The firmware team estimates this would require two weeks of additional work and would delay the regulatory submission. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd approach this by separating the immediate decision from the underlying process question. The immediate decision requires understanding the regulatory and safety implications of adding audible alerts. If the audible alerts are intended to convey safety-critical information — for example, alerting clinicians to a patient condition that requires attention — then this is a design change that affects the device's risk profile and usability, not just a feature enhancement. In that case, the change would need to go through the full design change process: updating the design inputs, assessing the impact on the risk management file, updating the usability engineering file per IEC 60601-1-6, and potentially repeating some verification and validation activities. Two weeks of firmware work is likely an underestimate of the total impact when you account for these activities. On the other hand, if the audible alerts are purely a convenience feature — for example, a different sound for a non-critical notification — the regulatory impact might be minimal, but it still needs to be assessed. My approach would be to facilitate a structured discussion where the marketing team presents the clinical rationale for the audible alerts, the firmware team presents the technical estimate, and the quality/regulatory team presents the compliance impact. I'd then work with the team to develop options: implement the change now and accept the delay, implement it in a future revision, or implement a partial version that addresses the most critical alerts. I'd also consider whether there's a workaround — for example, whether the existing visual alerts could be enhanced in a way that addresses the clinician feedback without requiring a full design change. The key is to make the decision based on data and risk assessment rather than on which team is most persuasive, and to document the decision and its rationale in the design history file. If we decide to defer the change, I'd ensure there's a clear process for capturing it as a design input for the next revision, so it doesn't get lost.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the audible alert change is safety-critical or purely a usability enhancement? What if the marketing team insists that the audible alerts are essential for competitive positioning, even though they're not clinically necessary?
