# medical-devices — Day 94

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start from the standard rather than from the schematic. IEC 60601-1 defines the measurement network — the MD (measuring device) with its specified input impedance and frequency weighting — and the allowable limits differ by applied-part classification, by condition (normal condition vs. single-fault condition), and by whether the current is patient leakage, earth leakage, or touch current. So the first step is to build a matrix: for each applied part, list its classification, the applicable leakage current types, the NC and SFC limits, and the source of each limit (general standard plus any particular standard). That matrix becomes the test plan.

For the measurement path itself, the practical concerns are: (1) the measurement network must match the standard's defined impedance — you can't substitute a generic DMM; (2) the measurement must be made with the device in its worst-case operating mode, which often means identifying which power state, which applied-part configuration, and which single-fault condition produces the highest current; and (3) the reference point matters — patient leakage is measured from the applied part to earth through the MD, and for multiple applied parts you have to consider both the "each applied part individually" case and the "all applied parts tied together" case, since the standard treats these differently.

Deciding which measurements are required comes down to: every applied part gets measured in every applicable condition, but the classification determines the limit and sometimes the network. A CF-type part has a tighter limit than a BF-type part, so the CF part usually drives the design. If the device has both, I'd measure each independently and also measure the combination where the standard requires it, and I'd document the rationale for any measurement I conclude is not required — that rationale is part of the verification record, not an afterthought.

The design implication is that the isolation scheme has to satisfy the tightest applicable limit across all parts and conditions, so the measurement plan and the isolation design are developed together, not sequentially.

**Follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a single-fault condition needs to be simulated by opening a protective earth conductor, by interrupting a component, or both? And how would you handle a device where the CF-type applied part is only connected during a specific operating mode?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it usually means something in the device is resonant or is being demodulated at that frequency, rather than a broad susceptibility problem. My first step is to characterize the failure precisely: sweep the band in fine steps, record the exact frequency, the field strength at which the malfunction begins, the modulation (CW vs. AM), and the device state at the time. That tells me whether it's a threshold effect (a little more field and it fails) or a sharp resonance.

Then I'd try to localize it physically. With the device running and monitored, I'd probe with a small hand-held field source or use a near-field probe to see which sub-circuit or cable is most sensitive — is it the sensor front-end, the power input, a specific cable harness, or the enclosure seam? Cable length is a common culprit: a harness that happens to be a quarter-wave or half-wave at the offending frequency acts as an antenna and couples energy into a high-impedance node. I'd also check whether the failure disappears when a particular cable is shortened, rerouted, or fitted with a ferrite, which points to a common-mode coupling path.

On the resolution side, the options are usually: improve the coupling path (ferrites, common-mode chokes, cable shielding and termination, rerouting), improve the victim's immunity (filtering at the affected input, adding hysteresis or digital filtering in firmware, tightening the analog front-end's common-mode rejection), or shift the resonance (change a cable length or a decoupling network so the resonance moves out of the test band). I'd prefer fixes at the coupling path and the victim's front-end because they're more robust than tuning to a single frequency, and I'd re-test across the full band, not just the failing point, to make sure I haven't moved the problem.

**Follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a resonance in a cable from a resonance in the enclosure or a PCB structure? And if the fix is a firmware filter, how would you verify it doesn't degrade the device's specified accuracy or response time?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The failure mode I'd design against is the "risk file as a retrospective artifact" — a binder assembled the week before submission that nobody used during design. To avoid that, I'd make the risk management file a living set of linked records that are updated as design decisions are made, not after.

Structurally, I'd anchor it on ISO 14971's process: risk management plan (scope, responsibilities, criteria for risk acceptability, how verification will be done), hazard identification and risk estimation, risk evaluation against the acceptability criteria, risk control (with the hierarchy of control options — inherently safe design first, then protective measures, then information for safety), verification of control implementation and effectiveness, and residual risk evaluation with overall benefit-risk. Each record should trace forward and backward: a hazard links to the design input it affects, to the risk control, to the verification evidence that the control works, and to the residual risk statement.

The practical habits that keep it useful: (1) hold risk reviews alongside design reviews so hazards are identified while the design is still fluid; (2) make the risk file the single source for design inputs that come from risk controls, so the DHF traceability is a byproduct rather than a separate exercise; (3) keep the acceptability criteria explicit and agreed up front, so evaluation isn't a debate each time; and (4) treat changes — a component substitution, a firmware change, a supplier change — as triggers to revisit the affected hazard records, with a documented impact assessment.

Auditability then comes almost for free: an auditor can pick any hazard, follow the links to the control and its verification, and see the residual risk decision. The file stays useful because the engineering team is actually using it to make decisions.

**Follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard where the risk control is "information for safety" in the labeling — how do you verify that control is effective? And how would you keep the risk file consistent when the design changes late in the project?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** I'd resist classifying by the software artifact alone and instead trace the failure to its clinical effect. The question isn't "is this a bug in safety code?" but "if this failure occurs, what happens to the patient, and how severe and how likely is that harm?" That's a risk management question, and the answer comes from the hazard analysis, not from the software architecture.

Concretely: I'd take the failure mode, walk it through the device's intended use and the clinical workflow, and ask what the user or patient experiences. If the failure can lead to incorrect therapy delivery, a missed or delayed alarm, or misleading physiological data that a clinician acts on, it's safety-related and needs a risk control with verification. If the failure degrades convenience, requires a restart, or produces an obviously wrong display that the user can recognize and recover from without clinical consequence, it may be a reliability or usability issue — but I'd still document the reasoning, because "obviously wrong" is an assumption that needs to be tested.

Two traps I'd watch for. First, assuming that because a function is "non-critical" its failure is non-safety — a logging or UI failure can starve a safety-critical task of CPU or memory, so the failure's effect on the rest of the system matters, not just its own function. Second, letting the software safety classification (IEC 62304 Class A/B/C) drive the answer — the classification is an output of the risk analysis, not an input, and a Class A component can still contribute to a hazardous situation if its failure propagates.

The output of this reasoning should be a documented decision: the failure mode, the hazard it could contribute to, the severity and probability estimate, and either a risk control or a justification for why no control is needed. That record is what makes the classification defensible later.

**Follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that is safety-related only in combination with a second independent failure? And how would you decide whether a firmware watchdog reset counts as a risk control or as a mitigation of a different hazard?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a disagreement about the risk estimate, not about whether quality "wins." The quality manager is usually reacting to something the engineering team has discounted — a low-probability but high-severity event, or a hazard whose probability estimate rests on an assumption nobody has written down. So my first move is to get the disagreement onto paper: what hazard, what severity, what probability, what's the basis for each estimate, and what does the risk management plan's acceptability criteria say about that combination. Often the conversation resolves once both sides are looking at the same record.

If the estimates genuinely differ, I'd look at what evidence would settle it — bench data, field data from a similar device, a literature review, a fault-tree analysis — and propose getting that evidence rather than arguing from intuition. If the hazard is high-severity even at low probability, the acceptability criteria may already require a control regardless of the probability estimate, in which case the quality manager is right and the engineering team's "negligible" was the wrong frame.

If, after that, the team still believes the control is disproportionate, I'd escalate the decision rather than let it stall: present both positions, the evidence, and the schedule impact to the design review board or the responsible management, and get a documented decision. What I would not do is quietly drop the control or quietly accept a schedule slip without a record — either outcome is fine if it's a documented, reasoned decision; neither is fine if it's a hallway compromise.

On the schedule side, I'd also ask whether the control can be implemented in a way that doesn't carry the full cost — a design change that's cheaper than the one first proposed, or a control that can be added in a later revision with an interim mitigation. Sometimes the "significant schedule impact" is an artifact of the first proposed solution, not of the requirement.

**Follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's concern turned out to be based on a misreading of the standard? And how would you make sure the decision — whichever way it went — is captured in the risk management file and the design review record?
