# medical-devices — Day 106

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement matrix, because the required measurements fall out of that matrix rather than from a single "leakage current" number. The key distinction is that BF and CF parts have different limits and, more importantly, different fault conditions that must be applied. For each applied part I'd enumerate: patient leakage current from the applied part to earth under normal condition, patient leakage current under single-fault conditions (open earth, open supply conductor), and — where the part is intended to be connected to other equipment — the relevant patient auxiliary current. CF parts carry tighter limits and, critically, must be evaluated with the mains voltage applied to the applied part under single-fault conditions, which is the test that most often exposes a design weakness.

For the measurement path itself, I'd build a dedicated test fixture rather than probing the device in situ. The fixture needs a low-impedance, well-defined measurement network that matches the standard's specified impedance (the measuring device is defined by the standard, not by convenience), a way to switch between the applied parts and the reference earth without disturbing the DUT, and guarding so that the measurement leads themselves don't pick up ambient fields and inflate the reading. I'd keep the patient-side wiring short and shielded, and I'd verify the fixture's own residual leakage with a dummy load before trusting any DUT measurement — otherwise you can spend a day chasing a "failure" that's actually your test setup.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes down to three filters: which applied parts are simultaneously accessible to the patient, which are simultaneously accessible to the operator, and which fault conditions the standard mandates for each classification. You don't measure every combination — you measure the combinations the standard defines as worst-case for the intended use. I'd document that mapping explicitly in the test plan so a reviewer can see why each measurement exists and why others were excluded, because "we didn't measure that" is the first question an auditor asks.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where a BF part and a CF part are simultaneously connected to the patient — does the classification of one affect the limits applied to the other? What would you do if a measurement sits just under the limit but the fixture's own uncertainty is a meaningful fraction of the margin?

## Q2: How would you approach developing a design verification test plan for a medical device that must measure both temperature and pressure, where the two sensors share a common ADC and multiplexer, and the device must maintain specified accuracy for both parameters simultaneously?

**Answer:** The shared ADC and multiplexer is the crux, so I'd build the test plan around the interactions rather than testing each channel in isolation. The risks are: crosstalk between channels through the multiplexer (charge injection, settling time, residual charge on the sampling capacitor), reference drift affecting both channels identically, and timing — if the mux scan rate is slow, a fast-changing pressure signal may be sampled at a different instant than the temperature, and any test that assumes simultaneity will be misleading.

I'd structure the plan in layers. First, per-channel accuracy verification against traceable references, sweeping the full specified range at multiple points, with the other channel held at a representative mid-scale value — this establishes the baseline and catches gross errors. Second, interaction testing: drive one channel with a full-scale step while the other is at a known value, and check that the second channel's reading doesn't move beyond its error budget. That's the test that catches mux settling and charge-injection problems. Third, simultaneous-accuracy testing across the operating envelope — both channels varying, at the extremes of the specified temperature range, because ADC reference and mux behavior drift with temperature and the worst case is usually at a corner, not at room ambient.

I'd also define the acceptance criteria in terms of the error budget, not just "within spec." If the total accuracy spec is, say, ±1 unit, I want to know how much of that is allocated to the ADC, the mux, the sensor, and the reference, so that when a test marginally fails I can localize the cause instead of guessing. And I'd include a repeatability/uncertainty analysis of the test setup itself — if the reference equipment's uncertainty is comparable to the margin, the test isn't actually verifying the spec.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you test the settling behavior of the multiplexer without access to the internal sampling node? If the two channels have very different source impedances, how does that change your test approach?

## Q3: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means a software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation with unacceptable risk, so the verification burden is about demonstrating that the software is correct by construction and by test, not just that it "works." I'd approach it in three strands: process evidence, structural coverage, and requirements-based verification.

Process evidence first: the software development plan, the architecture description showing segregation between safety-critical and non-critical functions, the detailed design, and the unit-level verification records. For Class C the standard expects the full set of activities — architecture design, detailed design, unit verification, integration testing, and system testing — with traceability from each requirement down to the test that verifies it and back up to the risk control it implements. I'd make sure the traceability matrix is a living artifact, because reconstructing it at the end is where these efforts usually fall apart.

Structural coverage is where Class C really bites. I'd target statement coverage plus branch coverage at minimum, and for the safety-critical functions I'd push toward MC/DC where the tooling supports it, with a documented justification for any coverage gap — an unreachable branch needs a rationale, not just an exclusion. The coverage analysis has to be tied to the actual released binary or a build traceable to it, not a debug build.

Requirements-based verification is the third strand: every safety-related requirement needs a test that exercises it, including the fault-handling paths — watchdog behavior, sensor plausibility checks, safe-state entry. I'd also include negative testing and boundary testing, and I'd verify that the risk control measures identified in the risk management file are actually implemented and tested in software, because that's the link auditors follow from ISO 14971 into IEC 62304.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a safety-critical function that can't achieve full branch coverage because of defensive code for conditions the hardware can't produce? What's your approach when a unit test passes but the coverage tool reports the branch as not taken?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes calibration on the bench but reads high in the field is the whole clue — it means the discrepancy is context-dependent, so the investigation has to reproduce the field context rather than re-run the bench test. I'd start by characterizing the complaint: how much higher, in which use scenarios, with which probe lots, and whether it correlates with a particular clinical setting or patient population. A consistent offset across many units points to a design or specification issue; a scattered offset points to a unit-level or use-level cause.

Then I'd enumerate the differences between field and bench. The big ones for a patient-contacting temperature sensor are: thermal contact and placement (the probe may be reading its own self-heating or the ambient rather than the patient), the thermal mass and response time of the probe versus the reference thermometer, the reference thermometer's own calibration and placement, and the possibility that the device is reading correctly but the displayed value is offset by a firmware scaling or cold-junction compensation error that only manifests at certain ambient temperatures. Self-heating is a classic — a sensor biased continuously can read above true temperature, and on the bench with a well-stirred bath that error may be masked.

I'd also look at the calibration check itself: what does it actually verify? If it's a single-point check at one temperature, it won't catch a gain error or a non-linearity that shows up across the clinical range. I'd want a multi-point calibration verification across the full operating range, plus a check of the probe's time response and its behavior when the ambient around the device differs from the patient's temperature.

The corrective action depends on root cause: if it's a design issue (self-heating, compensation), it's a design change with verification; if it's a use issue (placement, technique), it may be a labeling or training fix; if it's a lot-specific probe issue, it's a supplier and incoming-inspection action. Either way I'd feed it back through the risk management file and the complaint-handling process, because a consistent offset that clinical staff notice is a potential use-error or measurement-accuracy hazard even if no harm has occurred.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish self-heating from a genuine measurement error in a bench test? If the offset only appears in a subset of units, what would that tell you about the manufacturing process?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a disagreement about risk estimation, not about whether the quality manager is being unreasonable — and I'd resolve it with the risk management process rather than with opinion. The first step is to get both sides to state their reasoning explicitly: what severity and probability the engineering team assigned, what the quality manager thinks they should be, and on what evidence. Very often the disagreement is traceable to a missing data point — the engineering team may be assuming a failure mode is rare based on lab experience, while the quality manager is reasoning from the clinical use environment or from a similar device's field history. Once the assumptions are on the table, the gap usually narrows or the disagreement becomes concrete enough to resolve.

If it doesn't resolve, I'd go back to the risk acceptability criteria that were agreed at the start of the project. The question isn't "is this hazard negligible in our judgment" but "does the estimated risk, against our documented criteria, require a control measure?" If the estimate genuinely sits below the threshold, that's a defensible position and I'd document the rationale and the evidence behind the estimate. If it sits near the boundary, the conservative choice is usually to add the control — the cost of a control measure is almost always less than the cost of a field issue or a regulatory finding, and "we judged it negligible and were wrong" is a very hard position to defend.

On schedule: I'd separate the question of whether the control is needed from the question of how to implement it without derailing the plan. Sometimes the control can be implemented as a firmware check, a labeling change, or a manufacturing test rather than a hardware revision, which changes the schedule impact entirely. I'd bring that options analysis to the discussion rather than treating "add the control" as a single fixed cost. And I'd make sure the decision and its rationale are recorded in the risk management file either way, so the review has a documented outcome rather than an unresolved argument.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's position was based on a hazard you genuinely believed was already adequately controlled by an existing measure? How would you handle it if the disagreement persisted after the risk analysis was updated and the schedule impact was still significant?
