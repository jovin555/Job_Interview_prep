# medical-devices — Day 116

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement topology, because the required measurements fall out of that map rather than from a generic checklist. For a device with both BF and CF applied parts, the relevant patient leakage current measurements are: patient leakage current from each applied part to earth (with the applied part connected to the measurement network), patient leakage current between applied parts of different classifications where the standard requires it, and patient leakage current with mains voltage applied to the applied part under single-fault conditions. The key decision is which applied parts can be measured individually versus which must be measured as a group — if two applied parts are galvanically connected inside the device, they form a single patient circuit for measurement purposes, and treating them as separate can produce a measurement that doesn't reflect the real fault path.

Practically, I'd build a measurement matrix: rows are the applied parts (and any intentional groupings), columns are the normal-condition and single-fault-condition cases the standard specifies, and each cell references the applicable limit and the measurement setup. That matrix becomes the test procedure and the traceability artifact. For CF parts the limits are tighter and the isolation is more demanding, so I'd verify the CF path first — if it passes, the BF path is usually easier, and if it fails, the problem is more likely to be in the isolation design than in the measurement setup. I'd also confirm the measurement network and the leakage current meter's input impedance match what the standard specifies, because a meter with the wrong impedance can make a passing device look marginal or vice versa.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether two applied parts that share a connector but are isolated from each other internally should be measured separately or together? What would you do if a CF applied part passes in normal condition but fails under a single-fault condition that the standard requires you to test?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue that something in the device is resonant at that frequency — either a cable harness acting as an antenna, a slot or seam in the enclosure, or a trace/component combination with a self-resonance near the test frequency. I'd start by confirming the failure is genuinely RF-induced and not a test-setup artifact: repeat with the device in a different orientation, with cables dressed differently, and with the field applied at a slightly different modulation, to see whether the band moves or disappears. If it tracks the frequency regardless of orientation, it's likely internal; if it moves with cable position, it's likely a cable/common-mode coupling path.

Then I'd narrow the coupling path systematically. First, isolate which interface is affected — does the malfunction occur with the sensor cable disconnected, with the wireless module disabled, with the display cable shortened? That tells me whether the energy is entering through a cable, through the enclosure, or through the power input. Second, I'd add temporary ferrites or shielding to the suspected path and re-test; if the band shifts or the failure threshold rises, I've found the path. Third, I'd look at the affected circuit itself — is there a high-impedance node, an unshielded loop, or a reference that's picking up the field? Common fixes are common-mode chokes on the offending cable, a small RC or ferrite bead at the affected input, improved grounding of the shield, or a change in cable routing inside the enclosure. I'd avoid adding a blanket filter without understanding the path, because it can trade one band for another or degrade signal integrity.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cable-coupled resonance from an enclosure resonance without a full anechoic chamber? If the fix requires a PCB change, how would you decide whether to do a board respin or a cable/harness-level fix given schedule pressure?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file stays useful if it's built as a living traceability structure rather than a retrospective narrative. I'd structure it around a hazard analysis that's created early — at concept and architecture — and then maintained as a controlled document with clear ownership and review cadence. The core artifacts are: the risk management plan (scope, responsibilities, criteria for risk acceptability, review points), the hazard/hazardous situation analysis, the risk estimation and evaluation records, the risk control measures with their verification evidence, and the residual risk evaluation and overall benefit-risk conclusion. Each risk control should link forward to the design output that implements it and the verification evidence that proves it works, and backward to the hazard it addresses.

The practical discipline is to make the risk file a gate in the design process, not a deliverable at the end. Every design review should include a risk review item; every design change should trigger a risk impact assessment; every verification test that closes a risk control should update the file in the same cycle. I'd use a traceability matrix that maps hazard → risk control → design output → verification evidence → residual risk, so an auditor can follow any hazard to its closure without hunting through folders. I'd also keep a change log in the file itself, so the history of risk decisions is visible. The test of whether it's working is simple: if a new engineer joins mid-project and can answer "why does this design look like this?" from the risk file alone, it's structured well.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a risk control that's implemented in firmware, where the verification evidence is a test that's hard to make deterministic? What would you do if a design change late in the project invalidated a risk control that had already been verified?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means the software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation with unacceptable risk, so the verification has to demonstrate that the safety-related requirements are met with a level of rigor that matches that consequence. I'd start from the software requirements specification and the architecture: every safety-related requirement needs a verification method, and the architecture needs to show how the safety-related functions are segregated from non-safety functions and how failures are detected or mitigated. The verification evidence then has three layers. Unit-level: static analysis, code review against coding standards, and unit tests with coverage targets appropriate to Class C — typically statement and branch coverage, with justification for any untested paths. Integration-level: interface testing between software units and between software and hardware, including fault injection to confirm that the safety mechanisms actually respond. System-level: verification against the software requirements in the target environment, including abnormal-condition testing — sensor failures, memory corruption, timing overruns, watchdog behavior.

The documentation has to show the chain: requirement → design → code → test → result, with the test procedures and records under configuration control. I'd also make sure the problem resolution process is visible — defects found during verification, their severity, their disposition, and the regression evidence that closed them. For Class C specifically, I'd expect the reviewer to ask about independence: who wrote the code versus who reviewed and tested it, and whether the verification was performed by someone other than the author. That independence is part of the argument, not an afterthought.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you justify a coverage gap in a safety-related module if the untested path is defensive code that's hard to trigger? How would you handle a Class C requirement whose verification depends on a hardware behavior you can't fully control in the test environment?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a risk-evaluation disagreement to be resolved with evidence, not as a schedule negotiation. First, I'd ask the quality manager to walk through the hazard, the hazardous situation, and the estimated severity and probability, and I'd ask the engineering team to do the same — often the disagreement is about different assumptions (e.g., the quality manager is considering a use error the team assumed away, or the team is estimating probability from lab experience while the quality manager is using field data). Getting both estimates on the table, with their basis, usually narrows the gap or reveals that one side is missing information.

If the estimates still diverge, I'd apply the risk acceptability criteria from the risk management plan — the criteria were agreed up front, so the decision should follow them rather than whoever argues hardest. If the hazard falls below the acceptability threshold under the agreed criteria, the correct outcome is to document the evaluation and the rationale, not to add a control that isn't justified. If it's near the threshold, I'd consider whether a lower-cost control (a labeling change, a software guard, a test that demonstrates the hazard can't occur) could address the concern without the full schedule impact. If the quality manager still believes the risk is unacceptable, I'd escalate to the designated risk management reviewer or the management review process — that's what it's for, and it keeps the decision documented and defensible rather than resolved informally.

What I'd avoid is either capitulating to avoid conflict or dismissing the concern because the team is confident. Both leave the file weaker. The goal is a documented, criteria-based decision that the quality manager can sign off on and that an auditor can follow.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the risk management plan's acceptability criteria were ambiguous and both interpretations were defensible? How would you communicate the outcome to the clinical or marketing stakeholders who are affected by the schedule impact?
