# medical-devices — Day 58

## Q1: How would you approach determining the required creepage and clearance distances on a medical PCB that has a mains-referenced primary side, a secondary side, and a patient-connected applied part, where the applied part must be isolated from mains by two means of patient protection (2×MOPP)?
**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the insulation diagram before touching layout — identifying every boundary that needs isolation and what each boundary must withstand. For a 2×MOPP barrier between mains and the patient-connected part, the key inputs are the working voltage across the barrier, the pollution degree of the environment, the material group of the PCB laminate, and the altitude the device is rated for. Those feed the creepage and clearance tables in IEC 60601-1, and I'd apply the higher of the two MOPP requirements rather than treating them as additive in a naive way — the standard defines how the two means combine.

Practically, I'd separate the design into three zones: primary (mains-referenced), secondary (SELV, operator-accessible), and the patient-connected applied part. The 2×MOPP barrier sits between primary and the applied part, and I'd also need at least 1×MOPP between primary and secondary. On the layout side, that means a clearly marked isolation slot or routed barrier under the isolation component, no copper crossing the barrier except through the rated isolation device, and keeping the creepage path measured along the surface — including around any slots, vias, or component pads that could shorten it. I'd also check that the isolation component itself (transformer, optocoupler, digital isolator, or isolation amplifier) is rated for the full working voltage plus transients, not just the steady-state.

The clearance side is about the air gap and is driven by transient overvoltages, so I'd verify against the impulse withstand voltage for the applicable overvoltage category. For a patient-connected part, I'd also confirm the applied part classification (BF vs CF) because that changes the leakage current limits and sometimes the isolation strategy. Finally, I'd document the insulation diagram and the creepage/clearance calculations in the DHF so the rationale is traceable, and I'd flag any place where the mechanical enclosure or a connector pin could reduce the effective distance after assembly.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you verify the barrier after assembly, and what would you do if a hi-pot test showed margin that was tighter than your calculation predicted?
- If the isolation component's datasheet gives a working voltage but not a transient rating, how would you decide whether it's acceptable for 2×MOPP?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a medical device that must pass both conducted emissions and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?
**Answer:** I'd treat EMC filtering as a system-level design problem rather than a bolt-on at the end. The first step is understanding the noise sources and their frequency content — switching power supplies, motor drives, digital clocks, and any high-speed interfaces — and the coupling paths, both differential (on the conductors) and common-mode (through parasitic capacitance to chassis and cables). Conducted emissions are dominated by differential-mode noise at lower frequencies and common-mode noise at higher frequencies, so the filter topology needs to address both.

For a mains-input device, I'd typically start with a common-mode choke plus X-capacitors across the line and Y-capacitors to chassis, sized against the leakage current limit — which is the key constraint in medical devices, because the Y-capacitance that would normally help you pass emissions directly increases earth leakage current and can push you over the IEC 60601-1 limit. That tension is the central design trade-off: you can't just throw capacitance at the problem. So I'd look at reducing the source noise first — slowing switching edges where the efficiency budget allows, adding snubbers, improving return path layout — before adding filter components.

For radiated emissions, the layout matters as much as the filter. I'd keep high-di/dt loops small, provide a solid reference plane, avoid splitting ground returns under high-speed traces, and control cable routing and shielding. Cables are often the dominant antenna, so common-mode chokes on I/O cables and proper shield termination to chassis can do more than any board-level change. I'd also make sure the chassis ground and signal ground are connected at a single defined point to avoid ground loops that radiate.

Throughout, I'd keep the signal-integrity requirements in view — a filter that fixes emissions but adds too much series impedance or parasitic capacitance on a sensor line can degrade accuracy or bandwidth. So I'd verify the filter's effect on the signal path with the same rigor as its effect on emissions, and I'd plan pre-compliance testing early rather than waiting for the formal lab slot.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide between a ferrite bead and an LC filter on a sensor signal line, given that both affect emissions but only one is likely to preserve signal integrity?
- If the device passes conducted emissions but fails radiated emissions at a specific frequency, how would you narrow down whether the source is the board, a cable, or the enclosure?

## Q3: How would you approach classifying a device's software under IEC 62304, and what would you do if the hardware and software leads disagreed on the classification?
**Answer:** IEC 62304 classification is driven by the harm that a software failure could cause, not by the complexity of the software or how much of the system it controls. Class A is no injury or damage to health possible; Class B is non-serious injury possible; Class C is death or serious injury possible. The classification is a property of the software item in the context of the device's risk analysis, so the starting point is the hazard analysis — what hazardous situations can arise, and can software contribute to or fail to mitigate them?

The key subtlety is that a device that "only monitors" isn't automatically Class A. If the software's failure could cause a missed alarm that leads to serious harm, or if it could display misleading data that a clinician acts on, that can push it to Class B or C. So I'd work from the risk file, not from a gut feeling about how "critical" the software feels. I'd also distinguish between software that directly controls therapy and software that supports a clinical decision — both can be high classification depending on the harm pathway.

If the hardware and software leads disagreed, I'd bring it back to the risk analysis as the objective arbiter. I'd ask: what hazardous situation could arise from a software failure, what's the severity of the resulting harm, and is there an independent hardware or procedural risk control that reduces the severity? If the disagreement persists, that's a signal the risk analysis itself is incomplete or ambiguous, and I'd escalate to the risk management lead and quality/regulatory to get a documented decision. The classification drives the rigor of the software development process — architecture, detailed design, unit verification, and so on — so getting it wrong in either direction has real cost: too low and you under-verify, too high and you over-invest. I'd want the decision recorded with its rationale in the DHF so it's defensible during review.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If a risk control implemented in hardware reduces the severity of a software failure, how does that affect the software classification?
- How would you handle a situation where the classification changes late in development because the risk analysis was updated?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?
**Answer:** The fact that the device passes calibration at the manufacturer but reads high in the field is the key clue — it points to something about the field conditions, the measurement setup, or the comparison method rather than a simple sensor drift. I'd start by characterizing the complaint: how many units, what clinical environment, what reference thermometer was used, and whether the offset is consistent in magnitude and direction. A consistent offset suggests a systematic cause; a variable one suggests a field-dependent effect.

Then I'd look at the measurement chain. A patient-contacting temperature sensor's reading depends on thermal contact with the patient, the thermal mass and time constant of the probe, and the thermal path between the sensor and the tissue. If the probe isn't making good contact, or if it's measuring a surface that's influenced by ambient air or by the device's own heat dissipation, it can read differently than a reference thermometer placed at a different location or with different contact. I'd also consider whether the reference thermometer and the device are measuring at genuinely the same site — a difference in measurement location alone can produce a real, reproducible offset.

I'd also examine the calibration procedure itself. If the device is calibrated in a dry-block or stirred bath at the manufacturer, that verifies the electronics and sensor at the calibration points but doesn't necessarily reproduce the thermal environment of clinical use. A sensor that's accurate in a bath can still read high on a patient if its thermal coupling or self-heating behavior differs. Self-heating is worth checking — if the sensor or its excitation current dissipates enough power, it can bias the reading, and that bias may be small enough to pass a calibration tolerance but visible in a clinical comparison.

Finally, I'd look at whether the complaint correlates with a particular batch, firmware version, or usage pattern, and whether the offset is within the device's specified accuracy when measured correctly. If it is within spec, the issue may be a usability or training problem — clinicians comparing against a reference at a different site or with a different technique. If it's outside spec under realistic conditions, that's a design or verification gap, and I'd feed it back into the risk file and consider a corrective action. Throughout, I'd keep the investigation documented and involve quality and clinical stakeholders, because a temperature reading that clinicians don't trust is a safety and usability issue even if the device is technically within tolerance.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you design a verification test that reproduces clinical thermal conditions rather than a calibration bath?
- If the offset turns out to be within the device's specified accuracy, how would you decide whether a corrective action is still warranted?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a medical device project. During a design review, the quality manager insists that a new risk control measure must be added to address a hazard with an estimated risk level that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?
**Answer:** I'd treat the quality manager's concern as legitimate input rather than an obstacle, because risk management is supposed to be a cross-functional judgment, and "the engineering team considers it negligible" isn't by itself a sufficient basis for rejecting a proposed control. The first thing I'd do is make sure we're actually talking about the same hazard and the same risk estimate — often disagreements like this come from different assumptions about severity, probability, or detectability, and once those are on the table the gap narrows.

I'd go back to the risk analysis and ask: what's the severity of the harm if this hazard occurs, what's the probability, and is the estimate based on data or on engineering judgment? If the severity is high — even if probability is low — the risk may not be acceptable under the company's risk acceptance criteria, and the quality manager may be right. If the severity is genuinely low and the probability is well-supported, I'd want to document that rationale explicitly rather than just asserting it. The ISO 14971 framework expects risk to be evaluated against defined acceptance criteria, so the decision should be traceable either way.

If we still disagree after that, I'd look at whether there's a lower-cost way to address the concern — sometimes a documentation change, a labeling change, or a design change that's already in the pipeline can provide the risk control without the full schedule impact. I'd also consider whether the control can be added in a later revision or as a post-market commitment, if the risk is acceptable in the interim. But I wouldn't unilaterally overrule quality on a safety matter; if the disagreement can't be resolved at the engineering level, it should go to the risk management lead or a design review board with the authority to make the call, and the decision and its rationale should be recorded.

The schedule impact is real and I'd be transparent about it — I'd present the cost, the alternatives, and the risk trade-off so the decision-maker has what they need. But I'd frame it as "here's the trade-off and here's my recommendation," not "we can't do this because of the schedule." In a regulated environment, the schedule is a constraint, not a justification for accepting an unaddressed risk.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the risk acceptance criteria themselves were ambiguous or not documented?
- If the control is added and it turns out to have its own risks, how would you manage that in the risk file?
