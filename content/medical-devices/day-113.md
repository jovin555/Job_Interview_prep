# medical-devices — Day 113

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** Start from the standard's structure rather than from the schematic. IEC 60601-1 defines leakage current measurements by *what current is flowing, from where, to where, and under what condition* — patient leakage current (from the applied part, through the patient, to earth), patient auxiliary current (between applied parts, or between an applied part and earth via the patient, for the part of the circuit not intended to deliver physiological effect), earth leakage current, and touch current. The applied part classification (BF vs. CF) changes the *limits*, not the topology of the measurement.

The practical approach is to build a measurement matrix before touching a probe:

1. **Enumerate the applied parts and their classifications.** A device with one BF part and one CF part is not "a CF device" — each part is assessed against its own classification, and the CF part's tighter limits apply to it specifically.
2. **Enumerate the conditions.** Normal condition, then each relevant single-fault condition (e.g., protective earth open, one means of patient protection shorted, mains on an applied part where applicable). The fault list comes from the standard's clause on single-fault conditions, not from intuition.
3. **Enumerate the measurement points.** For each applied part: patient leakage to earth (with the part connected to the measuring device per the standard's Figure/Annex), patient auxiliary current between parts where the standard requires it, and — for F-type parts — the same measurements with the appropriate MOPP faulted.
4. **Decide what's actually required** by asking, for each cell in the matrix: does this applied part exist, is it F-type, is it intended for direct cardiac contact (which drives CF), and does the standard's measurement definition apply to this configuration? Measurements that don't map to a defined current path in the standard are not "extra safety" — they're noise in the report.

For the measurement path itself: use the standard's specified measuring device (the MD, with its defined input impedance and frequency weighting) rather than a generic DMM, keep the setup's earth reference unambiguous, and verify the setup by measuring a known-good reference configuration first. A large fraction of "failures" at this stage are setup errors — a floating scope, a ground loop through the bench supply, or the wrong MD impedance — so I'd sanity-check the rig before believing any number.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a part intended for transient contact with the patient needs to be classified as an applied part at all? What would you do if the CF part passes under normal condition but fails under a single-fault condition that you believe is unrealistic in use?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a gift — it means there's a resonant or coupling path with a specific frequency response, and the job is to find which one. I'd work it as a structured hunt rather than shotgunning fixes:

1. **Characterize the failure precisely.** What exactly malfunctions — a reset, an ADC reading shift, a display glitch, a communication dropout? Log the device's own telemetry during the sweep if possible. The *nature* of the failure points at the victim circuit: a reset suggests the MCU supply or reset line; an analog shift suggests the sensor front-end or reference; a comms dropout suggests the transceiver or its clock.
2. **Map the band.** Sweep finely around the failure frequency at the test level, and also at reduced level, to find the threshold. Note whether the band shifts with device orientation, cable position, or enclosure configuration — a shift with cable position says the cable is the antenna; a shift with orientation says it's a slot or a trace.
3. **Identify the antenna and the victim.** Near-field probes (H-field and E-field) scanned over the board at the failure frequency will show where the field concentrates. Compare against the victim circuit identified in step 1. Common culprits: a cable harness acting as a monopole, a board-edge trace, a connector, a slot in the enclosure, or a resonance in the ground plane.
4. **Identify the coupling mechanism.** Is it conducted in on a cable, radiated onto a trace, or coupled through a shared impedance? Temporarily adding ferrites, shielding the cable, or grounding a suspect point one at a time tells you which path matters.
5. **Fix at the right layer.** Preferred order: reduce the antenna (cable routing, ferrite/twisted pair, connector filtering), then harden the victim (RC filtering on the affected input, local decoupling, a stiffer reference, a lower-impedance reset pull-up with a capacitor), then improve the return path (stitching, ground plane continuity under the victim), and only then consider shielding. Firmware mitigation — e.g., re-reading a corrupted register, watchdog recovery — is a legitimate *supplement* but should not be the primary control for an immunity failure, because it treats the symptom.

The key discipline is to change one variable at a time and re-test at the exact failure frequency, so you know which change actually moved the threshold rather than which change happened to coincide with a pass.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the failure band moves when you rotate the device 90°, what does that tell you? How would you decide whether a firmware recovery mechanism is acceptable as a risk control for this failure?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The failure mode of a risk management file is that it becomes a retrospective narrative — someone writes it after the design is frozen, reverse-engineering hazards from the schematic. It's auditable in form but useless in function, and it tends to miss exactly the hazards that emerged during design because nobody was recording them at the time.

The structure I'd aim for makes the file a *living index* over work products that already exist, rather than a parallel document:

- **A hazard analysis that's traceable to design inputs.** Each hazard links to the design input(s) it threatens and to the design output(s) that address it. When a design input changes, the affected hazards are visible immediately.
- **Risk control measures as first-class items with verification evidence.** Every control has an owner, an implementation reference (schematic sheet, firmware module, IFU section), and a verification record. A control without verification evidence is a plan, not a control.
- **A traceability matrix that's generated, not hand-maintained.** Design input → design output → verification → hazard/control. If it's maintained by hand in a spreadsheet it will drift; if it's a view over the requirements and test management tooling, it stays current.
- **A review cadence tied to design milestones.** Risk review is an agenda item at each design review, with the hazard log as a standing artifact — new hazards added, residual risks re-evaluated, controls verified. This is what keeps the file honest.
- **Explicit residual risk acceptance with rationale.** Not just "acceptable" but *why* — against the benefit, against the state of the art, against the clinical use context. This is the part auditors and reviewers actually read.
- **A production/post-production feedback loop.** Complaints, field data, and manufacturing nonconformances feed back into the hazard analysis. The file doesn't close at design freeze.

The test of whether the structure works: if a design change lands on a Tuesday, can you answer by end of day which hazards are affected, which controls need re-verification, and whether residual risk changed? If yes, the file is doing its job.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard that's identified during verification, after the design is nominally frozen? What's the minimum viable risk management file for a derivative device built on an existing platform?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means a software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation resulting in death or serious injury, so the verification burden is about demonstrating that the software's safety-related behavior is correct *and* that the development process itself was rigorous enough to give confidence in that correctness. I'd structure it in layers:

1. **Confirm the classification and its scope first.** Class C applies to the software *system* or to specific software items — a device can have Class C safety-related items alongside Class A/B items. The verification plan should reflect that granularity, not blanket-apply Class C rigor to a UI module that can't cause harm.
2. **Requirements-based verification.** Every software requirement — especially safety-related ones — traces to at least one test. For Class C, the standard expects verification at unit, integration, and system level, with the depth of unit verification driven by the architecture. Requirements coverage is necessary but not sufficient; I'd also want *structural* coverage evidence (statement/branch, and MC/DC or equivalent for the highest-integrity items) to show the tests actually exercise the logic.
3. **Architecture-level evidence.** Class C expects the software architecture to support the safety requirements — segregation of safety-critical from non-critical functions, defensive design, error handling, resource management. Verification here means reviewing and testing the *interfaces*: what happens when a non-critical task starves the scheduler, when a shared resource is corrupted, when a safety function's inputs go invalid.
4. **Fault injection and robustness testing.** Deliberately corrupt memory, starve tasks, inject sensor faults, force watchdog resets, and verify the safety function degrades safely. This is where Class C verification earns its keep — it tests the failure behavior, not just the happy path.
5. **Process evidence.** Problem reports and their resolution, code review records, static analysis results, unit test coverage reports, and the software release record. The verification isn't only the test results — it's the evidence that the process that produced the code was controlled.
6. **Independent review where required.** For Class C, an independent verification of the safety-related items adds real confidence and is often expected by reviewers.

The trap to avoid is treating "all requirements have a passing test" as sufficient. For Class C, the question a reviewer will ask is: *how do you know the tests would have caught the failure modes you didn't think of?* Structural coverage, fault injection, and architecture review are the answers to that question.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you justify a lower structural coverage target for a specific module without weakening the overall Class C argument? What would you do if a safety-related requirement had no feasible test method?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** The instinct is to treat this as a schedule argument, but it's really a disagreement about risk estimation, and that's resolvable with evidence rather than negotiation. I'd handle it in three moves:

**First, separate the two questions.** Is the hazard's risk actually negligible, and is the proposed control the right response if it isn't? These get conflated. The engineering team may be right that the risk is low; the quality manager may still be right that the *file* doesn't demonstrate it. Those are different problems with different fixes.

**Second, go back to the risk estimation and make it explicit.** What's the severity, what's the probability of occurrence, and what's the evidence for that probability? If the team says "negligible," what's that based on — a fault tree, historical data, a design feature that makes the hazardous situation unreachable? If the estimation is documented and defensible, the quality manager's concern may be addressed by strengthening the rationale rather than adding hardware. If the estimation is hand-waving, the quality manager is doing their job and the team should take the concern seriously.

**Third, evaluate the control options honestly, including the cheap ones.** The proposed control may be the most expensive option on the table. Alternatives might include: a design change that makes the hazardous situation unreachable (often cheaper than a detection control), a firmware mitigation, a labeling/IFU control, or a verification activity that demonstrates the risk is lower than estimated. ISO 14971's hierarchy — inherently safe design first, then protective measures, then information for safety — often points at a cheaper and more effective control than the one initially proposed.

On the schedule question: I'd be transparent that the impact is real, and bring the trade-off to the review as a decision rather than absorbing it silently. But I'd frame it correctly — if the risk genuinely warrants control, the schedule impact is the cost of a correct device, not an argument against the control. The one outcome I'd resist is deferring the control to "post-launch" without a documented risk acceptance at the right level, because that's how residual risk gets accepted by nobody in particular.

The behavioral core: treat the quality manager as a partner in getting the risk file right, not an obstacle to the schedule. If we disagree on the estimation, the resolution is better evidence, not seniority.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the evidence supported the quality manager's concern but the control genuinely couldn't be implemented before the submission date? How would you document a decision to accept a residual risk that the engineering team considers negligible but the quality function does not?
