# medical-devices — Day 78

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?
**Answer:** The starting point is the standard's measurement topology, not the schematic. IEC 60601-1 defines patient leakage current as the current flowing from the applied part, through the patient, to earth — so the measurement network (the specified RC body model) sits between the applied part and earth, and the device is configured in the worst-case supply condition for each test. For a device with mixed applied part classifications, I'd first build a matrix: each applied part type (BF, CF) crossed with each condition (normal condition, single-fault condition), crossed with the relevant supply polarity and, for CF parts, the additional requirement that the measurement be made with the part referenced to earth and to mains. CF parts are the strictest — much lower limits and they must remain within limits even under single-fault conditions — so the CF path usually dominates the isolation design. I'd decide which measurements are required by reading the applied part definitions and the clause structure carefully: not every combination is a distinct test, and some configurations collapse into the same worst case. The practical engineering question is which applied part, in which fault condition, produces the highest leakage into the measurement network — that's the case that sizes the isolation barrier, the Y-capacitor budget, and the creepage/clearance. I'd also verify that the measurement setup itself doesn't load the circuit or introduce a ground loop that corrupts the reading, and that the test is repeatable across units. The output of this exercise is a test procedure that maps each required measurement to a specific device configuration, so the lab and the design team are testing the same thing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a single-fault condition in one applied part could push leakage in a different applied part over its limit? What design changes would you consider first if a CF applied part marginally exceeded its limit?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?
**Answer:** A narrow-band failure is almost always a resonance or a coupling path that only becomes efficient at that frequency, so I'd treat it as a signal-integrity and coupling problem rather than a generic "EMC problem." First, I'd characterize it: is the malfunction threshold-dependent (does it appear only above a certain field strength), is it reproducible with the device in a fixed orientation, and does it change if I move the cable harness or the enclosure? That tells me whether the coupling is radiated into the enclosure, conducted along a cable, or picked up by a specific trace or connector. Next, I'd look for the resonant structure — a cable whose length is a quarter-wave at that frequency, a slot or seam in the enclosure, a loop area on the PCB, or an unshielded sensor lead acting as an antenna. The fix usually follows the coupling path: if it's a cable, I'd address common-mode current with a ferrite, a common-mode choke, or a shield termination change; if it's the enclosure, I'd address the seam or add a gasket; if it's on the board, I'd look at return-path discontinuities, filter the offending I/O, or add local decoupling. I'd also check whether the failure is actually a firmware robustness issue — a transient that corrupts a register or a communication frame — in which case the fix might be a watchdog, a re-init routine, or a more robust protocol rather than more shielding. The key is to fix the coupling path, not just add a blanket filter, because a blanket fix often shifts the problem to another frequency.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a radiated coupling path from a conducted one during this investigation? If the fix is a ferrite on a cable, how would you verify it doesn't degrade signal integrity or emissions elsewhere?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?
**Answer:** The file should be a living record that grows with the design, not a retrospective compilation. I'd start by establishing the risk management plan early — scope, responsibilities, the risk acceptability criteria, and the review cadence — because the criteria are what every later decision is measured against. Then I'd build the hazard analysis from the intended use and the foreseeable misuse, not from the schematic: what can harm the patient, the operator, or the environment, and under what conditions. Each hazard gets an estimated risk, and each risk control gets traced to a design input, a verification activity, and a residual-risk evaluation. The discipline that keeps it useful is traceability in both directions: from hazard to control to verification evidence, and from any design change back to the hazards it might affect. I'd integrate risk reviews into the design review cadence so the file is updated when decisions are made, not after. I'd also keep the file structured so a reviewer can follow a single hazard end-to-end without hunting across documents — that's the test of whether it's auditable. Finally, I'd treat the residual-risk evaluation and the overall benefit-risk conclusion as explicit, signed-off steps, because those are the parts regulators and auditors scrutinize most.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard that's identified late, after the design is largely frozen? What's the difference between verifying a risk control and validating that it actually reduces risk in use?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?
**Answer:** The classification should follow from the harm analysis, not from how the failure feels or how hard it is to fix. I'd ask: if this failure occurs, can it contribute to an unacceptable risk to the patient or operator, directly or by removing a protective function? If yes, it's safety-related and belongs in the risk management process with a defined risk control and verification. If the failure only degrades usability, causes annoyance, or reduces availability without a credible path to harm, it's a reliability or usability issue — but I'd be careful, because "no harm" often depends on assumptions about how the device is used and what other protections exist. The IEC 62304 software safety classification is a related but distinct question: it's about the software's contribution to hazardous situations, and it drives the rigor of the development process. I'd document the reasoning either way, because the decision itself needs to be auditable. A useful discipline is to route every failure through the same triage: describe the failure, trace it to the hazardous situation it could cause, and let the risk analysis decide. That prevents both over-classification (which wastes effort and can obscure the real risks) and under-classification (which is the dangerous failure mode).

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that's safety-related only in combination with another independent failure? If the team disagrees on the classification, what evidence would you bring to resolve it?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a project where the clinical team has requested a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?
**Answer:** I'd treat it as a decision to be made with data, not a yes/no to be delivered on the spot. First, I'd understand the request precisely: what clinical problem does it solve, how often does that problem occur, and what's the consequence if it isn't addressed? A usability change that prevents a use error with patient harm is very different from one that's a convenience improvement. Second, I'd get a realistic estimate of the cost — hardware revision, firmware impact, re-verification, re-validation, and the regulatory impact — and I'd look for alternatives that capture most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost: a firmware-only change, a labeling or training change, a change that can be deferred to a post-market update, or a design change that fits within the existing hardware. Third, I'd bring the trade-off to the people who own the risk and the schedule — clinical, quality, regulatory, and program management — and frame it as options with consequences, not as a recommendation delivered unilaterally. If the change is genuinely necessary for safety or for the device to meet its intended use, then the schedule impact is the cost of doing it right, and I'd say so. If it's not, I'd document the decision and the rationale, because that record matters if the issue resurfaces later. The worst outcome is a silent "no" that leaves the clinical concern unaddressed and undocumented.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether the change can be handled as a post-market update rather than pre-submission? What would you do if the clinical team and the program manager disagreed on the urgency?
