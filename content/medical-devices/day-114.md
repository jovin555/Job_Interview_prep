# medical-devices — Day 114

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement network definitions, because the measurement path is dictated by the classification, not the other way around. For each applied part I'd determine whether it's BF or CF, whether it's floating or earth-referenced, and whether it's intended for direct cardiac contact — that last point matters because CF parts carry tighter limits and, in some cases, additional single-fault conditions.

The measurement path itself is a defined network: a measuring device (MD) with a specified input impedance placed between the applied part and earth, with the device under test powered at rated voltage and, where applicable, at 110% of rated mains voltage on the supply side. For patient leakage current I'd measure from each applied part, in turn, to earth — with the parts connected together where the standard requires it, and with the polarity of the mains reversed. The key discipline is that the measurement network, the source voltage, and the fault conditions are all specified by the standard, so I'd build the test setup to match the standard's figure rather than improvising a bench setup.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes down to a matrix: applied part classification × normal condition vs. single-fault condition × AC vs. DC component. For a device with both BF and CF parts, I'd expect to measure patient leakage on each part separately, and I'd also check the combination case where the standard requires parts to be connected together — because the worst case is often not a single part in isolation. I'd also confirm whether the CF part's tighter limit forces a different measurement network or a different fault condition than the BF part.

The practical trap is assuming that because the BF part passes, the CF part will too. It won't necessarily, because the CF limit is tighter and the isolation scheme may be shared. So I'd design the measurement plan around the tightest limit first, verify the isolation barrier and Y-capacitor placement support it, and only then confirm the BF measurements. If a shared barrier is used, I'd also check that a single fault on the BF side can't degrade the CF side's leakage path.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you lay out the PCB so that the CF applied part's leakage path stays within its tighter limit even with a shared isolation barrier? What would you do if the CF part passed in normal condition but failed under a single-fault condition?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device's software needs to be classified as Class A, B, or C under IEC 62304, and what would you do if the hardware and software leads disagreed on the classification?

**Answer:** The classification follows from the risk analysis, not from opinion. IEC 62304 ties the class to the severity of harm that a software failure could contribute to: Class A if no injury or damage to health is possible, Class B if non-serious injury is possible, and Class C if death or serious injury is possible. So the first step is to trace each software item back to the hazards it could contribute to in the ISO 14971 risk file, and ask what the worst credible outcome is if that software fails or behaves unexpectedly.

The subtlety is that "could contribute to" is broader than "directly controls." A monitoring function that drives an alarm can be Class C if a missed alarm could lead to death, even though the software doesn't deliver therapy. Conversely, a logging or UI function that can't affect a safety-related function might be Class A or B even in a device whose overall software is Class C. So I'd classify at the software-item level, not just the system level, and document the rationale for each.

If the hardware and software leads disagreed, I'd bring it back to the risk file rather than debating it abstractly. I'd ask each of them to point to the specific hazard, the sequence of events, and the severity that justifies their position. Often the disagreement dissolves once the hazard analysis is explicit — one of them is implicitly assuming a failure mode the other hasn't considered, or one is classifying at the system level while the other is classifying at the item level. If it still doesn't resolve, I'd escalate to the quality/regulatory function, because the classification drives the software development process rigor and ultimately the submission, and it needs a documented, defensible decision rather than a compromise.

I'd also record the decision and its rationale in the software development plan, because auditors will ask why a given item was classified the way it was, and "the team agreed" is not an answer.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a software item that is Class B on its own but whose failure could disable a Class C function? What documentation would you expect to see supporting a Class C classification?

## Q3: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it usually means something in the device is resonant or is being demodulated at that frequency, rather than a broad susceptibility problem. I'd start by characterizing the failure precisely: what is the exact frequency and bandwidth, what is the field strength threshold at which it starts, what is the modulation (CW vs. AM), and what is the actual malfunction — a reset, a display glitch, a sensor reading shift, a communication dropout? The nature of the malfunction tells you which subsystem is being disturbed.

Then I'd work the coupling path. At a specific frequency, the likely mechanisms are: a cable or trace acting as an antenna at a resonant length, a ferrite or filter whose impedance dips at that frequency, a connector or seam resonating, or a semiconductor junction demodulating the RF into baseband. I'd use a near-field probe to localize where the energy is entering — is it the patient cable, the power cable, the enclosure seam, or a specific board region? That localization step is what separates a guess from a fix.

Once I know the coupling path, the fix follows the path: if it's a cable, I'd look at common-mode chokes, ferrites selected for that frequency, cable shielding and termination, and the quality of the chassis ground bond at the connector. If it's a board-level resonance, I'd look at filter component self-resonance, ground plane stitching, and decoupling placement. If it's demodulation in an analog front-end, I'd look at the input filter's common-mode rejection at that frequency and whether the amplifier is being driven into nonlinearity.

I'd also check whether the frequency corresponds to something in the device itself — a switching regulator harmonic, a clock harmonic, or a crystal — because a self-generated frequency mixing with the applied field can produce a beat that looks like a narrow-band susceptibility. And I'd verify the fix at the exact frequency and at the band edges, not just at the center, because a fix that shifts the resonance rather than eliminating it can move the problem rather than solve it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cable resonance from a board-level resonance in the near-field scan? If the fix is a ferrite on the patient cable, how would you verify it doesn't compromise the leakage current margin?

## Q4: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?

**Answer:** I'd treat traceability as a living structure that's built alongside the design, not a matrix assembled at the end. The backbone is a unique identifier scheme: every design input gets an ID, every design output that satisfies it references that ID, every verification test references the output it verifies, and every validation activity references the user needs it addresses. If those IDs are assigned when the item is created and carried through, the traceability is a byproduct of doing the work rather than a separate exercise.

The practical mechanism is a requirements management tool or a structured spreadsheet with enforced links, plus a rule that no design output is accepted into a review without its upstream input and downstream verification identified. Design reviews become the checkpoint where gaps are caught — if an output has no input, it's either an undocumented requirement or scope creep; if an input has no output, it's an unmet requirement; if an output has no verification, it's untested.

The hard part is change. When a design input changes, I'd use an impact analysis that walks the links forward: which outputs are affected, which verification tests need re-running, which validation evidence is invalidated, and whether the risk file needs updating. That impact analysis is recorded as part of the change control record, so the DHF shows not just the current state but the reasoning behind each change. I'd also version the traceability matrix alongside the design, so a reviewer can see what the traceability looked like at each design review.

One discipline that pays off: keep the traceability granular enough to be useful but not so granular that it becomes unmaintainable. Tracing at the level of a requirement or a test, not at the level of every line of code, keeps it auditable without turning it into a full-time job.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design change that invalidates a validation activity that was expensive to run? What would you do if you inherited a project where the traceability was incomplete?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating two questions that often get conflated: is the hazard's risk actually acceptable, and is the proposed control the right response if it isn't? The engineering team's "negligible" is a risk-estimation judgment, and the quality manager's insistence may be based on a different estimate, a different severity assumption, or a concern about how the risk will be perceived in review. I'd ask the quality manager to walk through their hazard analysis — what severity and probability they're assuming, and what evidence supports it — and I'd walk through ours. Often the disagreement is about one input to the estimate, and resolving that input resolves the disagreement.

If the risk estimate genuinely supports "negligible" and the control wouldn't meaningfully reduce risk, I'd say so with the analysis in hand, and propose documenting the rationale for accepting the risk rather than adding a control that doesn't change the outcome. A control that doesn't reduce risk but adds schedule is not good risk management — it's documentation theater, and it can actually obscure the real risk picture.

If the quality manager's concern is legitimate — for example, they're worried about a failure mode the team underweighted, or about how the residual risk will be justified to a reviewer — then the right response is to address the concern, not to dismiss it because the team called it negligible. I'd ask what evidence would satisfy them, and whether a cheaper control (a warning in the IFU, a design change that's already in the pipeline, a test that demonstrates the failure mode can't occur) would address it.

The escalation path matters too. If we can't converge, I'd take it to the risk management review with both analyses documented, because the decision needs to be made on the record by the accountable function, not settled by whoever is more persistent in the meeting. And I'd be careful not to frame it as engineering vs. quality — the goal is a defensible risk file, and both functions want that.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document a decision to accept a risk without adding a control? What would you do if the quality manager's concern turned out to be about a failure mode the team hadn't considered?
