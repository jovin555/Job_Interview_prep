# medical-devices — Day 117

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** Start from the standard's measurement topology rather than from the schematic. IEC 60601-1 defines leakage current in terms of a measurement circuit (the MD circuit) inserted between the applied part and earth, with the device configured in each of its relevant operating modes and supply conditions. The first decision is therefore a classification decision: list every applied part, assign its type (B, BF, CF), and note which parts are simultaneously accessible to the patient. That list, not the bill of materials, determines the measurement matrix.

For the measurement path itself, the practical requirements are: a low-impedance, well-defined measurement network that matches the standard's MD circuit; isolation between the measurement instrument and earth so that the measurement does not itself create or break a ground path; and the ability to test each applied part individually and in combination. A common mistake is to measure with the device floating in a way that accidentally references the applied part to earth through the instrument's own ground — that changes the circuit under test and produces numbers that are neither passing nor failing, just wrong. Use an isolation transformer or a battery-powered instrument, verify the setup with a known resistor network before touching the device, and record the exact configuration for each measurement.

Which measurements are required follows from the type assignments and the standard's tables: patient leakage current for each applied part type under normal condition and single-fault condition, with the appropriate limit for that type (CF limits are tighter than BF, which are tighter than B). Where a device has both BF and CF parts, the CF part generally dominates the design constraints, but you still have to demonstrate the BF part meets its own limit — you cannot infer one from the other. Also check whether the parts are intended to be applied simultaneously; if so, the combination case matters, because return currents can sum through the patient.

The design implication is that the measurement plan should be written before the front-end is finalized. If you know you must demonstrate a CF limit, that drives the isolation barrier, the creepage/clearance, the Y-capacitor budget, and the choice of isolated supply — all of which are expensive to change later.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify your measurement setup is valid before trusting a marginal result? What changes in the measurement matrix if one applied part is intended for direct cardiac connection?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it usually means something in the device is resonant or is being demodulated at that frequency, not that the whole design has inadequate immunity. I would treat it as a signal-chain investigation rather than an EMC "add more shielding" problem.

First, characterize the failure precisely: what is the actual malfunction (display artifact, sensor offset, communication dropout, reset), at what field strength does it start, and how narrow is the band? Sweep finely around the frequency to find the center and the bandwidth. Then vary the setup systematically: change cable routing and length, change the device orientation relative to the antenna, and test with the enclosure open versus closed. If the band moves when a cable moves, the cable is the antenna. If it stays fixed, it is likely a resonance in the PCB, the enclosure, or a specific component.

Second, look for the demodulation path. A common mechanism is that an RF signal is rectified by a nonlinear junction — a semiconductor, an ESD diode, an op-amp input — and the resulting low-frequency envelope appears as a valid-looking signal in the analog front-end or on a control line. That is why the failure often looks like a plausible sensor reading rather than obvious noise. Check whether the affected node has adequate filtering and whether the filter is placed at the point where the RF enters, not downstream of a long trace.

Third, address it at the source of the coupling: improve the return path, add or resize ferrites and feedthrough capacitors at cable entries, tighten the loop area of the offending net, and check that the ground plane is continuous under the affected circuit. Only after those would I consider enclosure-level measures, because they tend to be the most expensive and least diagnostic.

Finally, re-test at the exact frequency and at the band edges, and confirm the fix does not degrade emissions or signal integrity elsewhere.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cable-coupled resonance from an enclosure resonance experimentally? What would you do if the fix worked at the test frequency but the device still failed at a nearby harmonic?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The classification should follow from the hazard analysis, not from how alarming the failure looks. The question I ask is: what is the worst plausible harm to the patient or operator if this failure occurs, given the device's intended use and the state the device is in when it happens? If the answer is "no harm, or harm that is already mitigated by an independent control," it is a reliability or usability issue. If the failure can contribute to a hazardous situation that reaches the patient without an independent barrier, it belongs in the risk management process.

Practically, I would trace the failure to the hazardous situations in the risk file. If it maps to an existing hazardous situation, the question becomes whether the existing risk controls still hold under this failure mode — if they do, the failure is covered; if they don't, it is a new safety-related failure and needs its own control and verification. If it maps to nothing in the file, that is itself a finding: either the hazard analysis missed a credible failure mode, or the failure genuinely has no path to harm, and I would document which.

I would also be careful about the "it's just usability" shortcut. A confusing display that leads a clinician to act on stale data is a usability issue on the surface and a safety issue underneath. The distinguishing test is whether the failure can cause a user to take a wrong action that the design does not otherwise prevent.

The output of this decision should be recorded — a short rationale per failure, linked to the hazard analysis — so that the classification is auditable and consistent across the team, rather than being re-argued case by case.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that is safety-related only in combination with a second independent failure? Where does this classification feed into the software safety class under IEC 62304?

## Q4: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?

**Answer:** Traceability works when it is a byproduct of how the team works, not a document produced afterward. I would set up the structure at the start: a unique identifier scheme for design inputs, a mapping from each input to the output(s) that satisfy it, and from each output to the verification evidence, with validation traced separately to user needs. The key discipline is that every artifact created during the project gets an ID and a link at the moment it is created — a requirement, a schematic block, a test procedure, a test report.

The hard part is change. When a design input changes, the traceability should make the downstream impact visible: which outputs are affected, which verification tests must be re-run, and whether validation is affected. I would handle this with a change-control step that requires the author to walk the links and record the impact, rather than relying on memory. A traceability matrix that is regenerated from a structured source (a requirements tool, or at minimum a controlled spreadsheet with consistent IDs) is far more maintainable than one maintained by hand in a document.

I would also build in periodic reviews — at each design review milestone, sample the matrix and confirm the links are real, not just present. A link that says "requirement 4.2 verified by test report TR-011" is only meaningful if TR-011 actually tests 4.2.

For a derivative product, the same structure applies, but the traceability should explicitly reference the parent device's evidence where it is reused, with a justification for why the reuse is valid — that keeps the DHF defensible without duplicating everything.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a verification test that covers several design inputs at once — one link or many? What would you do if you found, late in the project, that a design output had no corresponding design input?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I would treat the disagreement as a question about the risk file, not about whose judgment is better. The quality manager is usually raising a legitimate concern — either the hazard analysis under-estimated the severity or probability, or the existing risk controls are not as independent as the team believes. So the first step is to go back to the risk estimate together and ask what evidence supports "negligible": is it based on a documented rationale, or on the team's intuition?

If the estimate holds up under scrutiny, I would present that rationale explicitly — severity, probability, and the existing controls that reduce the risk — and ask the quality manager whether that changes their view. If it does not, the question becomes whether the residual risk is acceptable under the company's risk acceptability criteria. That is a management decision with a documented rationale, not an engineering one, and it should be escalated rather than argued to a standstill.

If the estimate does not hold up — for example, the team assumed a control that is not actually independent, or the hazard could combine with another failure — then the quality manager is right and the control should be added, and I would say so plainly.

On schedule: I would quantify the actual impact rather than treating it as a binary. Often a control can be implemented in a way that meets the intent without the full cost the team first assumed. I would bring options to the discussion — a design change, a labeling or IFU control, a verification activity — with their costs, and let the decision be made with the trade-off visible. What I would avoid is either dismissing the concern to protect the schedule or accepting a costly control without understanding what risk it actually retires.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document a decision to accept a risk that the quality function disagreed with? What would you do if the quality manager's concern turned out to be about a hazard not in the risk file at all?
