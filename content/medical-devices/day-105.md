# medical-devices — Day 105

## Q1: How would you approach designing the enclosure and internal layout of a medical device that must survive a specified drop onto a hard floor while in use, without compromising the electrical safety clearances inside?
**Answer:** I'd treat mechanical shock and electrical safety as one coupled problem rather than two separate ones. The starting point is the drop requirement itself: define the drop height, orientation, and surface from the applicable standard and the intended use environment, then translate that into an acceleration/energy budget the enclosure has to absorb. From there the design work splits into three layers. First, the outer shell: material choice (a ductile polymer rather than a brittle one), wall thickness, corner radii, and ribbing to distribute impact loads away from stress concentrators. Second, the internal mounting: the PCB and any heavy components — batteries, transformers, connectors — need to be restrained so they don't shift under shock, because a battery that moves a millimeter can crush a creepage gap or a wire that was fine in the static layout. Third, the safety clearances: creepage and clearance distances are defined in the assembled, worst-case condition, so I'd verify that the layout still holds those distances after the maximum expected deflection and after any internal part shifts. That means doing the clearance check against the deflected geometry, not the nominal CAD. I'd also look at the failure mode: if the enclosure does crack, does it crack in a way that exposes live parts or defeats an isolation barrier? The design should fail safe — a cracked cosmetic surface is acceptable, a cracked barrier is not. Verification is a combination of drop testing on real units and inspection of the clearances post-drop, plus a review of any internal movement.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a drop-induced crack in the enclosure is a cosmetic issue or a safety issue that requires a design change? What role would a shock-absorbing internal mount play, and what trade-offs does it introduce for thermal performance?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device's software needs Class B or Class C under IEC 62304 when the device monitors a physiological parameter and drives an alarm, but does not deliver therapy?
**Answer:** The classification follows from the risk analysis, not from the device category or from intuition. The question IEC 62304 asks is: could a software failure contribute to a hazardous situation, and if so, what is the severity of the resulting harm? So I'd start from the hazard analysis and work backwards. For a monitoring-and-alarm device, the key question is what happens if the software fails in a way that suppresses, delays, or mis-prioritizes an alarm. If the worst credible outcome of that failure is that a clinician is not alerted to a life-threatening condition and the patient comes to harm, then the software is contributing to a hazardous situation with serious severity, which points toward Class C. If the risk analysis shows that an independent hardware path, a redundant monitor, or a clinical workflow always catches the failure before harm can occur, the software may only be Class B. The classification is per software item or per function, not necessarily per device — a device can have Class C alarm logic and Class B logging. The important discipline is to document the reasoning: which failure modes were considered, what the severity was, and why the chosen class follows. If the hardware and software leads disagree, I'd bring it back to the hazard analysis and ask each of them to name the specific failure mode and the specific harm path they're relying on. Often the disagreement dissolves once the actual failure sequence is written down, and if it doesn't, the conservative classification is the safer default until the analysis proves otherwise.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the device has a hardware watchdog that resets the processor on a software hang, does that change the classification? How would you handle a function that is Class B in isolation but whose output feeds a Class C alarm decision?

## Q3: How would you approach verifying that a device's patient-contacting cable assembly maintains its electrical safety performance after repeated flexing and cleaning over the product's service life?
**Answer:** The cable is one of the easiest places for a medical device to degrade silently, because the failure is mechanical before it's electrical. I'd approach it as a life-cycle verification problem with three strands. First, define the use-cycle profile: how many flex cycles, at what bend radius, how many cleaning cycles with which agents, and whether the cable is ever pulled or pinched. That profile comes from the intended use and the clinical environment, and it should be written down as a requirement, not assumed. Second, design for it: strain relief at both ends, a jacket material compatible with the cleaning agents, conductor stranding chosen for flex life rather than cost, and — critically for a patient-contacting cable — an insulation and jacket system that maintains the required dielectric withstand and leakage performance even after the mechanical abuse. Third, verify it: a flex-and-clean cycling rig that runs the defined profile, with periodic electrical checks (insulation resistance, dielectric withstand, and where relevant patient leakage current) at intervals through the cycling, not just at the end. Testing only the end state hides the point at which performance starts to degrade. I'd also include a post-cycling visual and cross-section inspection to catch internal conductor damage that hasn't yet become an electrical failure. The output is a verification report that ties the test profile back to the use-cycle requirement, so the evidence is traceable rather than a one-off pass.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the number of flex cycles to test if the clinical use profile is uncertain? What would you do if the cable passes dielectric testing after cycling but shows a rising trend in leakage current?

## Q4: How would you approach handling a situation where, late in a project, a supplier notifies you that a critical component is being discontinued and the recommended replacement has different package dimensions and a different reference accuracy?
**Answer:** The first move is to quantify the impact before reacting, because "discontinued" and "must redesign" are not the same thing. I'd check whether the supplier has a last-time-buy window and whether the current inventory plus a buffer covers the remaining production and spares needs — sometimes the right answer is a controlled last-time buy that buys the project time to qualify a replacement properly. If a replacement is genuinely needed, I'd treat it as a design change with a full impact assessment rather than a drop-in substitution. The package difference affects the PCB layout, which means a board revision, which means re-verification of anything the layout touches — signal integrity, thermal, and, for a medical device, the safety clearances around the part. The reference accuracy difference is the more subtle one: it may change the device's measurement error budget, so I'd re-run the error analysis and check whether the specified accuracy still holds, and whether any calibration or firmware compensation needs to change. Then I'd plan the verification: bench characterization of the replacement in the actual circuit, a focused re-test of the affected performance parameters, and a decision on whether the change is significant enough to require a regulatory notification or a design review. Throughout, the change goes through the same design control process as any other — documented, reviewed, and traceable — because a component substitution that skips that process is exactly how a device ends up with an unverified configuration in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a component change requires a regulatory submission versus a documented internal change? What would you do if the replacement part's accuracy is worse and the error budget no longer closes?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and a clinical advisor tells you that a device feature the team considers fully specified will not actually be usable by nurses in the intended workflow. The fix would touch firmware, the user interface, and the instructions for use. How would you handle this?
**Answer:** I'd take the clinical advisor's input seriously as a usability finding rather than a late change request, because a feature that isn't usable in the real workflow is a design defect even if it meets its written specification. The first step is to understand the specific gap: what does the nurse actually do, where does the current design force a workaround, and is this a usability issue, a training issue, or a genuine mismatch between the design and the clinical workflow? I'd want to see it described concretely, ideally with the advisor walking through the workflow, before committing to a fix. Then I'd scope the change honestly: what firmware work, what UI work, what IFU changes, and what verification and usability validation would need to be redone. That scope feeds a decision that involves the whole project — regulatory, quality, and program management — not just engineering. I'd present the options rather than a single demand: fix now and absorb the schedule impact, fix in a follow-on release with a documented interim mitigation, or address it through training and IFU if the risk analysis supports that. The deciding factor is patient safety and usability risk: if the gap could lead to misuse or a hazardous situation, it needs to be fixed before release regardless of schedule. If it's a workflow efficiency issue with no safety consequence, a phased approach may be defensible. Either way, the decision and its rationale get documented, and the usability finding goes into the risk file so it's traceable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a usability finding that must block release from one that can be handled post-market? What would you do if the clinical advisor and the internal usability team disagreed on the severity of the gap?
