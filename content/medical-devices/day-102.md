# medical-devices — Day 102

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement matrix, because the required measurements are driven by classification, not by convenience. The matrix has two axes: the condition (normal condition versus each relevant single-fault condition) and the source of the leakage path — earth leakage, enclosure leakage, and patient leakage, with patient leakage further split by whether the measurement is from the applied part to earth, or from one applied part to another. For a mixed BF/CF device, the CF part carries the tightest limits, so I'd treat it as the binding constraint and design the measurement path around it rather than measuring everything to the loosest limit and hoping.

Practically, the measurement path needs to be built so it doesn't perturb what it's measuring. That means a low-impedance, well-defined measurement network that matches the standard's specified impedance, careful attention to the reference point (mains earth for the equipment under test), and keeping the measurement leads short and away from switching noise so the reading reflects the device and not the setup. I'd also verify the measurement network itself — a known resistor substitution or a calibration check — before trusting any number, because a measurement path that's wrong in the safe direction hides a real failure.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes down to: which applied parts exist, what each is classified as, which single-fault conditions are applicable to the device's construction, and whether any applied parts are intended to be connected to each other or to the same patient circuit. Parts that share a patient circuit may need part-to-part measurements that isolated parts don't. I'd document the rationale for each measurement performed and each one deliberately not performed, because a reviewer will ask why a measurement is absent, and "we didn't think it applied" is not an answer.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where an applied part's classification is ambiguous because it can optionally connect to a second device? What would you do if a single-fault condition required by the standard can't be physically induced on your design?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** I'd anchor the decision in the risk management process rather than in intuition, because "safety-related" is a conclusion that should fall out of hazard analysis, not a label applied afterward. The first step is to trace the failure to a hazardous situation: what is the sequence from the software failure, through the device's state and the user's interaction, to a possible harm? If that chain can't be constructed — if the worst credible outcome is an inconvenience, a delayed reading, or a nuisance alarm with no path to patient harm — then it's a reliability or usability issue and belongs in the normal defect process. If the chain can be constructed, even with low probability, it's safety-related and needs to go through risk estimation and control.

The subtle part is that "the software can't cause harm directly" is often wrong. Software rarely harms anyone; it enables a hardware output, suppresses an alarm, displays a misleading value, or fails to detect a condition. So I'd ask specifically: does this failure affect a safety function, an alarm, a displayed value used for a clinical decision, or a control output? Any yes pulls it into the safety process. I'd also check whether an existing risk control was credited with mitigating this failure — if the risk file says a watchdog or a plausibility check covers this class of failure, then a failure that defeats that control is safety-related by definition, because the residual risk argument depends on it.

I'd also be careful about the asymmetry: misclassifying a safety failure as non-safety is far worse than the reverse. When it's genuinely borderline, I'd err toward treating it as safety-related and let the risk analysis downgrade it with documented rationale, rather than the other way around. And I'd make sure the classification decision and its reasoning are recorded, so it's auditable and consistent across similar failures later.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would this change if the failure only occurs in a diagnostic mode not used during patient monitoring? What role should the software safety classification under IEC 62304 play in this decision?

## Q3: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware correctly handles a situation where the device's non-volatile memory becomes corrupted during normal operation, given that the device must continue monitoring and displaying physiological data in real time?

**Answer:** The key framing is that corruption during normal operation is a different problem from corruption during a firmware update — here the device is mid-monitoring, so the requirement isn't "recover the data," it's "keep monitoring safely and don't present corrupted data as valid." I'd design the verification around three questions: does the device detect the corruption, does it fail safe in a way that's visible to the user, and does the real-time monitoring path keep running while the storage subsystem deals with the problem?

Detection first. I'd verify the integrity mechanisms actually catch the corruption modes I care about — not just a checksum over a block, but whether the mechanism covers the metadata, the record boundaries, and the case where a write was interrupted mid-record. A checksum that passes on a half-written record is worse than no checksum, because it certifies bad data. I'd inject corruption at the bit, byte, block, and record-structure levels, and also simulate power loss during a write, since that's the most realistic corruption source in the field.

Then the isolation behavior. The monitoring and display path should not be blocked on storage. I'd verify that a storage fault — whether detected corruption, a busy device, or a failed write — degrades gracefully: monitoring continues, the display keeps updating, and the user gets an indication that logging or storage is impaired rather than silently losing data. The test should confirm the device doesn't reset, doesn't hang, and doesn't present stale data as live.

Finally, recovery and data integrity on the way back. I'd verify that the device can mark the affected region bad, continue logging to a known-good region, and that any data flagged as suspect is never later presented as clinically valid. The pass criteria should be written as observable behaviors — monitoring continuity, correct fault indication, no corrupted record ever surfacing as valid — rather than "the device recovers," which is too vague to test.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you test the case where corruption is intermittent rather than a single event? What's the risk of a repair-on-read strategy in a device that logs data used for clinical decisions?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes calibration on return is the most informative clue, not a reason to close the complaint. It tells me the discrepancy is probably not a static offset in the electronics — it's something about the field condition, the measurement setup, or the interaction between the sensor and the patient that doesn't reproduce on the bench. So I'd resist the temptation to attribute it to user technique and instead build a hypothesis list that explains both observations at once.

The candidates I'd work through: thermal path differences — the sensor's contact with the patient, insulation, or a probe cover changing the time constant and the equilibrium reading; self-heating in the sensor or its excitation circuit that only matters in continuous use; a reference thermometer measuring at a different site or depth, so the two are legitimately reading different temperatures; drift that's time- or temperature-history-dependent and resets or is masked by the bench calibration procedure; and finally a genuine but small offset that the calibration tolerance accepts but that's clinically meaningful at the margin. I'd also check whether the complaint clusters by device serial number, by clinical site, or by user — clustering points at manufacturing lot or technique, while scattering points at design or specification.

The investigation would combine returned-device analysis with field data. On the returned units I'd do a calibration check that mirrors the field use case — same probe configuration, same duration, same ambient — not just a quick single-point check. I'd pull the logged data if the device records it, to see whether the discrepancy is present in the device's own records or only in the comparison. And I'd get the clinical staff's actual measurement method documented, because "reference thermometer" can mean several different things.

The corrective action depends on which hypothesis survives. If it's a specification or use-case gap, the fix may be in the instructions, the probe design, or the calibration procedure rather than the electronics. If it's a real offset within tolerance but clinically significant, that's a specification question that goes back through risk assessment, not just a tweak.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine device error from a legitimate site-of-measurement difference? What would you do if the discrepancy only appears in a subset of patients?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a disagreement about the risk analysis, not a tug-of-war over schedule, and try to move the conversation onto the evidence rather than the conclusion. The quality manager's position deserves a real hearing — "the team considers it negligible" is an assertion, and if the risk file doesn't show the reasoning behind that estimate, the quality manager is right to push. So my first move is to ask what specifically is driving the concern: is it the severity estimate, the probability estimate, the detectability, or a concern that the hazard isn't adequately captured at all? Those lead to different resolutions.

If the disagreement is about probability or severity, I'd go back to the risk analysis and check whether the estimate is defensible and documented — what data, what similar-device history, what fault-tree reasoning. If the analysis holds up, I'd walk the quality manager through it and see whether the concern is really about residual risk acceptance rather than the control itself. Sometimes the resolution is not adding the control but strengthening the justification, or adding a monitoring or labeling measure instead of a hardware change. If the analysis doesn't hold up — if we can't actually defend "negligible" — then the quality manager has found a real gap and the schedule impact is the cost of fixing it.

If we still disagree after that, I'd escalate on the basis of the risk file rather than on opinion: bring it to the design review board or the responsible risk management authority with both positions documented, and let the decision be made against the acceptance criteria. I'd also be clear about the schedule consequence in that forum so the trade-off is visible to the people who own it, rather than absorbed silently by engineering. What I wouldn't do is let the disagreement sit unresolved or quietly drop the control, because an unresolved risk question in a design review tends to resurface at a much worse time — during regulatory review or after a field event.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's concern turns out to be valid but the control can't be implemented before the submission? How do you keep the relationship constructive when you've escalated over their head?

**Possible follow-ups (Q5 continued):** How would you document a decision to accept residual risk without the additional control, so it's defensible later?
