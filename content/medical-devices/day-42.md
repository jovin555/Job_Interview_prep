## Q1: How would you approach designing a test strategy for verifying that a medical device's firmware correctly handles a situation where a sensor provides valid but out-of-range physiological readings (e.g., a heart rate of 240 bpm or a temperature of 42°C)?

**Answer:** I'd start by distinguishing between three cases: readings that are physiologically impossible (sensor fault), readings that are possible but dangerous (true clinical event), and readings that are possible but unlikely (edge cases). The firmware needs to handle each differently. For the test strategy, I'd first review the requirements and risk documentation to confirm what the device is supposed to do in each scenario — for example, whether it should alarm immediately, continue monitoring with a "verify" indicator, or trigger a sensor self-check. Then I'd build a traceability matrix linking each requirement to specific test cases. The test approach would include: injecting simulated sensor values at the firmware boundary (using a test harness or debug interface) rather than trying to physically create the condition; testing values just below, at, and just above the alarm threshold to verify hysteresis; testing the transition from out-of-range back to in-range to confirm the alarm clears correctly; and testing sustained out-of-range conditions to verify the alarm persists and is not treated as a transient. I'd also test the interaction with other parameters — for example, if heart rate reads 240 bpm, does the device still correctly display and alarm on blood pressure? Finally, I'd include a test for how the device behaves when the out-of-range reading is accompanied by a sensor quality flag (e.g., low signal-to-noise), since that combination might warrant a different response than a clean but extreme reading.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether an out-of-range reading should trigger an alarm or be treated as a sensor fault? What role would the risk management file play in defining these thresholds?

## Q2: How would you approach developing a design verification test plan for a medical device that must measure both temperature and pressure, where the two sensors share a common ADC and multiplexer, and the device must maintain specified accuracy for both parameters simultaneously?

**Answer:** The key concern here is cross-channel interference and the timing of multiplexer switching. I'd structure the test plan around three layers. First, individual channel accuracy: verify each sensor's accuracy independently, with the other channel disconnected or held at a known fixed value, to establish a baseline. Second, simultaneous accuracy: verify both channels meet their accuracy specifications when both are active and measuring realistic values — this catches issues like settling time, charge injection from the multiplexer, or crosstalk through the shared ADC input. Third, worst-case scenarios: test with one channel at its extreme range while the other is at its most sensitive range, since that maximizes the potential for interference. For the test methodology, I'd use calibrated reference sources (e.g., a precision temperature bath and a pressure calibrator) and record readings over a sufficient duration to capture any drift or periodic interference. I'd also vary the multiplexer switching rate if it's configurable, since a slower rate might improve settling but reduce update rate. An important detail is to test at multiple points across each sensor's range, not just at the calibration points, because linearity errors can interact with crosstalk. Finally, I'd include a test where one sensor is rapidly changing while the other is held steady, to verify that the firmware's filtering doesn't introduce lag or artifacts that affect accuracy.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether any observed cross-channel error is acceptable? What if the error only appears at certain multiplexer switching rates?

## Q3: During a design review for a medical device that uses a wireless link to transmit physiological data to a display unit, the firmware engineer proposes using a proprietary protocol with a simple checksum, while the clinical team requires that data loss be detectable and that the display unit indicate when data is stale. How would you approach this trade-off?

**Answer:** I'd frame this as a risk-based decision rather than a technical preference. The clinical requirement — detecting data loss and indicating staleness — is a patient-safety requirement, so it's not optional. The question is how to meet it with the simplest reliable design. A proprietary protocol with a simple checksum can work if it includes the necessary features: a sequence number or timestamp on each packet, a timeout mechanism on the display side, and a clear "data stale" indicator. The checksum alone is insufficient because it detects corruption but not loss. So I'd ask the firmware engineer to specify how the protocol handles: packet loss detection (sequence numbers), reconnection after link dropout, and the display's behavior when no valid packet arrives within a defined interval. If the proprietary protocol can provide these, it may be acceptable — but I'd also ask whether a standard protocol (e.g., Bluetooth with its built-in link supervision timeout) would reduce development risk and simplify verification. The trade-off is between protocol simplicity and verification effort: a proprietary protocol requires more thorough testing to prove reliability, while a standard protocol has known behavior and existing test tools. I'd also consider the clinical environment — if the link is used in a hospital with potential interference from other wireless devices, the protocol needs to handle retries or at least detect persistent loss and alert the user. My recommendation would be to require the protocol to meet a defined set of performance criteria (maximum detection time for data loss, behavior on reconnection, alarm on stale data) and then let the team choose the simplest implementation that meets those criteria, with a bias toward standard protocols unless there's a compelling reason otherwise.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific performance criteria would you define for "data loss detection" and "stale data indication"? How would you verify that the display unit correctly indicates staleness after a link dropout?

## Q4: How would you approach developing a design verification test plan for a medical device that must operate correctly during and after exposure to a defibrillation pulse, given that the device is intended for use in a hospital environment where defibrillation may occur?

**Answer:** This is a critical safety scenario, so I'd start by reviewing the applicable standard — IEC 60601-2-xx for the specific device type, which typically defines the defibrillation test requirements, including the test waveform, the number of pulses, and the required device behavior during and after the pulse. The test plan needs to cover three phases: during the pulse, immediately after, and over a recovery period. During the pulse, the device must not present a hazard to the patient or operator — for example, no arcing or dielectric breakdown on patient-connected parts. Immediately after, the device must either resume normal operation or enter a defined safe state (e.g., a reset with a clear indicator). Over the recovery period, the device must return to full functionality, including accurate sensor readings and alarm capability. For the test setup, I'd use a defibrillation pulse generator and a test fixture that simulates the patient load, with the device's patient-connected parts properly terminated. I'd also test with the device in different operating states — powered on and monitoring, in standby, and possibly during a firmware update if that's a realistic scenario. An important consideration is that the defibrillation pulse can induce large voltages on patient cables, so the test should verify that the device's protection circuitry (e.g., spark gaps, series resistors, or clamping diodes) limits the energy reaching sensitive electronics. I'd also include a test where the defibrillation pulse is applied while the device is connected to a patient simulator that's also measuring other parameters, to verify that the device doesn't corrupt its own measurements or those of other connected equipment. Finally, I'd document the pass/fail criteria clearly — for example, the device must resume monitoring within a specified time, must not require user intervention to recover, and must not generate false alarms that could distract clinical staff during a resuscitation event.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify that the device's protection circuitry doesn't degrade over multiple defibrillation exposures? What if the device is connected to a patient during defibrillation — how would you test that scenario safely?

## Q5: You're leading a project where a field complaint reports that a medical device's patient-contacting silicone sensor pad is causing skin irritation in several patients. How would you approach the investigation and corrective action process?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a structured investigation following a formal root-cause analysis process, since patient safety is involved and regulatory reporting may be required. First, I'd ensure the complaints are properly documented and that the severity is assessed — if the irritation is serious (e.g., blistering, chemical burns), I'd escalate immediately and consider whether a field safety corrective action is needed while the investigation is ongoing. Then I'd gather data from multiple angles: the specific lot numbers of the affected pads, the duration of contact, the patient population (e.g., neonates vs. adults), and any common factors among the affected patients. I'd also request the affected pads for analysis — testing for residual chemicals from manufacturing (e.g., mold-release agents, unreacted silicone components), verifying the material meets the ISO 10993 biocompatibility specifications, and checking whether the manufacturing process changed recently. Simultaneously, I'd review the risk management file to see what biocompatibility risks were identified during design and whether the current complaints align with those assumptions. If the investigation points to a material or process issue, I'd work with the supplier to identify the root cause — for example, a change in raw material source or a curing process deviation. The corrective action would depend on the root cause: it might involve a manufacturing process change, a material substitution, or a design change to the pad's geometry or surface treatment. I'd also update the risk management file and the design history file to reflect the new information, and I'd verify the corrective action through testing (e.g., repeat ISO 10993 testing on the revised material). Throughout the process, I'd keep the quality team and regulatory affairs informed, since this type of complaint may require reporting to regulatory authorities depending on the severity and the jurisdiction. Finally, I'd review the post-market surveillance process to see if there were earlier signals that were missed, and I'd consider whether the complaint criteria or follow-up procedures need to be updated.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to issue a field safety corrective action (recall) versus a less urgent action? What if the investigation finds that the irritation is caused by a patient-specific reaction rather than a product defect?
