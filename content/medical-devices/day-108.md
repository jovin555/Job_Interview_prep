# medical-devices — Day 108

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The measurement path has to be built around the standard's definition of the patient circuit, not around the physical connector layout, because the applied parts may share a common patient-side reference even though they carry different classifications. I'd start by drawing the patient circuit explicitly: which conductors touch the patient, where the patient-side ground reference sits, and whether the BF and CF parts are galvanically separated from each other or share a return. That drawing determines whether you can measure each applied part independently or whether you must measure the combination.

For the measurement network itself, the key elements are a low-leakage, high-impedance measurement front end that doesn't load the circuit under test, a means of applying the specified mains voltage through the standard's measuring device (MD), and a way to reverse polarity and switch between normal condition and single-fault condition without disturbing the setup. I'd build the path so the MD and the polarity reversal are switchable in a fixed, documented configuration, because repeatability across labs and across re-tests matters as much as the absolute number. Stray capacitance to earth in the test setup itself can dominate a marginal reading, so the fixture layout and cable routing need to be controlled and documented.

Deciding which measurements are required comes down to the classification and the configuration: patient leakage current for each applied part type under normal and single-fault conditions, with the appropriate limit for BF versus CF, plus the applied-part-to-applied-part and applied-part-to-earth paths where the standard requires them. Where a BF and CF part share a patient-side reference, I'd treat the worst-case combination as the governing measurement and document the rationale, rather than assuming the individual measurements cover it. I'd also confirm with the test lab early which configurations they intend to measure, so the fixture design supports them from the start rather than being reworked mid-test.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you keep the measurement fixture's own leakage and capacitance from corrupting a marginal reading? What would you do if a CF applied part passes individually but the combined BF/CF configuration exceeds the limit?

## Q2: How would you approach developing a design verification test plan for a medical device that must measure both temperature and pressure, where the two sensors share a common ADC and multiplexer, and the device must maintain specified accuracy for both parameters simultaneously?

**Answer:** The shared ADC and multiplexer are the crux, because the failure modes that matter are the ones that only appear when both channels are active — crosstalk between mux channels, charge injection from switching, settling time that's adequate for one channel but not the other, and reference drift that affects both channels in a correlated way. So the test plan has to go beyond verifying each parameter in isolation and explicitly verify the simultaneous case.

I'd structure it in layers. First, characterize the analog front end per channel: each sensor's accuracy against a traceable reference across its full range, with the other channel idle, to establish the baseline. Second, verify the multiplexer and ADC behavior under switching: settling time after a channel change, channel-to-channel crosstalk at the worst-case source impedance, and whether the mux on-resistance variation across channels introduces a gain error that differs between the two sensors. Third, and most important, the simultaneous test: both sensors driven across their ranges at the same time, including the combinations most likely to expose interaction — for example, a fast-changing pressure signal while temperature is at an extreme — and verify both stay within spec.

I'd also plan for the reference and supply as variables, since a shared ADC means a shared reference: verify accuracy at the reference's tolerance extremes and across the operating temperature range, because a reference drift that's small per channel can still push both parameters out together. The test equipment side needs care too — the pressure and temperature references have their own uncertainties, so the test uncertainty ratio has to be established up front, and where the ratio is marginal I'd either tighten the reference or document the guard band. Finally, I'd define pass/fail criteria and sample sizes against the accuracy specification before running anything, so the plan is verifiable rather than exploratory.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you isolate whether an accuracy failure in the simultaneous test comes from the mux, the ADC, or the sensor itself? What settling-time margin would you design in, and how would you verify it?

## Q3: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it points to a resonance or a specific coupling path rather than broadband susceptibility, so the first job is to characterize it precisely before changing anything. I'd map the band: sweep in fine steps to find the exact center frequency, the bandwidth, and the field strength threshold at which the malfunction starts. Then I'd check whether the frequency corresponds to something physical — a cable length that's a quarter-wave at that frequency, an enclosure cavity resonance, a filter corner, or a clock harmonic on the board. That comparison usually narrows the suspect list fast.

Next I'd localize the coupling path. The classic split is whether the energy is entering through cables (common-mode current on the patient cable or power lead) or directly into the enclosure and onto the PCB. I'd test that by repeating the sweep with cables dressed differently, with ferrites clamped at different points, and with the enclosure partially shielded, watching which change moves the threshold. A current probe on the cables during the sweep tells you directly whether common-mode current is present at the failure frequency. On the board side, a near-field probe can show whether a specific trace or component is picking up the field.

For the fix, the answer depends on the path: if it's cable-borne common-mode, a common-mode choke or improved cable shield termination at the connector is usually the highest-leverage change; if it's a resonance, damping or detuning the resonant structure (cable length, enclosure vent geometry) may be more effective than adding filtering. On the circuit side, I'd look at the affected signal's filtering and at whether the firmware can be made more tolerant — but I'd treat firmware tolerance as a mitigation to be verified, not a substitute for fixing the coupling. Whatever the fix, I'd re-verify across the full band plus margin, not just at the original frequency, because a fix that shifts the resonance can create a new failure elsewhere.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cable resonance from an enclosure resonance experimentally? If the fix works at the test lab but the device is later installed with a different cable, how would you protect against regression?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The decision has to be driven by the hazard analysis, not by how the failure looks in isolation. The question I'd ask first is: what is the worst credible harm to the patient or operator if this failure occurs, given the device's intended use and the clinical environment? A failure that produces an incorrect displayed value on a monitor that a clinician acts on is a different proposition from a failure that produces a cosmetic UI glitch, even if both are "software bugs."

I'd work it through the risk management process: identify the hazardous situation the failure could lead to, estimate severity and probability, and compare against the risk acceptability criteria. If the failure can contribute to a hazardous situation with non-negligible severity, it belongs in the risk file with a risk control, regardless of how rarely it occurs — probability affects priority, not whether it's a safety issue. If the failure genuinely cannot lead to harm — for example, it only affects a non-clinical convenience feature, or there's an independent hardware interlock that makes the hazardous situation impossible — then it can be handled as a reliability or usability issue with normal defect tracking.

The subtle cases are the ones where the failure is a contributing factor rather than a direct cause: a display lag that delays an alarm, a logging gap that removes evidence, a UI state that makes a clinician misread a value. Those need to be assessed in the context of the whole use scenario, including reasonably foreseeable misuse. I'd also be careful about the "it's only a usability issue" shortcut — usability problems that lead to use error are safety issues under the risk process, and IEC 62304 classification follows from the hazard analysis, not from the developer's intuition. Where the team disagrees, I'd take it back to the hazard analysis and the risk acceptability criteria and let the documented rationale settle it, escalating to the quality/regulatory function if the criteria themselves are ambiguous.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure whose severity is high but whose probability is estimated as extremely low? What documentation would you expect to exist to justify a "non-safety" classification if it were challenged in an audit?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat the disagreement as a risk-acceptability question rather than a scheduling argument, because that's what it actually is. The first step is to make sure both sides are looking at the same hazard analysis: what hazardous situation, what severity, what probability estimate, and what the risk acceptability criteria say. Often the disagreement dissolves once the estimates and the criteria are on the table — either the engineering team's "negligible" estimate doesn't survive scrutiny, or the quality manager's concern is already covered by an existing control that wasn't visible in the review.

If the estimates genuinely differ, I'd want to understand why: different assumptions about exposure, different views of the clinical scenario, or different interpretations of the criteria. That's a technical discussion, and it should be resolved with evidence — clinical input, field data, or a more rigorous estimate — rather than by seniority. I'd also separate two questions that often get conflated: whether the risk is acceptable as-is, and whether the proposed control is the right one. Sometimes the quality manager is right that the risk needs attention but the proposed control is disproportionate; in that case the productive move is to find a control that addresses the hazard at lower cost, not to argue about whether to act.

On schedule, I'd be honest that the impact is real and put it in front of the project leadership with the risk rationale, rather than letting the engineering team and quality negotiate it bilaterally. If the risk is judged acceptable without the control, that decision needs to be documented with its rationale and accepted by the accountable function — not just asserted in a meeting. If the control is required, the schedule impact gets managed like any other scope change. Either way, the outcome should be a documented risk decision, because an undocumented "we decided it was fine" is exactly what an auditor will challenge later.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's position is supported by the risk acceptability criteria but the engineering team believes the criteria themselves are too conservative? How would you prevent this kind of disagreement from recurring on the next design review?
