# medical-devices — Day 73

## Q1: How would you approach designing a patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The starting point is the standard, not the schematic. IEC 60601-1 defines the measurement network (the MD, or measuring device, with its specified impedance and frequency response), the test conditions (normal condition and each relevant single-fault condition), and the limits — which differ by applied part classification and by whether the current is patient leakage, earth leakage, or enclosure leakage. So the first step is to build a matrix: for each applied part, list its classification (BF vs CF), whether it is intended for direct cardiac contact, and which fault conditions are applicable. CF parts carry tighter limits than BF, and the measurement network itself differs for CF (the patient leakage measurement for CF parts uses a different MD configuration), so you cannot reuse one setup for both.

For the physical measurement path, I'd treat it as a controlled, repeatable fixture rather than an ad-hoc probe setup. That means: a defined reference earth, a properly characterized MD network, guarding and shielding to keep the measurement from picking up ambient noise, and a stable, low-impedance connection to the applied part under test. The applied part should be connected exactly as the standard specifies — for a CF part, that often means the measurement is made with the part immersed or connected in a defined way, not simply clipped to a wire. I'd also verify the fixture itself by measuring a known reference before trusting any result.

Deciding which measurements are required is a documentation exercise as much as a test one. You enumerate every applied part, classify it, determine which of the leakage current types apply (patient, enclosure, earth), and then determine which single-fault conditions are relevant — for example, an open protective earth conductor, an open one of the means of patient protection, or a mains voltage applied to an applied part. Not every fault applies to every part; a part that is not connected to mains-referenced circuitry may have a different set of applicable faults. The output is a test matrix that maps each applied part and each fault condition to a specific measurement and a specific limit, and that matrix becomes part of the verification evidence.

A practical point: for a device with both BF and CF parts, the CF part usually dominates the isolation design, because its limits are tighter and its fault conditions are more demanding. So the measurement plan should be built around the CF part first, and the BF part verified against the same isolation scheme to confirm it also passes.

- How would you verify that your measurement fixture itself is not contributing to the measured leakage current?
- If a device has a BF part that is not isolated from mains by two means of patient protection, how does that change the applicable fault conditions?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow-band failure is a strong clue: it usually means something in the device is resonant or is being demodulated at that frequency, rather than a broad susceptibility problem. The first step is to characterize the failure precisely — is it a reset, a communication dropout, a sensor reading error, or a display artifact? The nature of the malfunction tells you which subsystem is being disturbed. Then I'd sweep the frequency in fine steps around the failure band to find the exact center and the bandwidth, and vary the field orientation and the cable routing, because a narrow-band failure often depends on the physical configuration of the harness.

The likely mechanisms are: (1) a cable or trace acting as an unintentional antenna at that frequency, coupling energy into a sensitive node; (2) a resonant structure — a cable, a connector, or a slot in the enclosure — that concentrates the field; (3) demodulation of the RF carrier by a nonlinear junction, such as a semiconductor input or a poor connector contact, producing a low-frequency artifact that the firmware then misinterprets; or (4) a clock or reference that is being pulled or disturbed at that frequency.

To isolate the mechanism, I'd use a near-field probe to find where the energy is actually entering the device, and I'd try targeted mitigations one at a time: adding a ferrite or common-mode choke on the offending cable, improving the shielding or grounding of the enclosure, adding a small filter capacitor or RC snubber at the sensitive node, or improving the connector's RF bonding. If the failure is demodulation at an input, a small series resistor and capacitor at the input pin often fixes it. If it's a resonant cable, changing the cable length or adding a ferrite at the right point can shift the resonance out of the test band.

The key discipline is to change one thing at a time and re-test at the exact failure frequency, because a fix that works at one orientation may not work at another. Once a fix is found, I'd verify it across the full frequency range and all orientations, not just the failure band, to make sure the fix hasn't introduced a new susceptibility elsewhere. And I'd document the root cause and the fix in the design history file, because a narrow-band immunity failure is exactly the kind of thing that can reappear if the cable or enclosure changes later.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish between a cable resonance and a demodulation problem at the input pin?
- If the fix requires a change to the PCB rather than the cable, how would you decide whether that change is worth the schedule impact?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The failure mode I want to avoid is the "risk file as a final deliverable" — a binder assembled in the last month before submission, disconnected from the actual design decisions. The way to avoid that is to make the risk management file a living artifact that is updated at the same cadence as the design, and to tie each entry to a specific design decision or test result.

Structurally, I'd start with the risk management plan, which defines the scope, the applicable standards, the risk acceptability criteria, and the roles and responsibilities. Then the hazard analysis: a systematic identification of hazards — energy hazards, biological hazards, environmental hazards, use hazards, and software hazards — using a method appropriate to the device, such as a fault tree, a failure mode and effects analysis, or a hazard and operability study. Each hazard gets a risk estimate (severity and probability), and each risk that is not acceptable gets a risk control measure. The risk control measure is then verified — not just "we added a fuse," but "we tested that the fuse opens under the specified fault current and the device remains safe." That verification is the link between the risk file and the verification test plan.

The part that keeps it useful is traceability. Each risk control should trace to a design input, and each design input should trace to a verification test. That way, when a design change happens, you can see immediately which risks are affected and which verifications need to be re-run. I'd keep the traceability in a structured tool — a requirements management tool or at minimum a controlled spreadsheet — rather than in prose, because prose traceability rots quickly.

I'd also build in a review cadence: the risk file is reviewed at each design review, and any new hazard identified during testing or from field data is added. And I'd keep the residual risk evaluation explicit — after all controls, what risk remains, and is it acceptable per the criteria defined in the plan? That residual risk acceptance is a documented decision, not an implicit one. The result is a file that a reviewer can follow from hazard to control to verification to residual risk, and that stays current because it's updated as part of the normal design process.

- How would you handle a hazard that is identified late in the project, after the design is largely frozen?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The decision should be driven by the risk analysis, not by intuition about how "serious" the failure feels. The starting question is: can this failure, directly or in combination with other failures, lead to an unacceptable risk to the patient or operator? If the answer is yes, it's safety-related and needs formal risk controls. If the answer is no — the failure only affects convenience, or it produces a nuisance that the user can recover from without harm — then it's a usability or reliability issue, and it should be handled through the normal defect and reliability processes, not through the safety risk file.

The practical way to make that determination is to trace the failure to the hazard analysis. For each software function, ask what the hazardous situation would be if the function failed, and what the severity of the resulting harm would be. A failure that could contribute to a hazardous situation with serious harm is safety-related. A failure that only degrades a non-critical display, or that produces an alarm that the user can verify and dismiss, is not. The IEC 62304 software safety classification is the formal expression of this: Class A for no injury possible, Class B for non-serious injury possible, Class C for death or serious injury possible. The classification is per software item or per system, and it drives the rigor of the development process.

There's a subtlety: a failure can be non-safety-related in isolation but safety-related in combination. For example, a logging failure is not itself hazardous, but if the log is used to reconstruct a clinical event, its corruption could mislead a clinician. So the analysis has to consider the function's role in the overall clinical workflow, not just its immediate output. I'd also consider whether the failure is detectable by the user — an undetectable failure that silently produces wrong data is more dangerous than a failure that produces an obvious error message.

Once classified, the handling differs. A safety-related failure gets a formal risk control, a verification that the control is effective, and a place in the risk management file. A non-safety failure gets a defect ticket, a root-cause analysis if it's recurring, and a fix in the normal release cycle. The important thing is that the classification is documented and defensible, because a reviewer will ask why a particular failure was or wasn't treated as safety-related.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a failure that is safety-related only when combined with a specific user action?
- If a failure is classified as non-safety-related but later field data suggests it can contribute to harm, how would you handle the reclassification?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a project where the clinical team has requested a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the clinical need from the proposed solution. The clinical team is asking for a change because they've identified a usability problem — but the specific change they've proposed may not be the only way to address it, and it may not be the most cost-effective. So I'd start by understanding the underlying need: what task is difficult, what error is the user making, and what is the clinical consequence of that error? That reframes the conversation from "should we do this change?" to "how do we address this need?"

Next, I'd evaluate the options against the schedule and regulatory impact. A hardware revision late in development is expensive not just in engineering time but in re-verification, re-validation, and potentially a new regulatory submission. So I'd ask: can the need be met with a firmware change, a labeling change, a training change, or a change to the user interface that doesn't require new hardware? If a hardware change is genuinely required, can it be scoped to a small, well-understood modification that doesn't invalidate the existing verification? And if the change does require a full re-spin, what is the actual regulatory impact — is it a new submission, or a supplement to an existing one?

Then I'd bring the decision to the right forum. This is not a decision for the engineering lead alone; it's a cross-functional decision involving clinical, regulatory, quality, and program management. I'd present the options with their trade-offs: the clinical benefit of the change, the schedule and cost impact, the regulatory impact, and the risk of not making the change. If the clinical need is significant — for example, the usability problem could lead to a use error with patient harm — then the change may be necessary regardless of schedule, and the right response is to replan the submission rather than ship a device with a known usability hazard. If the need is a convenience improvement, it may be better to defer it to the next revision.

Whatever the decision, I'd document it. If we defer the change, the rationale and the plan for addressing it later should be recorded, and if the usability issue is a potential hazard, it should be entered into the risk management file. If we make the change, the impact on the design history file, the verification plan, and the regulatory submission should be assessed and planned before the change is implemented.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle the situation if the clinical team and the program manager disagree on whether the change is necessary?
- If the change is deferred, how would you ensure the usability issue is tracked and addressed in the next revision?
