# medical-devices — Day 112

## Q1: How would you approach designing the alarm signal chain for a device that must meet IEC 60601-1-8, where the same microcontroller drives both the audible and visual alarm outputs?

**Answer:** The core principle in IEC 60601-1-8 is that the alarm system must be reliable enough that a single fault cannot silently defeat the alarm function, and that the priority of an alarm is communicated consistently across all its modalities. If one microcontroller pin drives both the buzzer and the LED, you have a single point of failure that can disable both pathways at once — which is exactly the architecture the standard is trying to prevent.

My approach would be to separate the two modalities at the hardware level. The visual alarm typically goes through a dedicated LED driver with its own enable, while the audible alarm goes through a separate driver stage — ideally with independent control signals from the MCU, or better, with the audible path able to be triggered by a hardware watchdog or supervisory circuit if the firmware stops servicing it. That way a firmware hang or a shorted output pin doesn't take out both.

On the firmware side, I'd treat the alarm state machine as a safety-critical module with its own self-test: at startup and periodically, drive each output and confirm feedback (e.g., a sense line on the LED string, or a current-sense on the buzzer) so the device can detect a failed modality and escalate — for example, if the audible path fails, the visual alarm should go to its highest urgency pattern and the device should flag a technical alarm. I'd also make sure the alarm priority mapping (high/medium/low) is defined in one place and that the timing patterns (pulse rates, duty cycles) are verified against the standard's tables, not just "looks right on the bench."

Verification would include fault injection: open the buzzer, short the LED driver, stall the MCU, and confirm the remaining pathway still annunciates at the correct priority and that the technical alarm for the failed modality is raised.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a distributed alarm system (e.g., a remote nurse-call interface) needs its own independent alarm generation? What would you do if the only buzzer available can't meet the sound pressure level requirement at the specified distance without drawing more current than the battery budget allows?

## Q2: How would you approach determining which parts of IEC 60601-1 apply to a device that is battery-powered and has no mains connection at all?

**Answer:** It's a common misconception that a battery-powered device is "exempt" from IEC 60601-1 — it isn't. The standard applies to the device as a whole, and many clauses are still relevant even without a mains supply. The first thing I'd do is establish the device's classification under the standard: is it internally powered, what is its type of protection against electric shock (B, BF, CF), what is its mode of operation, and what is its intended environment (professional healthcare facility vs. home healthcare). Those classifications drive which clauses actually bite.

For an internally powered device, the mains-related clauses (protective earth, mains fuses, mains isolation) drop away, but a surprising amount remains: patient leakage current limits still apply and are actually harder to meet in some ways because there's no earth reference — the leakage path is through the patient to whatever else is nearby. Dielectric strength testing still applies between applied parts and accessible parts. Creepage and clearance still apply between circuits at different potentials, including between the battery circuit and the patient-connected circuit. Mechanical safety, enclosure robustness, temperature limits (touch temperature), and IP rating for the intended environment all still apply. And if the device has any means of connecting to mains — even a charger — then the moment it's charging while in use, you're back in the mains-connected world and need to think about 2×MOPP or 1×MOPP depending on the applied part type.

I'd build a clause-by-clause applicability matrix early, with a justification for each "not applicable" so it's defensible in the test report and the DHF. That matrix also becomes the backbone of the test plan.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device that is normally battery-powered but has an optional mains adapter for charging — does the whole device become "mains-connected" for classification purposes? What changes if the device is intended for home use versus professional use?

## Q3: How would you approach a situation where, during design verification, a device passes all its functional tests but the test lab reports that the measured patient leakage current is uncomfortably close to the limit — within, say, 10% of the allowable value — even though it technically passes?

**Answer:** Passing with a thin margin is a design risk, not a win. The limit is a ceiling, not a target, and a device that measures within 10% of the limit on one unit at one temperature is one component tolerance, one cable routing change, or one production batch away from failing in the field or at re-test. I'd treat it as a design issue to be understood and, ideally, reduced.

First I'd characterize the margin properly: is the measurement stable across units, across temperature, across the full range of applied-part configurations, and across the normal/single-fault conditions the standard requires? A single passing measurement at room temperature tells you very little. I'd want to know the distribution — build or borrow several units, measure at the temperature extremes, and see whether the worst case is actually 10% or whether it's 2% on a cold unit. That tells me whether I have a design problem or a measurement problem.

Then I'd trace where the current is coming from. Patient leakage current in a well-designed isolated front-end is dominated by the isolation barrier's capacitance and any Y-capacitors or filter components that bridge the barrier. If the barrier has more capacitance than it needs, or if there's a filter component bridging the barrier that isn't strictly necessary, that's a candidate for reduction. I'd also check the measurement setup itself — a leakage current measurement is sensitive to the test fixture, the reference earth, and the meter's own input impedance, and it's not unheard of for a "close to limit" reading to be partly a setup artifact.

If the margin can't be improved without a redesign, I'd document the worst-case analysis, add a production test with a guard band, and flag it as a residual risk in the risk management file — but I'd push hard to get the margin to a comfortable level first, because a thin margin is a latent field failure.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you set a production test limit that's tighter than the standard's limit without rejecting good units? What design changes would you consider if the dominant contributor turned out to be the isolation transformer's inter-winding capacitance?

## Q4: How would you approach building a design history file for a device that is a derivative of a previously cleared product — same platform, new sensor and new enclosure — so that the DHF is complete and defensible without duplicating everything from the parent device?

**Answer:** The temptation with a derivative device is to copy the parent DHF wholesale and change the cover page, which produces a document that's technically complete but doesn't actually demonstrate that the new device was designed and verified. The other failure mode is to start from scratch and lose the traceability to the platform that justified reusing so much of the design. The right approach is somewhere in between, and it starts with a clear boundary document.

I'd begin with a gap analysis: enumerate every design input of the new device, and for each one, mark whether it's satisfied by the inherited platform (with a reference to the parent's evidence), satisfied by new design work, or affected by the change and therefore requiring re-verification. That gap analysis becomes the map for the whole DHF. The parent's verification evidence can be referenced rather than duplicated, but only where the change doesn't invalidate it — and the justification for "not invalidated" has to be written down, not assumed.

The new sensor and enclosure will drive most of the new work: new design inputs (accuracy, biocompatibility, ingress protection, drop), new risk analysis (the new sensor introduces new failure modes; the new enclosure changes the thermal and EMC picture), new verification (sensor accuracy, enclosure drop and IP testing, EMC re-test because the enclosure is part of the EMC solution), and new usability work if the enclosure changes the user interface. I'd also re-run the risk management file rather than just appending to it, because the hazard analysis for a new sensor and enclosure is genuinely different.

Throughout, I'd keep the traceability matrix as the single source of truth: every design input links to its design output, its verification evidence (new or referenced), and its risk control. At the end, the DHF should let a reviewer pick any requirement of the new device and follow it to evidence in two or three hops — and that's the test I'd apply before calling it done.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a change to the enclosure is significant enough to require a new 60601-1-2 EMC test versus a justified delta test? What would you do if the parent device's DHF turned out to have gaps that the new device now depends on?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and a clinical advisor tells you that a feature the team considers fully specified — say, the way the device displays a trend over time — will not actually be usable by nurses in the intended workflow, because it requires too many taps during a busy shift. The fix touches firmware, the user interface, and the instructions for use. How would you handle this?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is resist the instinct to defend the specification. A clinical advisor telling you the workflow doesn't work is exactly the kind of input usability engineering is supposed to capture, and if it's arriving late, that's a process gap worth understanding — but the immediate job is to evaluate the claim honestly rather than argue about whose fault the timing is.

I'd ask the advisor to walk me through the actual workflow, ideally with the device in hand or a prototype, and observe where the friction is. "Too many taps" is a symptom; I want to understand the underlying task — is the nurse checking a trend during a handover, during a response to an alarm, or during a routine round? Each of those has a different time budget and a different tolerance for interaction. I'd also want to know whether this is one advisor's opinion or a pattern across several clinicians, because a single data point shouldn't drive a late change on its own.

Then I'd scope the fix. There's usually a spectrum from "reorder the existing screens and add a shortcut" (firmware and UI only, IFU update, no hardware change) to "add a physical control" (hardware revision, new regulatory submission). I'd push hard to find the smallest change that actually solves the workflow problem, and I'd bring the firmware and UI leads into that scoping early so the estimate is real. If the smallest viable fix still requires a hardware revision and a submission delay, that's a business decision, not an engineering one — I'd present the options with their cost, schedule, and risk implications to the project leadership and let them decide, with a clear recommendation.

Whatever the outcome, I'd capture the finding in the usability engineering file and the risk management file, because "the intended user found the feature unusable in the intended workflow" is a use-related hazard, and it needs to be documented and dispositioned even if the decision is to defer the fix to a future revision.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you validate that the revised interaction actually solves the workflow problem without running a full formative usability study? If the decision is made to defer the fix, how do you document that in a way that's defensible to a regulator?
