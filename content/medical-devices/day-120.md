# medical-devices — Day 120

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The measurement path has to be built around the standard's circuit model, not around convenience. IEC 60601-1 defines leakage current as flowing through a defined measuring device (the MD) connected between the applied part and earth, with the device under test powered in its worst-case configuration. So the first step is to build a measurement setup that faithfully reproduces that model: a properly characterized MD with the correct input impedance and frequency response, a low-impedance earth reference, and a way to switch the mains polarity and to apply the single-fault conditions the standard requires (open neutral, open protective earth, reversed mains). The measurement chain itself should be short, shielded, and referenced to the same earth as the DUT, otherwise you measure your own loop.

For a device with both BF and CF applied parts, the key decision is which measurements are actually required, and that comes from the classification and the standard's tables. BF and CF parts have different limits and different fault-condition requirements — CF is the most stringent because it's intended for direct cardiac contact, so its patient leakage limits and its isolation requirements are tighter. You enumerate the applied parts, classify each one, and then work through the standard's measurement matrix: patient leakage from each applied part to earth, patient leakage between applied parts where the standard requires it, and the applicable normal-condition and single-fault-condition cases. You don't measure every combination blindly — you measure the combinations the standard defines for that classification, plus any combination your risk analysis identifies as a credible fault path (for example, a fault that could bridge a BF part to a CF part).

A practical point: the measurement path for a CF part often needs to be physically separate and better guarded than for a BF part, because the currents are smaller and the setup is more sensitive to stray coupling. I'd build the fixture so each applied part has its own dedicated, switchable connection to the MD, with the ability to isolate the non-tested parts, and I'd document the exact configuration for each measurement so the test is repeatable and auditable. The output of this exercise is a measurement plan that maps each required measurement to a standard clause, a limit, and a test configuration — that's what makes the leakage testing defensible rather than ad hoc.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify that your measurement setup itself is not contributing error — for example, that the MD and cabling aren't adding stray capacitance that inflates the reading? What would you do if a BF part passes its limit but a CF part on the same device is marginal, and the two share a common reference?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The classification should be driven by the hazard analysis, not by how the failure looks in isolation. The question I ask is: if this failure occurs, what is the resulting hazardous situation, and can it lead to harm? A software failure that produces a wrong displayed value, a missed alarm, a stuck output, or a delayed response can be safety-related even if the code path looks trivial, because the consequence is what matters. Conversely, a failure that only affects convenience — a log that doesn't rotate, a UI animation that stutters — is a reliability or usability issue unless it degrades a safety function.

The practical process is to trace the failure to the risk management file. For each software function, I'd look at whether it implements or supports a risk control, or whether it's part of a function whose failure could contribute to a hazardous situation identified in the hazard analysis. If it does, the failure is safety-related and needs to be handled under the software safety process — with the appropriate rigor for its software safety class, verification evidence, and traceability to the risk control. If it doesn't, it can be handled as a normal defect with normal severity/priority triage.

The subtle cases are the ones where the failure is "only" a usability problem but the usability problem could lead a clinician to act incorrectly — for example, a confusing alarm state that causes a user to silence the wrong alarm. That's where usability engineering and risk management have to talk to each other: a usability issue that can lead to a use error with a hazardous outcome is a safety-related issue, and it should be captured as such. So my rule of thumb is: classify by consequence and by whether the function is in the safety chain, document the rationale, and when in doubt, err toward treating it as safety-related and let the risk analysis justify de-escalating it rather than the other way around.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that is safety-related only in combination with another independent failure — does that change the classification? What evidence would you want in the software problem report to justify a "non-safety" classification if a reviewer challenged it?

## Q3: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue that something in the device is resonant or that a specific coupling path is being excited at that frequency. I'd start by characterizing the failure precisely: sweep the band in fine steps, record the exact frequency, the field strength at which it starts, the modulation, and the exact malfunction symptom. Reproducibility is valuable — it means I can iterate quickly — so I'd build a bench setup that reproduces the same field at that frequency so I'm not dependent on lab time for every experiment.

Then I'd work the coupling path. At a single frequency, the likely mechanisms are: a cable or trace acting as an antenna and resonating (common-mode current on an I/O cable is a classic), a slot or seam in the enclosure resonating, a component or trace whose self-resonance lands in that band, or a ground/reference impedance that becomes significant at that frequency. I'd use a near-field probe and a current probe to find where the energy is actually entering and where it's largest, and I'd compare the behavior with cables dressed differently, with the enclosure shielded or not, and with individual subsystems disconnected or bypassed to localize the path.

Once I know the path, the fix follows the mechanism. If it's common-mode current on a cable, I'd look at common-mode chokes, ferrites, cable shielding and termination, and the quality of the chassis ground bond at the connector. If it's a resonance in the enclosure, I'd look at seam bonding, gasketing, and slot length. If it's a trace or component resonance, I'd look at layout, filtering at the pin, and damping. I'd also check whether the malfunction is a firmware-visible upset (a reset, a corrupted register, a communication error) versus an analog measurement shift, because that tells me whether I'm fixing an immunity problem at the digital core or at the analog front-end. The fix should be verified at the bench first, then confirmed in the lab across the full band, and I'd want margin — not just passing at the exact frequency, but passing with headroom across the band, because production variation will move the resonance.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a resonance in the enclosure from a resonance on a cable without a full anechoic chamber? If the fix is a ferrite on a cable, how would you make sure it's effective in production and not just on your bench unit?

## Q4: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?

**Answer:** Traceability is only useful if it's maintained continuously, so I'd treat it as a living structure rather than a document assembled at the end. The backbone is a requirements management approach where every design input has a unique identifier, every design output is linked to the input(s) it satisfies, every verification test is linked to the output(s) it verifies, and every validation activity is linked to the user needs and intended use it addresses. The DHF then becomes a set of views over that linked structure rather than a pile of separate documents.

The hard part is change. When a design input changes, the traceability has to propagate: which outputs are affected, which verification tests need to be re-run or re-evaluated, which risk controls are affected, and whether validation is impacted. I'd handle this with a change control process that requires the impact analysis to be recorded as part of the change, and with a traceability matrix that is regenerated or reviewed at each design review rather than only at the end. The matrix should show gaps explicitly — an input with no output, an output with no verification, a verification with no result — because those gaps are exactly what a reviewer or auditor will find.

Practically, I'd keep the identifiers stable and meaningful, keep the links in a tool that can produce the matrix on demand, and make the design review agenda walk the traceability rather than just the documents. I'd also make sure the risk management file is linked into the same structure, because risk controls are design inputs and their verification is part of the same traceability. The test of whether it's working is simple: if someone asks "why does this test exist and what requirement does it verify," the answer should be findable in a couple of minutes, and if a requirement changes, the set of affected tests should fall out of the structure rather than being reconstructed by memory.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle traceability for a requirement that is verified by analysis or inspection rather than by test? What would you do if you inherited a project where the traceability matrix was incomplete and the design was already at verification stage?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a risk-acceptance disagreement to be resolved with evidence, not as a schedule fight. The first step is to make sure we're actually arguing about the same thing: I'd ask the quality manager to walk through the hazard, the sequence of events, the severity and probability estimates, and the basis for those estimates, and I'd have the engineering team present the basis for calling it negligible. Often the disagreement turns out to be about a missing piece of information on one side or the other — a fault path the team hadn't considered, or a probability estimate the quality manager had assumed was higher than the data supports.

If the estimates are genuinely different, I'd look at what evidence would settle it: fault tree or FMEA review, field data from similar products, test data, or a targeted analysis. I'd want the decision documented in the risk management file either way, because "we discussed it and decided it was negligible" needs a rationale that a reviewer can follow. If the quality manager still wants the control after that, I'd evaluate the control on its merits: does it actually reduce the risk meaningfully, is it proportionate to the severity, and what does it cost in schedule and complexity? A control that's cheap and effective is worth doing even for a low risk; a control that's expensive and adds its own failure modes for a hazard that's genuinely negligible is a different conversation.

On the schedule question, I'd separate the decision from the timing. If the control is justified, I'd look at whether it can be implemented in a way that doesn't move the critical path — a firmware guard, a test, a labeling change — and if it genuinely can't, I'd bring the trade-off to the project decision-makers with a clear statement of the risk, the cost, and the options, rather than unilaterally deciding. The quality manager's job is to protect the risk process, and my job is to make sure the decision is made on evidence and recorded, not to win the argument. If we still disagree after that, the escalation path is the design review board or the management with risk acceptance authority, and I'd make sure the disagreement and its resolution are documented.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality manager's concern turned out to be valid but the control they proposed introduced a new hazard? What would you want in the risk management file to show that a "negligible" determination was made rigorously rather than conveniently?
