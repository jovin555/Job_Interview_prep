# medical-devices — Day 125

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement matrix, because the required measurements are driven by classification, not by convenience. The general framework is: identify each applied part and its type (B, BF, CF), then for each one determine the applicable leakage current limits under normal condition and single-fault condition — patient leakage current, patient auxiliary current where relevant, and for CF parts the tighter limits that apply because of the direct cardiac connection assumption. The measurement path itself has to be built so that the meter sees only the current flowing through the patient connection of interest, with the rest of the device configured per the standard's test circuit — mains polarity, earth conditions, and the single-fault condition being applied in the specified way. Practically, that means a dedicated measurement jig with a low-impedance, calibrated measurement network, guarded so that stray capacitance and the meter's own loading don't corrupt the reading, and with the applied part isolated from everything except the measurement path. For a device with both BF and CF parts, I would not assume one measurement covers both — the CF part typically needs its own measurement with the tighter limit, and the BF part is measured separately. I'd also decide which measurements are actually required by working from the standard's decision tree rather than measuring everything: some measurements are only required when a particular configuration exists (e.g., whether the applied part is intended for direct cardiac connection, whether the device has a mains connection at all, whether parts are simultaneously accessible). The output of that exercise is a measurement plan that lists each applied part, its classification, the applicable limit, the test condition, and the pass criterion — which then becomes the verification evidence.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where two applied parts of different classifications are simultaneously accessible to the patient — does that change the measurement approach? What would you do if a measurement is marginally passing and you suspect the test setup itself is contributing to the reading?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it usually points to a resonance or a specific coupling path rather than a broad susceptibility problem. My first step is to characterize the failure precisely: sweep the band in fine steps to find the exact center frequency and the bandwidth over which it fails, and note whether the malfunction correlates with field strength (i.e., is it a threshold effect?) and with the modulation used in the test. That tells me whether I'm looking at a linear coupling problem or something demodulating the RF envelope. Next I'd localize the coupling path. Common candidates are cable harnesses acting as antennas, unshielded seams or apertures in the enclosure, and PCB structures — a trace or plane resonance, a poorly decoupled reference, or a connector interface. I'd use a near-field probe to scan the board and cables while injecting at the failing frequency, and I'd try targeted mitigations one at a time: ferrites or common-mode chokes on the offending cable, additional shielding or a gasket at a seam, local decoupling at the affected IC, or a small RC snubber at a susceptible node. The key discipline is changing one thing at a time and re-testing at the exact failing frequency, so I know which mitigation actually moved the result. If the root cause is a firmware-visible symptom — e.g., a communication link dropping — I'd also check whether the link has adequate error detection and recovery, because a robust protocol can turn a marginal physical-layer hit into a recoverable event rather than a malfunction. The fix should be verified not just at the failing frequency but across the full test band, to make sure the mitigation didn't shift the problem elsewhere.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a coupling problem on a cable versus a resonance on the PCB itself? If the mitigation works in the lab but the device is a sealed unit, how do you confirm the fix is manufacturable and repeatable?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file stays useful only if it's built as a living part of the design process, not as a retrospective compilation. I'd structure it around a small number of linked artifacts: a hazard analysis that starts at the concept stage and is revisited at each design review; a risk estimation and evaluation record that ties each hazardous situation to its severity and probability and to the acceptability criteria defined up front; a risk control record that traces each control to its verification evidence; and a residual risk evaluation that captures the overall benefit-risk judgment. The critical structural choice is traceability — every risk control should point to the design output that implements it and the verification that confirms it works, and every design change should trigger a review of whether it introduces a new hazard or invalidates an existing control. I'd also keep the file's structure aligned with the design history file so that a reviewer can move from a requirement to a hazard to a control to a test without hunting. Practically, that means assigning ownership, scheduling risk reviews as standing agenda items at design gates, and treating the file as a configuration-controlled artifact with a revision history — so that when an auditor asks "why is this control here and how do you know it works," the answer is a two-click trace rather than a reconstruction. The failure mode I'd actively avoid is the file that gets written once at the start and then diverges from the actual design, because that's worse than no file — it's misleading evidence.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design change late in the project that invalidates a previously verified risk control? What's the minimum content a risk management file needs to be defensible to an auditor?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means the software's failure could contribute to a hazardous situation with unacceptable risk, so the verification has to demonstrate that the safety-related requirements are met with a level of rigor that matches that consequence. I'd start from the software requirements specification and the architecture, because the standard's requirements are structured around them: the safety-related requirements need to be identified and traced, the architecture needs to show how the safety-critical functions are segregated or protected, and the detailed design needs to be documented to the depth the classification demands. Verification then proceeds in layers — unit-level testing of the safety-critical modules, integration testing of the interfaces between them, and system-level testing against the requirements, with each test traced back to the requirement it verifies. For Class C specifically, I'd expect the verification to include things like boundary and fault-injection testing of the safety functions, static analysis or code review evidence for the critical modules, and evidence that the build and configuration are controlled. The other half of the answer is the process evidence: problem reports and their resolution, the software configuration management record, and the release criteria that show the software was only released when the verification was complete and the known anomalies were assessed. I'd also make sure the verification is independent enough — a reviewer who didn't write the code should be looking at the critical modules — because the standard's intent is that the evidence is credible, not just present.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you demonstrate that a safety-critical function is adequately segregated from non-critical code in the architecture? What would you do if a known anomaly in a Class C module can't be fixed before release?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a disagreement about risk acceptability, not about whether the quality manager is being unreasonable — and the way to resolve it is to make the disagreement explicit and evidence-based rather than positional. First, I'd ask the quality manager to walk through the hazard and the reasoning behind the proposed control, because sometimes the concern is legitimate and the engineering team has underestimated the severity or the probability, or hasn't considered a use scenario the quality manager has in mind. If after that discussion the team still believes the risk is negligible, I'd go back to the risk management file: what does the hazard analysis say about severity and probability, what's the acceptability criterion we agreed at the start of the project, and does the current design meet it? If the file supports the team's position, that's the evidence to bring to the conversation — the decision should be traceable to the agreed criteria, not to who feels more strongly. If the file is ambiguous or the criteria weren't clearly defined, that's a gap worth fixing regardless of the outcome. On the schedule question, I'd separate the risk decision from the schedule decision: first agree on whether the control is needed, then, if it is, evaluate the cost and timing options — can it be implemented in a way that doesn't move the submission, can it be a design change for the next revision with a documented rationale, or does it genuinely have to be now? I'd also escalate to the design review chair or the appropriate decision authority if we can't converge, because a documented escalation is better than an unresolved disagreement sitting in the file. The outcome I'd want is a decision that's defensible to an auditor either way — either the control is in and verified, or the rationale for not adding it is documented and agreed.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's concern turns out to be valid but the control can't be implemented before the submission deadline? How do you make sure a decision like this doesn't set a precedent that every negligible hazard gets a control?
