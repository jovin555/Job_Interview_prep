# medical-devices — Day 111

## Q1: How would you approach deciding whether a device's software needs to be classified as Class A, B, or C under IEC 62304, and what would you do if the hardware and software leads disagreed on the classification?

**Answer:** The classification is driven by the *software system's* contribution to hazardous situations identified in the ISO 14971 risk analysis — not by how complex or "important" the code feels. The process is: start from the risk file, ask which hazardous situations could arise from software behavior (including software failing to function, functioning incorrectly, or being used in a way that produces a hazard), and then determine whether that software failure could contribute to an unacceptable risk. Class A applies when no injury or damage to health is possible; Class B when non-serious injury is possible; Class C when death or serious injury is possible. The key discipline is to classify the *software item or system* based on the worst credible outcome it can contribute to, and to document the rationale — not just the label — so it's auditable.

If the hardware and software leads disagreed, I'd treat it as a risk-analysis disagreement rather than a turf issue. I'd bring both back to the hazard analysis: which specific hazardous situations does each of us think the software can contribute to, and what's the severity of the resulting harm? Often the disagreement dissolves once you trace it to concrete hazards — one person is thinking of a benign failure mode, the other of a worst-case one. If it doesn't resolve, I'd escalate to the risk management lead or quality, because the classification drives the rigor of the whole software lifecycle (documentation, verification depth, traceability), and getting it wrong in either direction is a real problem — under-classifying is a compliance and safety risk, over-classifying wastes effort and can mask the real risk picture. I'd also note that classification can be revisited as the design matures; it's a living decision, not a one-time stamp.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the software is decomposed into items, can different items carry different classes, and how do you handle a Class C item that depends on a Class A library? How would you document the classification rationale so a reviewer can follow it without you in the room?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating the problem into frequency domains and understanding where the noise is actually coming from before adding any components. Conducted emissions (typically 150 kHz–30 MHz) are dominated by differential-mode and common-mode currents on the power and interface cables; radiated emissions (30 MHz and up) are driven by loop areas, cable acting as an antenna, and fast edges on the PCB. The filtering strategy has to address each mechanism at its source where possible, then at the boundary.

On the power input, a common-mode choke plus X-capacitors (differential) and Y-capacitors (common-mode to chassis) forms the classic front-end, but the Y-cap value is constrained by leakage current limits under IEC 60601-1 — that's the central tension in medical designs, because you often want more Y-capacitance than the leakage budget allows. So I'd design the filter around the leakage budget first, then push the burden back onto the source: slow the switching edges where the topology permits, add snubbers, improve layout to shrink high-di/dt loops, and keep the noisy switching node physically small. On the signal side, the same principle applies — filter at the connector for cable-borne common-mode, but keep the filter's passband and impedance compatible with the signal so you don't degrade signal integrity or introduce ringing.

The signal-integrity side is where the trade-off gets real: an aggressive low-pass filter that kills emissions can also kill edge rate, add jitter, or load a sensitive analog front-end. So I'd define the signal's actual bandwidth requirement first, choose filter corner frequencies with margin above that, and verify with both a spectrum analyzer (for emissions) and eye/time-domain measurements (for SI) rather than optimizing one at the expense of the other. I'd also plan for pre-compliance iteration — filter design for EMC is rarely right the first time, and having a test plan that lets you swap component values quickly saves schedule.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide between a ferrite bead and an LC filter on a signal line, and what parasitic effects bite you at higher frequencies? If the Y-capacitance is capped by leakage current, what other levers do you have to meet the conducted emissions limit?

## Q3: How would you approach developing a clinical evaluation report (CER) for a novel medical device that has no substantially equivalent predicate device on the market?

**Answer:** With no predicate, the CER has to stand on the clinical evidence itself rather than on equivalence, so the strategy shifts from "demonstrate substantial equivalence" to "demonstrate clinical benefit and acceptable risk through the evidence available." I'd start by defining the clinical claims and the intended use precisely, because the entire evidence plan flows from them — what clinical benefit is being claimed, in what population, against what comparator or standard of care. Then I'd map the available evidence sources: published literature on the clinical problem and on comparable technologies or approaches, any clinical data generated during development (usability studies, formative/summative evaluations, any pilot or first-in-human data if applicable), and post-market data from similar devices in the field if it exists.

The appraisal step matters as much as the search: I'd apply a structured method (e.g., a recognized literature appraisal framework) to judge the quality and relevance of each source, and be explicit about the gaps. For a truly novel device, the honest conclusion may be that the evidence base is thin and that additional clinical investigation is required before or as a condition of market entry — and the CER should say so rather than overstate. I'd also make sure the CER is written to be maintained: it's a living document tied to post-market surveillance, so the evidence review, the benefit-risk conclusion, and the plan for updating it should be structured so the next revision is an update, not a rewrite.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the benefit-risk analysis when the clinical evidence is limited but the device addresses an unmet need? What role does the post-market clinical follow-up plan play in supporting a CER with acknowledged evidence gaps?

## Q4: During IEC 60601-1 testing, a device fails the dielectric strength (hi-pot) test between the patient connection and mains earth. How would you diagnose and resolve it?

**Answer:** A hi-pot failure means the insulation barrier isn't holding the test voltage, so I'd treat it as a barrier-integrity investigation and work from the outside in. First, confirm the failure is real and reproducible — check the test setup itself (test voltage ramp rate, dwell time, humidity, whether the device was properly conditioned), because a setup error or a stray test lead can produce a false failure. Assuming it's real, I'd localize it: does it fail immediately (a hard short or gross creepage violation) or break down after some seconds (a marginal gap, contamination, or a component with insufficient rating)? I'd inspect the barrier physically — creepage and clearance distances against the required values for the applicable means of protection, solder mask integrity, conformal coating coverage, any contamination or flux residue, and whether a component straddling the barrier (an optocoupler, an isolation transformer, a Y-capacitor, a connector) is rated for the working voltage and test voltage.

Common root causes are: a creepage path that's shorter than calculated because of a component pad or via too close to the barrier, a Y-capacitor or optocoupler with an inadequate isolation rating, moisture or contamination providing a leakage path, or a PCB fabrication defect like insufficient spacing or a plating bridge. The fix depends on which: increase spacing, change to a higher-rated isolation component, improve coating or cleaning, or correct the layout. I'd verify the fix by re-running hi-pot at the correct test voltage and also re-checking the related leakage current measurements, since the two are linked — a barrier that's marginal on hi-pot is often also marginal on patient leakage current. And I'd feed the finding back into the risk file and design review so the barrier design is re-examined as a whole, not just patched at the failure point.

**Possible follow-ups:** How do you decide whether a hi-pot failure is a design issue versus a manufacturing/process issue, and how does that change your corrective action? What's the relationship between the hi-pot test voltage and the working voltage of the barrier, and how do you confirm the test itself isn't overstressing good units?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd resist framing it as "engineering vs. quality" and instead take it back to the risk management process, because that's where the disagreement actually lives. The question isn't whether the hazard *feels* negligible — it's whether the risk estimate and the acceptability decision are documented and defensible. So I'd ask the quality manager to walk through the hazard: what's the severity, what's the probability, what's the resulting risk level against the acceptability criteria, and what evidence supports that estimate? And I'd ask the engineering team the same, because "negligible" is a judgment that needs to be backed by the hazard analysis, not asserted.

Often the disagreement is really about *probability estimation* — engineering may be reasoning from bench experience that the failure mode is rare, while quality is reasoning from the worst credible case or from a regulatory expectation. If the risk estimate is genuinely borderline, the right move may be to gather more data (test, analysis, field data from similar designs) rather than argue. If the risk is truly acceptable per the documented criteria, I'd want that conclusion recorded with its rationale so it's auditable — and I'd be open to the possibility that quality is right and the team has anchored on "negligible" without evidence. If we still disagree after that, I'd escalate to the risk management review or a design review board rather than let it become a schedule fight, because the decision needs to be made on the risk file, not on who's more senior or more persistent.

On the schedule impact: I'd be honest that it's real and quantify it, but I'd frame the trade-off correctly — a risk control that's genuinely needed isn't optional, and a risk control that isn't needed shouldn't be added just to close the conversation. The worst outcome is adding an unnecessary control that adds cost and complexity without reducing risk, or skipping a necessary one to protect a date. Either way, the decision and its rationale go in the record.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the risk is judged acceptable but quality still wants a control, what options do you have short of a full design change — for example, labeling, IFU changes, or monitoring? How would you handle it if the quality manager's concern turns out to be valid but the schedule truly can't absorb the fix before submission?
