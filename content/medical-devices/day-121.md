# medical-devices — Day 121

## Q1: How would you approach designing the mains-to-secondary isolation barrier on a medical PCB where the secondary side also connects to a patient-applied part, and how do you decide whether the barrier needs one or two means of patient protection?

**Answer:** The first step is to establish the classification of the applied part and the type of protection required, because that determines the barrier topology before any layout work begins. If the applied part is Type B, the patient connection needs to be isolated from mains by one means of patient protection (1×MOPP); if it is BF or CF, the requirement is two means (2×MOPP), and CF additionally tightens the leakage current limits. So the barrier design is driven by the applied part classification, not by convenience.

For a 2×MOPP barrier, I would treat it as two independent, individually compliant isolation systems in series — for example, a reinforced isolation transformer or isolated supply for the first means, and a second galvanic barrier (another transformer, optocouplers, or digital isolators with the appropriate rating) for the second. The key discipline is that each means must independently satisfy the creepage, clearance, and dielectric strength requirements, and a single fault must not defeat both. That means no shared creepage path, no shared slot that a single contamination event could bridge, and physical separation between the two barriers on the board.

On the PCB itself, I would work from the applicable creepage and clearance tables using the actual working voltage, pollution degree, and material group, and then add margin for the fact that the board will be coated, cleaned, and handled in production. I would define the barrier as an explicit keep-out zone in the CAD tool, with slots or routed barriers where the standard requires them, and I would review every net that crosses the barrier — including the ones people forget, like shield connections, LED indicators, test points, and programming headers. A programming header that bridges the barrier is a classic finding in a design review.

Verification is a separate design activity: I would plan dielectric strength (hi-pot) testing at the type-test voltage for each means, plus the appropriate patient leakage current measurements under normal and single-fault conditions, and I would make sure the test points and test fixturing are designed in rather than improvised at the lab.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design where the second means of protection has to pass a low-level analog signal, so an optocoupler's drift and nonlinearity are unacceptable? What would you do if a late change request adds a USB port on the secondary side that a user could connect to a mains-referenced laptop?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device's software needs Class A, B, or C under IEC 62304, and what would you do if the hardware and software leads disagreed on the classification?

**Answer:** The classification follows from the risk analysis, not from the team's intuition about how "important" the software feels. The question IEC 62304 asks is: could a software failure or latent defect contribute to a hazardous situation, and if so, what is the severity of the resulting harm? Class A means no injury is possible; Class B means non-serious injury is possible; Class C means death or serious injury is possible. So the correct process is to trace from the hazard analysis: identify which hazardous situations the software could contribute to, determine the severity of harm for each, and let the highest severity drive the classification of the software item or subsystem.

In practice I would do this at the architecture level rather than assigning one class to the whole product, because a device often has a Class C monitoring path and a Class A logging or UI path, and segregating them properly lets you apply the right rigor where it matters without over-engineering everything. The segregation has to be real — separate software items with defined interfaces, and ideally the lower-class item cannot corrupt or block the higher-class one.

When the hardware and software leads disagree, the disagreement is almost always about the hazard analysis rather than about the standard. So I would bring it back to the risk file: walk through the specific hazardous situations together, agree on the severity of harm, and let the classification fall out of that. If the disagreement persists, it usually means the risk analysis is incomplete or the architecture isn't yet clear about what the software can and cannot affect — which is itself a finding worth resolving before the classification is frozen. I would document the rationale either way, because an auditor will ask why the class was chosen, and "the team felt it was Class B" is not an answer.

**Possible follow-ups:** If a device monitors a physiological parameter and drives an alarm but delivers no therapy, how would you argue the classification? How would you handle a case where the software class changes mid-project because the risk analysis revealed a hazard that wasn't previously considered?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2 while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I would treat emissions and signal integrity as a single design problem rather than two sequential ones, because the filter that fixes one can easily break the other. The starting point is to understand where the noise is coming from and where it needs to go: switching power supplies, digital clocks and their harmonics, and any high-current loops are the usual suspects for conducted and radiated emissions, while the sensitive analog front-end is what you are trying to protect.

For conducted emissions on the mains input, the standard approach is a multi-stage filter — common-mode chokes plus X and Y capacitors — sized against the frequency range of interest and the impedance the device presents to the line. The important discipline is to keep the filter's return paths short and to place it at the entry point so that noise doesn't couple past it. For radiated emissions, the levers are loop area, cable routing, shielding, and clock edge control; slowing edges or spreading clocks can buy a lot of margin, but you have to check that the timing budget for the digital interfaces still closes.

The signal-integrity side is where the trade-off lives. A ferrite bead or an RC filter on an analog input reduces high-frequency noise but also adds series impedance and can interact with the source impedance to create a pole that shifts with temperature or part tolerance. So I would define the passband the signal actually needs, choose filter corner frequencies with margin above that, and verify the filter's effect on the measured accuracy, not just on the emissions plot. For differential or high-speed digital lines, I would be careful that common-mode chokes don't distort the differential signal or introduce skew.

The practical method is to model and pre-compliance test early — near-field probing and a spectrum analyzer before the formal lab visit — so that the filter values are tuned against real measurements rather than guessed. And I would keep the filter component footprints flexible on the first prototype so that values can be changed without a respin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a common-mode choke and a differential-mode filter for a particular emissions failure? What would you do if adding the filter that fixes emissions pushes your analog accuracy out of specification?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes calibration at the manufacturer but reads high in the field is the key clue — it points to something about the field condition, the installation, or the way the device is used that isn't reproduced on the bench. So the investigation should start by trying to reproduce the field condition rather than re-verifying the bench condition.

First I would gather the complaint data carefully: how many units, what the offset is, whether it's consistent across patients or sites, whether it correlates with a particular probe batch, a particular cleaning agent, a particular ambient temperature, or a particular way the probe is applied. A consistent offset across many units suggests a systematic design or calibration issue; a scattered offset suggests a unit-level or use-level issue.

Then I would look at the measurement chain end to end. A patient-contacting temperature sensor's reading depends on the sensor itself, the thermal path from the patient to the sensing element, the probe's contact pressure and placement, the cable and connector, and the electronics' cold-junction or reference compensation. A common cause of "reads high in the field, fine on the bench" is a thermal path issue — for example, the probe picking up heat from the patient's surroundings or from the device itself rather than from the contact point, or a change in contact pressure when applied by staff versus on a test fixture. Another common cause is a self-heating effect that only shows up under continuous operation, which the bench calibration (done quickly, at room temperature) wouldn't catch.

I would also check whether the reference thermometer itself is being used correctly — an offset between two measurement methods can come from either side — and whether the cleaning and disinfection protocol is affecting the probe's thermal properties or the connector.

The corrective action depends on the root cause: if it's a design issue in the thermal path, it may need a hardware change; if it's a use issue, it may need instructions-for-use changes or training; if it's a batch issue, it may need a supplier corrective action. Either way, the investigation should be documented as a formal complaint investigation with the rationale for the conclusion, because it feeds into post-market surveillance and potentially a field safety corrective action decision.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a bench test that reproduces the field thermal condition? If the root cause turns out to be a use error rather than a device fault, how does that change your corrective action?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I would treat this as a risk-acceptability discussion rather than a schedule argument, because the quality manager is raising a legitimate question and the team's "negligible" judgment needs to be defensible, not just asserted. The first step is to get the hazard and its risk estimate on the table explicitly: what is the hazardous situation, what is the severity of harm, what is the probability of occurrence, and what evidence supports that estimate? If the team's estimate is based on real data — field history, test results, a fault tree — then that evidence should be able to withstand scrutiny. If it's based on intuition, that's the gap to close.

I would also separate two questions that often get conflated: whether the risk is acceptable as-is, and whether a particular control measure is the right way to address it. Sometimes the quality manager is right that the risk needs attention but the proposed control is not the most efficient one; sometimes the team is right that the risk is low but the control is cheap enough that arguing about it costs more than implementing it. So I would ask what the control actually buys in risk reduction versus its cost and schedule impact, and whether there's a lower-cost control that achieves the same reduction.

If after that discussion there's still disagreement, I would escalate the decision rather than let it stall — this is a risk acceptance decision that belongs with the designated risk management authority, not with the engineering team alone. I would document both positions and the rationale for whatever is decided, because in an audit the question will be "why was this risk accepted or controlled," and a documented, evidence-based decision is defensible either way.

The one thing I would not do is let the schedule pressure silently override the risk discussion. If the control is genuinely needed, the schedule has to absorb it; if it isn't, the evidence should show that.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality manager's concern turns out to be valid but the proposed control would require a hardware respin? What would you do if the risk management authority sided with the quality manager and the schedule impact was unacceptable to the business?
