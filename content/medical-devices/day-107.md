# medical-devices — Day 107

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The measurement path has to be built around the standard's measurement network, not around convenience. IEC 60601-1 defines a specific patient leakage current measuring device — essentially a passive network with a defined input impedance and frequency response — and the measurement is only meaningful if that network is used, calibrated, and connected exactly as the standard prescribes. So the first design decision is to build or buy a proper measurement network and treat it as calibrated test equipment, not as a bench lash-up.

The second decision is topology. For patient leakage current, the measurement is made from the applied part, tied together, through the measuring device to earth. With mixed BF and CF applied parts on the same device, the applied parts are not interchangeable from a measurement standpoint: the CF part has a tighter limit and, in some configurations, a different fault-condition treatment. The practical approach is to measure each applied part individually and in combination, because the standard's intent is to capture the worst-case current that could flow through a patient who is simultaneously connected to more than one applied part. Measuring only one part at a time can miss a shared return path that only shows up when both are connected.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes from a structured read of the standard against the device's classification and intended use: normal condition and single-fault condition, AC and DC components where applicable, and the specific limits for BF versus CF. I'd build a measurement matrix — rows for each applied part and each combination, columns for normal/single-fault and AC/DC — and populate it from the clause text rather than from memory. Anything the matrix doesn't justify gets documented as not applicable with a reason, which is what an auditor will ask for.

The design implication is that the measurement path has to be physically accessible and repeatable. That means test points or a test fixture designed in from the start, isolation between the measurement network and the device under test, and a layout that keeps the measurement leads short and away from switching noise. If the measurement setup itself is noisy, you'll chase phantom failures.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a case where the combined-applied-part measurement exceeds the limit but each part individually passes? What would you do to keep the measurement repeatable across different test operators?

## Q2: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?

**Answer:** Traceability is a data model problem before it's a documentation problem. The approach I'd take is to assign stable, unique identifiers to every design input, every design output, every verification and validation activity, and every risk control measure, and then maintain the links as explicit relationships rather than as prose in a document. If the links live in a spreadsheet or a requirements tool with a real ID scheme, a change to one item propagates visibly; if they live in narrative text, they rot the moment the design moves.

The structure I'd aim for: design inputs trace forward to the design outputs that satisfy them and to the verification activities that demonstrate the output meets the input; design outputs trace to the risk management file where they serve as risk controls; and validation traces back to the user needs and intended use. The risk management file and the DHF are not separate universes — a risk control measure is a design output, and its verification is both a risk control verification and a design verification. Treating those as one link rather than two parallel documents is what keeps them consistent.

The hard part is change. When a design change lands, the traceability has to be updated in the same change control action, not afterward. I'd make the impact assessment part of the change record: which inputs, outputs, verifications, validations, and risk items are affected, and what re-verification or re-validation is required. If the change record can't answer that, the change isn't ready to be approved.

Practically, I'd keep the traceability matrix generated from the underlying data where possible rather than hand-maintained, review it at each design review as a standing agenda item, and do a completeness audit before each phase gate. The failure mode to avoid is the matrix that's assembled at the end from memory — it will be incomplete, and the gaps will be exactly where the design changed most.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design input that turns out to be unverifiable as written? What would you do if you inherited a project with a traceability matrix that was already badly out of date?

## Q3: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it points to resonance rather than to broad susceptibility. Something in the device is behaving as an antenna or a resonant structure at that frequency, and the energy is being coupled into a specific node rather than into the system generally.

The first step is to characterize the failure precisely before touching the design. What exactly malfunctions — a sensor reading, a communication link, a display, a reset? Does it recover when the field is removed, or does it latch? Does the failure depend on device orientation, cable position, or the presence of the enclosure? That characterization tells you whether you're looking at a conducted path (cable acting as an antenna, bringing energy to a connector), a radiated path into a slot or seam, or a resonance in a PCB structure or a component.

The second step is localization. I'd use a near-field probe sweep at the offending frequency to find where the field is actually coupling into the board, and compare that against the physical layout — a cable routed near a high-impedance node, a seam in the enclosure that's a quarter-wave at that frequency, a trace that's resonant with its return path. If the failure is in an analog front-end, I'd look at whether the offending frequency is landing in the passband of the signal chain or being rectified by a nonlinear junction somewhere. If it's a digital or communication failure, I'd look at the clock and data lines and whether the coupling is corrupting a specific transition.

The fix follows from the mechanism. If it's a cable coupling path, common-mode chokes, ferrites, and improved cable shielding and grounding at the connector are the usual levers. If it's an enclosure seam or slot, gasketing, seam overlap, and changing the slot dimensions to move the resonance away from the test frequency. If it's a PCB resonance or a trace acting as an antenna, layout changes — shorter traces, better return path, added series termination, local shielding — plus filtering at the affected node. The key discipline is to fix the coupling mechanism, not to blanket the board with capacitors and hope; a fix that works at one frequency by damping a resonance can create a new problem elsewhere.

I'd verify the fix by re-testing not just at the failing frequency but across the full band, because a resonant fix often shifts the problem rather than eliminating it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a resonance in the enclosure from a resonance on the PCB? If the fix requires a layout change and the test lab slot is already booked, how would you sequence the work?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The classification should follow from the hazard analysis, not from how the failure feels. The question isn't "is this failure bad?" but "can this failure, in combination with the device's intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse, lead to a hazardous situation that could result in harm?" That's a risk management question, and it should be answered by tracing the failure to a hazardous situation in the ISO 14971 analysis rather than by intuition in a review meeting.

The practical approach: take the failure, describe the sequence from failure to hazardous situation to possible harm, and see whether that sequence is credible. If the failure can cause the device to display a wrong physiological value that a clinician acts on, or to fail to alarm when an alarm condition exists, or to deliver or withhold therapy incorrectly, that's a safety-related failure and it needs a risk control with verification. If the failure only degrades convenience — a menu that takes an extra tap, a log that's harder to read, a battery indicator that's slightly pessimistic but still safe — it's a usability or reliability issue and belongs in a different process.

Two things complicate this. First, some failures are safety-related only in combination with another failure or with a particular use environment; those need to be analyzed as sequences, not as isolated events. Second, the boundary is not always obvious, and the honest answer in a borderline case is to treat it as safety-related until the analysis shows otherwise — the cost of over-classifying is documentation effort, and the cost of under-classifying is a missed risk control.

I'd also make sure the classification is documented with its rationale, because the reviewer's question will be "why did you decide this was not safety-related?" and "because it seemed minor" is not an answer that survives an audit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a disagreement between the software lead and the risk management lead on a borderline classification? What evidence would you want before accepting a "not safety-related" conclusion?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a project where the clinical team has requested a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the clinical need from the proposed solution. The clinical team has identified a real problem with how the device works in their workflow; the specific change they've proposed is one way to solve it, and it may not be the only one. So before accepting the schedule impact, I'd want to understand what the underlying usability problem is, how often it occurs, what the consequence is when it occurs, and whether there's a lower-cost way to address it — a firmware change, a labeling or training change, a workflow accommodation, or a smaller mechanical modification that doesn't require a full hardware revision.

The second thing is to quantify the actual impact honestly, not defensively. What does the hardware revision actually cost in schedule — layout, fabrication, re-verification, re-validation, and the regulatory submission date? Is the submission date a hard external constraint or an internal target? What's the risk of shipping without the change — is this a usability issue that could generate complaints or a use error that could reach the hazard analysis? I'd bring that assessment back to the clinical team and the project leadership as a decision, not as a refusal.

The third thing is to make sure the decision is made at the right level. A late usability change with a multi-month regulatory impact is a business and clinical risk decision, not an engineering one. My job as lead engineer is to make the trade-off legible — here's what the change costs, here's what not making it costs, here's the risk of each path — and then to make sure the people accountable for the product decision have the information to make it. If the decision is to proceed, I'd want the change controlled properly: a formal change request, an impact assessment against the DHF and risk file, and a revised plan that reflects the new verification and validation work.

What I'd avoid is either silently absorbing the change and letting the schedule slip without a decision, or refusing it on schedule grounds without understanding the clinical consequence. Both are failures of the lead engineer's job, which is to make the trade-off visible and then execute the decision well.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the clinical team's request turns out to be driven by a use error that should have been caught in formative usability testing? What would you do if leadership decides to proceed but doesn't want to move the submission date?
