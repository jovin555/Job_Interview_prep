# medical-devices — Day 115

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The measurement path has to be built around the standard's measurement network, not around convenience. IEC 60601-1 defines a specific patient leakage current measuring device — essentially a low-impedance resistive network with a defined frequency response — and the measurement is only meaningful if you use that network, connect it between the applied part and earth (or between applied parts, depending on the configuration), and apply the correct supply conditions for each test case. So the first design decision is physical: build a dedicated test fixture that presents the correct network at the correct point, with short, well-controlled leads, so that the measurement isn't contaminated by the fixture itself.

For a device with both BF and CF applied parts, the key insight is that the classifications are not interchangeable. CF parts have a tighter limit because they're intended for direct cardiac contact, and the standard treats them separately — you cannot average or lump them. I'd enumerate the applied parts, classify each one, and then build a matrix of test cases: each applied part measured individually against earth, applied parts measured against each other where the standard requires it, and each case run under normal condition and under the applicable single-fault conditions (e.g., earth open, one means of protection defeated). The matrix is what tells you which measurements are actually required — it's driven by the classification and the number of applied parts, not by a generic checklist.

Practically, the measurement path design matters because leakage current is small and the test environment is noisy. I'd keep the measurement return separate from chassis return, avoid creating inadvertent parallel paths through the fixture or the device under test's own grounding, and verify the fixture itself with a known current source before trusting any reading. If a reading comes in near the limit, the first question is whether it's real or an artifact of the measurement setup — a stray capacitance to earth or a shared ground in the fixture can easily dominate a microamp-level measurement.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a given applied part is BF or CF in the first place, and who owns that decision? What would you do if a CF applied part measured just under the limit but the margin felt uncomfortably thin?

## Q2: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a disagreement about the risk estimate, not about whether the quality manager gets to have an opinion — because under ISO 14971 the risk management process is supposed to be the arbiter, and if two functions disagree about whether a risk is negligible, that usually means the risk file isn't doing its job. So the first move is to go back to the hazard analysis together and look at what the actual estimate is built on: severity, probability, and the detectability/controllability assumptions. Often the engineering team's "negligible" is really "low probability given our assumptions about use," and the quality manager's concern is really "those assumptions aren't documented or aren't robust." Making the assumptions explicit frequently resolves the disagreement in one direction or the other.

If after that the estimates still diverge, I'd want to understand what's driving the quality manager's position — sometimes it's a regulatory precedent, sometimes it's a post-market signal from a similar device, sometimes it's a conservative reading of the standard. That context matters because a risk control measure that looks like overkill from a pure engineering standpoint may be cheap insurance against a regulatory question later. I'd also separate the two questions that tend to get conflated: whether the control is *justified* and whether it's *affordable on the current schedule*. Those are different decisions and should be made separately.

On the schedule side, I'd look for the least disruptive way to implement the control — sometimes the same risk reduction can be achieved in firmware or in the instructions for use rather than a hardware change, and sometimes it can be staged so the submission isn't blocked. If the control genuinely requires a hardware revision and the schedule impact is real, that's a decision for the project leadership with the trade-off laid out clearly, not something I'd unilaterally overrule. What I would not do is let the disagreement sit unresolved or quietly drop the item — that's how risk files end up with gaps that surface in an audit.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the quality manager's concern turns out to be based on a misreading of the standard — how do you push back without it becoming a territorial fight? How would you document the resolution so it's defensible later?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** The core tension is that emissions filtering and signal integrity both want to control the same high-frequency energy, but for opposite reasons — the filter wants to attenuate it before it leaves the board, and the signal wants to preserve it. So the strategy has to be layered by frequency and by where the energy is allowed to flow, rather than solved with a single filter.

I'd start at the source. The most effective emissions control is reducing the noise generated in the first place: choosing switching edges and clock rates that aren't unnecessarily fast, keeping high-di/dt loops small, and returning currents on a continuous reference plane so the loop area is minimized. A layout that keeps the return path tight does more for radiated emissions than any filter you can add afterward, and it costs nothing in signal integrity.

Then I'd separate the problem into conducted and radiated. For conducted emissions, the filter sits at the power input and at any cable interface — a common-mode choke plus X and Y capacitors sized against the applicable limits, with the Y-capacitor value constrained by leakage current requirements, which is a real medical-device-specific tension because a bigger Y-cap helps emissions but hurts patient/earth leakage. That constraint often pushes you toward a better choke and better layout rather than more capacitance. For radiated emissions, the dominant paths are usually cables acting as antennas and slots/enclosure openings, so cable termination, common-mode chokes on interface cables, and enclosure seam treatment matter more than board-level filtering.

For signal integrity, the rule is that filters go where they don't compromise the signal: on power rails, on slow or low-bandwidth interfaces, and at cable entry points. High-speed differential pairs get their integrity from impedance control and reference-plane continuity, not from series ferrites. Where a filter is unavoidable on a signal line — say a low-frequency analog input — I'd choose a component with a defined, characterized response and verify the filter's effect on the signal's amplitude and phase against the accuracy budget, not just assume it's transparent.

The verification loop is the important part: pre-scan early with a near-field probe and a spectrum analyzer so you're fixing the actual emissions mechanism rather than guessing, and re-check signal integrity after every filter change, because a filter that fixes emissions by degrading a sensor reading is not a fix.

**Possible follow-ups:** How does the leakage current limit on Y-capacitors change your filter topology compared to a non-medical design? What would you do if a filter that fixes conducted emissions measurably degrades an analog channel's accuracy?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means a software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation with unacceptable risk, so the verification burden is about demonstrating that the software is correct by design and by test, with evidence at every level — not just "it passed system test." I'd structure the approach around the standard's required activities and make sure each one produces an artifact that's traceable.

The foundation is the software requirements specification: requirements have to be granular, verifiable, and traceable to the system requirements and to the risk controls. For Class C, the standard expects the architecture to support detailed design and to address the safety-related requirements explicitly — so I'd want an architecture description that shows how the safety-critical functions are isolated, how they're protected (watchdogs, memory protection, redundancy where the risk analysis calls for it), and how failures are detected and handled. The risk control measures identified in the ISO 14971 file have to map onto specific software requirements, or the chain breaks.

Verification then runs at each level: unit verification against the detailed design, integration testing against the architecture, and system testing against the software requirements. For Class C, the standard expects unit-level verification to be rigorous — that means testing at the function level with coverage analysis, and where the risk analysis justifies it, structural coverage (statement, branch, and for the highest integrity, MC/DC) with documented justification for any coverage that isn't achieved. I'd also want static analysis and coding-standard compliance as part of the evidence, because they catch classes of defects that testing misses.

The part people underestimate is the process evidence: problem reports and their resolution, the configuration management record showing exactly what was tested, and the traceability matrix that lets an auditor walk from a risk control to a requirement to a test to a result. For Class C, the verification isn't complete until that chain is closed and the anomalies found during testing are either resolved or formally assessed for their effect on safety.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you justify a decision not to achieve full MC/DC coverage on a particular module? What's the difference between verifying a Class C requirement and validating it, and where does each fit?

## Q5: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?

**Answer:** The mistake I'd avoid is treating traceability as a document produced at the end. If it's assembled retrospectively, it's always incomplete and it always has gaps that are hard to defend. So the approach is to make traceability a byproduct of the work: every design input gets an identifier when it's created, every design output references the inputs it satisfies, every verification test references the output it verifies, and every validation activity references the user needs it addresses. If those links are created as the artifacts are created, the matrix is just a view of data that already exists.

Structurally, I'd anchor the whole thing on the design inputs, because they're the contract. User needs and intended use flow into design inputs; design inputs flow into design outputs (requirements, drawings, software specs); design outputs flow into verification; and user needs flow into validation. The risk management file sits alongside and cross-references both — risk controls become design inputs, and their verification is both a design-verification activity and a risk-control-verification activity. Keeping those two views linked is what makes the file coherent rather than two parallel paper trails.

The hard part is change. When a design input changes, the traceability has to show what downstream artifacts were affected and how they were re-verified — that's what a change impact analysis is for, and it should be a required step in the change control process, not an afterthought. I'd want the tooling to make this visible: a query that shows everything downstream of a changed input, so the impact assessment is based on the actual links rather than memory. If the tooling can't do that, a disciplined spreadsheet with unique IDs and a change log can work, but it needs the same discipline.

Finally, I'd build in periodic traceability reviews — not just at design reviews, but as a standing check that every input has an output, every output has verification, and every user need has validation coverage. Gaps found early are cheap to close; gaps found during an audit or a submission review are not.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design input that turns out to be unverifiable as written — do you change the input or the test? What role does the traceability matrix play in a design review, versus in the DHF itself?
