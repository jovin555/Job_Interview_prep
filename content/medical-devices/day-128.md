# medical-devices — Day 128

## Q1: How would you approach designing a patient-connected front-end where the applied part must be defibrillation-proof, and what does that requirement actually change about the circuit compared to a standard BF-type input?

**Answer:** I'd start by being precise about what "defibrillation-proof" means functionally: the applied part must survive a defibrillation discharge without the patient coming into contact with hazardous energy, and the device must recover to its specified performance afterward. That drives three design threads. First, the energy path — the defib pulse is a large, short-duration surge, so I'd design a clamping and current-limiting network at the patient connection that shunts the bulk of the energy away from sensitive circuitry and limits what reaches the isolation barrier. Second, the isolation barrier itself has to be rated for the defib working voltage and the transient, not just the normal mains-derived working voltage, which typically pushes toward a higher creepage/clearance and a barrier component (transformer, digital isolator, or optocoupler) explicitly rated for defib-proof applications. Third, recovery behavior — after the pulse, the front-end must come back to its accuracy spec without a power cycle, so I'd avoid protection schemes that latch or that leave the amplifier saturated for a long time, and I'd verify recovery time as an explicit test. Compared to a standard BF input, the key differences are the surge-rated barrier, the energy-handling protection network, and the requirement to demonstrate post-pulse performance rather than just survival. I'd also make sure the protection network's own leakage and capacitance don't degrade the measurement accuracy or the leakage current budget — those two constraints often fight each other, so it's a real trade study, not a bolt-on.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify post-pulse recovery — what would the test actually measure, and over what time window? If the protection network adds capacitance at the input, how does that interact with your leakage current and accuracy budgets?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device's alarm system needs to be treated as a separate risk control with its own verification, versus being covered as part of the normal functional verification?

**Answer:** I'd treat the alarm system as a risk control whenever it's the mitigation for a hazardous situation — for example, if the alarm is what prevents a clinician from missing a desaturation or an apnea event, then the alarm *is* the risk control, and ISO 14971 requires that risk control be verified for its effectiveness, not just its function. So the reasoning is: trace each hazard in the risk file to its control, and if the control is "the device alarms," the alarm pathway gets its own verification evidence — priority behavior, audibility, visual distinctness, latency, and behavior under single-fault conditions like a failed transducer or a muted speaker. If the alarm is purely informational and no hazard depends on it, it can live under normal functional verification. The practical consequence is that the alarm verification has to test the *hazard-relevant* properties, not just "does it beep" — for instance, that a single component failure can't silently disable both the audible and visual pathways, and that alarm conditions are still annunciated when the device is in a paused or suspended state. I'd also make sure the alarm verification is linked back to the risk file so an auditor can follow hazard → control → verification evidence in one line.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure the single-fault analysis for the alarm pathway — what failure modes would you enumerate first? If a clinician can silence an alarm, how does that interact with the risk control claim?

## Q3: How would you approach the design transfer of a medical device from R&D to manufacturing so that production can build and test it repeatably without R&D involvement on every unit?

**Answer:** I'd think of design transfer as producing a manufacturing *package* that is complete enough that a trained operator who has never met the design team can build, test, and disposition a unit. That means: a released BOM with approved manufacturer part numbers and approved alternates, assembly drawings and work instructions with the critical process parameters called out, a test specification that distinguishes design verification tests (done once, in R&D) from production tests (done on every unit), and acceptance criteria with clear pass/fail limits and measurement uncertainty considered. I'd pay particular attention to the boundary between what must be tested 100% versus what can be sampled or covered by process validation — for a medical device, safety-critical characteristics like isolation integrity and leakage current usually warrant 100% test or a validated process with statistical justification. I'd also run a pilot build with manufacturing engineering present, capture every question they ask, and fold the answers back into the work instructions — those questions are the gaps in the transfer package. Finally, I'd define the change-control path so that when manufacturing finds an issue, it routes back through the design change process rather than being worked around on the floor, and I'd make sure the DHF records the transfer evidence (first article inspection, process capability, training records) so the transfer is auditable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide which characteristics get 100% production test versus process validation? What would you do if the pilot build reveals a test that R&D specified but manufacturing can't perform repeatably?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a complaint where a device's displayed value is plausible but clinicians report it doesn't match their clinical impression, and the device passes all its bench accuracy checks when returned?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes bench checks is itself the most useful clue — it tells me the failure is either environmental, use-related, or intermittent in a way the bench doesn't reproduce. I'd structure the investigation in layers. First, characterize the complaint: is it one unit, one site, one patient population, one use scenario? That tells me whether to look at a design issue, a manufacturing lot issue, or a use issue. Second, look at what's different between the bench and the field — the bench typically uses a calibrated simulator in a controlled environment, while the field has real sensors, real patient contact, real ambient conditions, and real user technique. So I'd examine the sensor interface and the patient-contact conditions: contact quality, cable and connector wear, cleaning residue, ambient temperature and humidity, and whether the accessory in use is the one the device was validated with. Third, I'd pull any logged data the device records — raw sensor values, not just the processed display — because the raw data tells me whether the discrepancy originates in the sensing chain or in the processing/display chain. Fourth, I'd try to reproduce with the actual returned unit in the actual use configuration, not the bench configuration. Throughout, I'd keep the complaint in the CAPA system and make sure the investigation is documented against the risk file, because if the discrepancy could lead to a misinformed clinical decision, it's a potential safety issue and may need a health hazard evaluation before I have a root cause.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the raw logged data matches the display, where does that point the investigation? How would you decide whether this warrants a field safety corrective action versus a design change in the next revision?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and a supplier notifies you mid-project that a critical analog component is being discontinued, and the recommended replacement has a different package and a different specified accuracy. How would you handle it?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a change-control problem first and an engineering problem second, because the regulatory and verification consequences are what usually get underestimated. Step one is to characterize the delta precisely: not just "different accuracy," but how the new part's error budget, drift, noise, and temperature behavior compare to the original across the actual operating range the device is specified for. If the new part's worst-case error consumes margin the design doesn't have, that's a design change, not a drop-in. Step two is to assess the physical change — a different package means layout changes, which means a board revision, which means re-verification of anything the layout touches, including EMC and isolation if the part sits near a barrier. Step three is to bring quality and regulatory in early, because a component change on a cleared or submitted device may trigger a design change assessment and potentially a new submission depending on how the change affects safety and performance. Step four is to look for alternatives before accepting the recommended replacement — a second-source part, a last-time-buy of the original to cover the current build, or a redesign that's cleaner than forcing the replacement in. I'd also want the supplier's written discontinuance notice and timeline in the file, and I'd make the decision with the full cost of re-verification on the table rather than optimizing only for BOM continuity.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a last-time-buy and a redesign — what factors would drive that? If the replacement is pin-compatible but has worse drift, what verification would you insist on before accepting it?
