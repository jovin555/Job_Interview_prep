# medical-devices — Day 97

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** Start from the standard's measurement topology rather than from the schematic. IEC 60601-1 defines leakage current as flowing through a defined measuring device (the MD) connected between specified points — the applied part and earth, the applied part and mains, or across the patient — under both normal condition (NC) and single-fault condition (SFC). The first step is to build a measurement matrix: rows are the applied parts (each BF part, each CF part, and any combination that can be simultaneously connected to a patient), columns are the measurement configurations the standard requires for that classification, and cells are the applicable limit. BF and CF have different limits and, importantly, CF parts have tighter limits because they are intended for direct cardiac contact, so a device with both types cannot be tested as if it were homogeneous — each part is measured against its own classification, and any part-to-part configuration where current could flow between a BF and a CF part through the patient needs its own entry in the matrix.

For the physical measurement path, the key design decisions are: a dedicated, short, low-impedance return from each applied part's patient-connection point to a test access point on the PCB or a test fixture, so the MD can be inserted without disturbing the circuit; keeping that access point on the patient side of the isolation barrier, not the mains side; and ensuring the fixture itself doesn't add stray capacitance that inflates readings at 50/60 Hz or during the high-frequency component of the measurement. I would also define which measurements are *not* required and document the rationale — for example, if two applied parts are internally connected and cannot be separated in use, the standard may treat them as one, but that has to be justified in the test plan, not assumed.

Deciding what's actually required comes from three inputs: the applied part classification in the risk analysis and labeling, the patient-connection diagram (which parts can touch the patient simultaneously), and the particular standard if one applies, since some IEC 60601-2 standards add or modify leakage requirements. I would produce a traceability table mapping each required measurement to its clause, limit, and test condition, and have it reviewed by the safety engineer before the lab sees the device — discovering a missing configuration at the test house is expensive.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where a BF part and a CF part are physically adjacent and could be bridged by conductive gel or fluid in use? What would you do if a measured value sits just under the limit but the lab's fixture gives a slightly different reading than yours?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted emissions and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** Treat it as a single allocation problem, not two separate fixes. Conducted and radiated emissions are coupled: a filter that suppresses differential-mode noise on a cable can convert it to common-mode if the layout is asymmetric, and a common-mode choke that fixes a radiated problem can degrade a differential signal's eye if its impedance is wrong for the data rate. So the first step is to characterize the noise sources and their paths — switching power supplies (both the main converter and any point-of-load regulators), digital clocks and their harmonics, and cable-borne common-mode currents driven by ground bounce — and separate them into differential-mode and common-mode components, because the filter topologies differ.

For conducted emissions on the mains input, the standard approach is a two-stage filter: a differential-mode stage (X-capacitors plus a differential inductor) sized for the switching frequency and its low harmonics, and a common-mode stage (Y-capacitors plus a common-mode choke) sized for the higher-frequency content. The Y-capacitor value is constrained by earth leakage current limits under IEC 60601-1, so the leakage budget and the EMC filter budget have to be negotiated together — this is where medical designs differ most from commercial ones. For radiated emissions, the levers are usually layout and shielding before filtering: minimizing loop areas on switching nodes, keeping high-di/dt traces short and over a continuous reference plane, and controlling cable exit points, since cables are often the dominant antenna.

For signal integrity, the constraint is that any filter in a signal path must be transparent at the signal's bandwidth. That means choosing filter corner frequencies with margin, using common-mode chokes with specified differential impedance at the data rate, and verifying with eye diagrams or BER measurements, not just with a spectrum analyzer. I would allocate an EMC budget early — how much attenuation is needed at which frequencies — and verify each subsystem against its allocation in pre-compliance rather than waiting for the full system.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a ferrite bead or a common-mode choke is the right part for a given interface? What would you do if the filter that fixes emissions also pushes a signal just outside its timing margin?

## Q3: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** Start from the device's intended use and clinical claims, not from its bill of materials. The IEC 60601-2 series is organized by device type, and applicability is driven by what the device does and how it is used, so the first step is to write down each clinical function separately and search the particular-standard catalog for each one. A combined device will often map to two or more particular standards, and the standard itself anticipates this: where a device falls under more than one particular standard, each applies to the relevant function, and the manufacturer has to demonstrate compliance with all of them.

The harder question is conflicts. Particular standards can modify, replace, or add to clauses of the general standard — for example, changing a leakage limit, adding a test, or redefining an applied part classification for that device type. When two particular standards appear to conflict, the resolution path is: first, check whether the conflict is real or apparent (often one standard's requirement is a specialization that the other doesn't address, so both can be met); second, check the scope and normative references of each to see which takes precedence for the specific clause; third, if a genuine conflict remains, document the interpretation and the rationale in the compliance matrix and, where the standard allows, seek clarification from the test lab or a notified body before testing rather than after. I would not silently pick one.

Practically, I would build a clause-by-clause compliance matrix with columns for the general standard, each applicable particular standard, and the device's evidence, flagging every cell where a particular standard modifies the general one. That matrix becomes the test plan's backbone and the reviewer's map.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device whose therapy function is outside your team's core expertise — would you bring in a specialist, and at what point? What documentation would you keep to justify an interpretation where two standards seemed to conflict?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file should be a living index, not a binder. I would structure it around the ISO 14971 process rather than around document types: a risk management plan at the start (scope, responsibilities, risk acceptability criteria, how production and post-production information feeds back), a hazard analysis that grows with the design, a risk evaluation and control record, and a risk management report at the end that confirms the plan was executed and residual risk is acceptable. The key structural decision is to make each hazard traceable in both directions — forward from hazard to risk control to verification evidence, and backward from every design decision to the hazard it addresses — so that when the design changes, the affected entries are obvious.

To keep it useful rather than ceremonial, I would tie the risk file to the design history file's change process: any design change triggers a review of the hazard analysis, and the review is recorded, even if the conclusion is "no new hazard." I would also keep the risk file's granularity matched to how the team actually thinks — a hazard entry per credible failure mode or use error, with the control and its verification linked, rather than one giant spreadsheet row per subsystem. And I would involve the people who will actually use the file (test engineers, quality, clinical) in reviewing it at design reviews, so it reflects real understanding rather than being written to satisfy an auditor.

The audit test I would apply to myself: pick any risk control in the file and ask whether I can produce, in under a minute, the hazard it addresses, the verification evidence, and the residual risk evaluation. If not, the structure is wrong.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard that is identified late, after design freeze, without derailing the schedule? What role should production and post-market data play in keeping the file current?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I would treat the disagreement as a question about the risk acceptability criteria, not about whose judgment is better. The first move is to go back to the risk management plan and check what criteria were agreed at the start — if the hazard's estimated risk clearly falls below the agreed acceptability threshold, that's the basis for the discussion; if the criteria are ambiguous or the estimate is uncertain, that's the real problem to fix. I would ask the quality manager to articulate the specific concern: is it that the severity estimate is too low, that the probability estimate lacks data, or that the hazard could combine with another failure to produce a worse outcome? Often the disagreement dissolves once the estimate is examined, because one side is reasoning from a different severity assumption.

If the concern is substantive — for example, the hazard is low-probability but high-severity, or the team's probability estimate is based on assumption rather than data — then the control is probably justified regardless of schedule, and I would say so and work the schedule problem separately: can the control be implemented in a way that doesn't require a full hardware revision, can it be staged, what's the actual critical path impact rather than the feared one. If the concern is not substantive and the criteria are clear, I would document the disagreement, the rationale, and the decision in the risk file, and escalate to the design review board or the appropriate management level rather than letting it become a private argument. What I would not do is dismiss the quality manager's concern on schedule grounds alone, because that's exactly the reasoning that produces field problems — and the decision record matters as much as the decision.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's concern turned out to be valid but the control would delay a regulatory submission by months? How would you make sure the team doesn't treat risk reviews as a box-ticking exercise after a disagreement like this?
