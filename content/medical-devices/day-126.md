# medical-devices — Day 126

## Q1: How would you approach designing the overvoltage and overcurrent protection for a patient-connected sensor input, where the protection network itself must not degrade the measurement accuracy the device is specified to meet?
**Answer:** The core tension is that every protection element you add — series resistance, clamp diodes, TVS devices, ferrite beads — sits in the signal path and contributes leakage, capacitance, noise, or thermal drift, so the design has to start from the accuracy budget rather than from a protection checklist. I'd first quantify how much error the measurement chain can absorb: input offset, input bias current, source impedance tolerance, and the resolution of the ADC all set a ceiling on what the protection network is allowed to contribute. Then I'd choose the protection topology to fit inside that ceiling. For a high-impedance sensor, series resistance is the cheapest current limiter but it forms a divider with the amplifier's input impedance and adds Johnson noise, so its value is bounded from both ends. Clamp diodes need low leakage at the maximum expected signal voltage, and their junction capacitance has to be small relative to the source impedance at the frequencies of interest, otherwise it forms a low-pass pole that shifts with temperature and bias. TVS devices are coarser — they're there for the transient energy, not the steady-state signal — so I'd place them where their capacitance is buffered from the sensitive node, often ahead of a series element. I'd also think about the failure mode: a protection component that fails short should fail in a way that's detectable, not one that silently biases the reading. Finally, I'd verify the whole chain against the accuracy specification with the protection in place, not with it bypassed, and I'd test at the extremes of the transient and steady-state envelope, because protection behavior at the edges is where accuracy claims usually break down. The design is only done when the protection network is transparent to the measurement under normal conditions and effective under fault conditions.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a resettable fuse or a one-time fuse is appropriate on a patient-connected input, given the serviceability and single-fault requirements? What test would you run to confirm the protection network doesn't shift the measurement's temperature coefficient?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device's software needs Class A, B, or C under IEC 62304 when the device monitors a physiological parameter and drives an alarm, but does not deliver therapy — and how would you handle a disagreement between the hardware and software leads on the classification?
**Answer:** The classification isn't a property of the software in isolation — it flows from the risk analysis. The starting point is the hazard analysis: for each software-contributed hazardous situation, what's the severity of the resulting harm, and could a software failure initiate that hazard or fail to mitigate it? If a software failure can contribute to a hazardous situation with serious injury or death as the plausible outcome, that drives toward Class C; if the hazardous situation is non-serious, Class B; if no hazardous situation can arise from software failure, Class A. For a monitoring device with alarms, the key question is whether the alarm function is itself a risk control — if the alarm is the mitigation that prevents a hazardous situation, then a failure of the alarm path is a failure of a risk control, which pushes the classification up. That's the crux of most disagreements: one lead is thinking about the primary monitoring function, the other about the alarm as a safety mechanism, and they're each right about their own piece. My approach to resolving the disagreement is to make the argument concrete rather than abstract — walk both leads through the hazard analysis line by line, identify which software items are implicated in each hazardous situation, and let the severity drive the classification per software item rather than per system. IEC 62304 allows different classes for different software items, so a system-level argument that forces a single class is often the wrong frame. If the two leads still disagree after that, I'd escalate to the risk management owner and document the rationale in the risk management file, because the classification decision needs to be defensible to a reviewer, not just internally agreed. The worst outcome is picking the lower class to save documentation effort — that decision has a way of surfacing during audit.
**Possible follow-ups:** If the alarm is classified as a risk control, what does that imply for how you verify the alarm software? How would you document a per-software-item classification in a way that a reviewer can follow without re-deriving the whole hazard analysis?

## Q3: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?
**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue — it usually means something in the device is resonant or is being demodulated at that frequency, rather than a broadband susceptibility problem. The first thing I'd do is characterize the failure precisely: is the malfunction threshold-dependent (does it appear only above a certain field strength), is it modulated by the test signal's modulation envelope, and does it correlate with a specific device state (a particular sensor active, a particular display update, a particular communication burst)? That characterization often points at the mechanism before any hardware work. Then I'd think about the two common mechanisms. The first is resonance: a cable, a trace, or an enclosure slot is acting as an antenna at that frequency, coupling energy into a sensitive node. The fix is usually to change the resonant structure — add a ferrite or common-mode choke on the offending cable, change cable routing or length, add a ground stitch or shield termination, or adjust the enclosure seam. The second is demodulation: a semiconductor junction somewhere — an ESD diode, an op-amp input, a regulator — is rectifying the RF and producing a low-frequency offset or a logic glitch. That's harder to find because the failure appears at the device's functional level, not at the RF level. I'd use a near-field probe to localize where the RF is actually entering the board, and I'd try targeted mitigations one at a time — a temporary shield over a suspect region, a bypass capacitor at a specific node — to confirm the coupling path before committing to a fix. The resolution should be a design change that addresses the coupling path, not a test-only workaround like a different cable arrangement that won't hold in the field. And I'd re-run the full immunity sweep afterward, because a fix at one frequency can shift a resonance to another.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a resonance problem from a demodulation problem experimentally, without a full EM simulation? If the fix requires a board respin, what interim evidence would you gather to show the design change will resolve the issue before the respin is tested?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?
**Answer:** The failure mode I'd design against is the risk file that's written backward from the conclusion — a tidy document produced after the design is frozen, listing hazards that were already mitigated and risks that were already accepted. That file is auditable in form but useless in substance, and it tends to fall apart under a reviewer who asks "when was this hazard identified, and what changed as a result?" The structure I'd aim for keeps the file live by tying it to the design process at fixed points. Hazard identification starts early, from the intended use and the use environment, and it's revisited at each design review — not as a formality but with a specific question: what new hazards has this design decision introduced, and what hazards has it closed? Each risk control gets a verification record that points to the specific test or analysis that demonstrates it works, and that record is created when the verification happens, not reconstructed later. Residual risk acceptance is a decision with a named owner and a date, and it's revisited if the design changes. The file's index should let a reviewer trace from any hazard to its risk controls to their verification to the residual risk decision, and from any design change back to the hazards it touched. The practical discipline that keeps this working is treating the risk file as a design input, not a design output — if a design review can't proceed without an updated risk assessment, the file stays current by construction. I'd also keep a change log in the file itself, so the history of what was considered and rejected is visible, because that history is often what a reviewer actually wants to see.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard that's identified late in the project, after the design is largely frozen — what does the risk file need to show about how it was handled? What's the minimum content of a risk control verification record that makes it defensible without duplicating the test report?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the two questions that are getting conflated: is the hazard's risk actually negligible, and is the proposed control the right response if it isn't? The engineering team's "negligible" is a risk-estimation judgment, and the quality manager's insistence is often a signal that the estimation isn't well documented — if the risk file shows the severity, probability, and detectability with a clear rationale, the disagreement usually resolves itself one way or the other. So I'd start by asking for the specific risk estimate and the evidence behind it, and I'd ask the quality manager what concern is driving the request. Sometimes the concern is legitimate but the proposed control is a blunt instrument — there may be a cheaper control, a design change, or an information-for-safety measure that addresses the same hazard at a fraction of the cost. That's the conversation I'd want to have before accepting either "add it" or "don't." If the risk genuinely is negligible and the file supports that, I'd document the disagreement and the rationale rather than silently overruling — the quality manager's signature on the risk acceptance matters, and a documented dissent is healthier than a forced consensus. If the risk estimate is actually uncertain, I'd treat that uncertainty as the real problem and resolve it with data — a test, a literature review, a clinician's input — rather than arguing from intuition. On schedule, I'd be honest about the impact and let the decision-maker weigh it, but I wouldn't let schedule pressure drive a risk decision that the file can't defend later. The one thing I'd avoid is treating this as a turf dispute; the quality manager is a stakeholder in the same outcome, and the goal is a defensible risk file, not a win.
**Possible follow-ups:** If the quality manager's concern turns out to be valid but the proposed control is too expensive, how would you go about finding an alternative control that satisfies both of you? How would you document a risk acceptance where the engineering team and quality function disagreed, so that it holds up under audit?
