# medical-devices — Day 91

## Q1: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware correctly handles a situation where the device's non-volatile memory becomes corrupted during normal operation, given that the device must continue monitoring and displaying physiological data in real time?

**Answer:** The core principle is that the monitoring function must never depend on the integrity of the logging subsystem — corruption of stored data is a data-integrity problem, not a patient-safety problem, and the architecture should enforce that separation. I'd start by defining what "corrupted" means and how it's detected: per-record checksums or CRCs, a structured header with a magic number and version, and a write-then-verify pattern so a record is only marked valid once it's fully committed. On detection, the firmware should quarantine the bad region rather than halt — mark the block unusable, log the event to a separate diagnostic area, and continue writing to the next valid region. The real-time display path should be fed from RAM, not from flash, so a storage fault can't stall the monitoring loop; the logging task runs at lower priority and its failure must be contained. I'd also design for graceful degradation: if the filesystem or wear-leveling layer can't recover a region, the device should surface a service indication (not a clinical alarm unless the risk file says otherwise) and keep monitoring. Verification-wise, I'd build fault injection into the test strategy — deliberately corrupt headers, flip bits in records, simulate a mid-write power loss, and simulate a flash block going bad — and confirm the device keeps displaying live data, flags the storage fault, and recovers cleanly on the next boot. Finally, I'd trace this back to the risk file: what hazard does lost or corrupted logging create, and does the mitigation (detection + quarantine + indication) reduce it to acceptable residual risk?

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a single corrupted record from a systematically failing flash region, and would the response differ? Where would you draw the line between logging a storage fault silently versus alerting the clinician?

## Q2: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I'd start from the intended use and the device's clinical function, not from a keyword search of the standard catalog. The classification drives everything: what is the device's primary function, what applied parts does it have, what is the patient population, and where is it used. From there I'd map each function to its likely particular standard — monitoring functions to the relevant monitoring standard, therapy delivery to the relevant therapy standard — and confirm applicability against each standard's scope clause, because scope clauses are where combination devices get explicitly included or excluded. For a combination device, the honest answer is often that more than one particular standard applies, and each one must be satisfied for its respective function. On conflicts, the general standard sets the baseline and the particular standard takes precedence where it's more specific — that's the hierarchy the 60601 family is built on. But "conflict" is usually the wrong frame: more often the particular standard adds requirements or tightens a limit rather than contradicting the general one. Where a genuine tension exists, I'd document the interpretation, get it reviewed by regulatory/quality, and where ambiguity remains, consider a pre-submission or standards-committee interpretation rather than guessing. The output of this exercise should be a compliance matrix — clause by clause, general and particular, with the evidence that satisfies each — because that matrix becomes the backbone of the test plan and the DHF.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a particular standard that was written before the combination use case existed and doesn't cleanly address it? Who in the organization should own the applicability decision, and how do you document it?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that has to pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** The key insight is that emissions filtering and signal integrity are usually pulling in the same direction if you get the topology right — the conflict only appears when filtering is added late as a band-aid. I'd work in layers. First, source suppression: slow down the fastest edges that don't need to be fast (series termination, slew-rate control, spread-spectrum clocks where the standard permits), because the cheapest way to reduce emissions is to stop generating them. Second, keep the noise currents local: proper return-path design, solid reference planes, and decoupling that gives high-frequency return current a short loop — most "filtering problems" are actually layout problems. Third, filter at the boundaries: a well-designed input filter on the power entry (common-mode choke plus X and Y capacitors, laid out so the filter's input and output are physically separated and the filter's ground reference is clean) handles conducted emissions, and ferrites plus shielding handle cable-borne radiation. The signal-integrity constraint is that any filter in a signal path must be characterized in the band the signal actually occupies — a ferrite that kills a 100 MHz emission may also round off a fast edge or add group delay that breaks timing. So I'd model or measure the filter's insertion loss against the signal's spectrum, not just its DC behavior. I'd also verify the filter doesn't compromise the isolation barrier — Y-capacitors across a patient isolation boundary are a safety question, not just an EMC one. Finally, pre-compliance testing early, with the filter design treated as part of the schematic review rather than a post-failure retrofit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a common-mode choke belongs on the power input or on a signal cable, and what layout mistakes most often defeat an input filter? How do you handle a Y-capacitor that helps emissions but eats into your leakage-current margin?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** I'd anchor the decision in the risk management process rather than in the software team's intuition, because "is this safety-related?" is really "can this failure contribute to a hazardous situation?" The first step is to trace the failure to a hazardous situation in the risk file: if the software failure can, directly or through a chain of events, lead to patient harm, it's safety-related and needs a risk control with verification. If it can only cause annoyance, data loss that has no clinical consequence, or a degraded-but-safe state, it may be a reliability or usability issue — but I'd be careful, because "usability" failures can absolutely be safety-related when they cause use error. The second step is to consider detectability and the state the device lands in: a failure that leaves the device in a safe state and is clearly annunciated is a different risk than one that silently produces plausible-but-wrong output. The third step is to apply the software safety classification framework — the severity of the harm that the software could contribute to drives the class, and that classification then dictates the rigor of the development and verification process. I'd also insist that this decision is documented with rationale and reviewed, not made informally, because it's exactly the kind of judgment that gets scrutinized in an audit. Where the team disagrees, the tie-breaker is the risk file: if you can't show the failure is non-hazardous, treat it as safety-related until you can.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure mode that's safety-related only in combination with a second independent failure? What evidence would you want before accepting a "non-safety" classification in the risk file?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat the disagreement as a risk-acceptability question to be resolved with evidence, not as a schedule negotiation, and I'd want to keep the relationship with quality constructive because quality is doing its job. First, I'd make sure we're actually arguing about the same thing: get the hazard, the estimated severity and probability, and the proposed control written down explicitly, because "negligible" means different things to different people. Then I'd ask what the risk estimate is based on — is there data, a similar-device history, or is it an assumption? If the engineering team's "negligible" rests on an unstated assumption, that's the gap to close, and closing it may resolve the disagreement in either direction. I'd also separate the two questions that often get conflated: is the control *necessary* to reduce risk to acceptable levels, and is it *worth the schedule cost*? The first is a regulatory and ethical question that schedule doesn't get to override; the second is a project-management question where trade-offs are legitimate. If the control is genuinely not needed to reach acceptable residual risk, I'd document that rationale and take it through the risk management review so the decision is made by the process, not by me. If quality still disagrees after that, I'd escalate to the designated risk-acceptance authority rather than let it stall — and I'd look for a lower-cost implementation of the same control (a procedural control, a firmware check instead of a hardware change) that satisfies the concern without the full schedule hit.

**Possible follow-ups:** Who should hold the authority to accept residual risk, and how do you keep that decision defensible in an audit? How would you handle it if the quality manager's concern turned out to be based on a misreading of the hazard analysis?
