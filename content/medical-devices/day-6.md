# medical-devices — Day 6

## Q1: During IEC 60601-1 leakage current testing, you measure elevated patient leakage current on a BF-type applied part. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving this?

**Answer:** I would start by confirming the measurement setup is correct — verifying that the measuring device (MD) is properly connected between the applied part and earth, and that the test is being performed under normal and single-fault conditions (e.g., neutral open, mains on applied part). If the setup is correct, I'd isolate the source by systematically disconnecting sub-circuits from the patient connection path. Common culprits include: Y-capacitors between the patient circuit and earth that are too large in value, insufficient isolation in the power supply's patient-side secondary, or leakage through optocouplers or isolation amplifiers that have inadequate creepage/clearance. I'd check the isolation barrier design — for BF-type applied parts, IEC 60601-1 requires at least 1 MOP (means of patient protection) between the applied part and earth, typically achieved through reinforced insulation or two layers of basic insulation. If the leakage is capacitive, I might need to reduce Y-capacitor values or add a shielding layer connected to protective earth between the primary and secondary of the isolation transformer. If it's resistive, I'd look for contamination, moisture, or insufficient creepage distances on the PCB around the isolation barrier. I'd also verify that any patient-connected cables have appropriate insulation ratings and that the PCB conformal coating is intact.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between capacitive and resistive leakage current contributions during troubleshooting? What leakage current limits apply to BF-type applied parts under normal and single-fault conditions?

## Q2: How would you approach selecting materials for a patient-contacting sensor housing that must meet both biocompatibility (ISO 10993) and mechanical durability requirements?

**Answer:** I'd start by classifying the device's patient contact according to ISO 10993-1: duration (limited, prolonged, or long-term) and nature of contact (surface, external communicating, or implant). For a sensor housing that contacts intact skin for less than 24 hours, that's typically a surface-limited contact device. I'd then identify candidate materials that meet the mechanical requirements — for a housing that must withstand repeated cleaning and handling, I'd consider medical-grade thermoplastics like polycarbonate, ABS, or polysulfone, or silicone elastomers for flexible contact surfaces. Each candidate material should have documented biocompatibility test data from the supplier, ideally per ISO 10993-5 (cytotoxicity), ISO 10993-10 (sensitization), and ISO 10993-11 (irritation) for skin contact. If supplier data isn't available or the material is modified (e.g., with a colorant or mold release agent), I'd plan for device-specific biocompatibility testing. I'd also consider the manufacturing process — injection molding can introduce surface residues or mold release compounds that affect biocompatibility. A risk assessment should document material selection rationale, including chemical composition, leachables, and any processing additives. Finally, I'd ensure the material can withstand the intended sterilization method (e.g., ethylene oxide, gamma radiation, or autoclaving) without degrading or releasing toxic byproducts.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the ideal mechanical material doesn't have existing biocompatibility data? What role does the risk management file play in material selection decisions?

## Q3: How would you approach creating a clinical evaluation report (CER) for a novel medical device that has no substantially equivalent predicate device on the market?

**Answer:** For a novel device without a predicate, the CER must rely on a combination of clinical investigations, scientific literature, and possibly performance testing data. I'd start by defining the intended purpose, clinical claims, and target patient population clearly in a clinical evaluation plan (CEP). Since there's no equivalent device, I'd conduct a systematic literature review to identify relevant clinical data from analogous technologies — for example, if the device is a new type of respiratory monitor, I'd look for studies on similar physiological measurement principles, even if the implementation differs. I'd also search for published data on the underlying technology components (sensors, algorithms) used in other clinical contexts. If the literature review reveals significant gaps, I'd plan for a clinical investigation — this could be a feasibility study to establish safety and performance, followed by a pivotal study to support the intended claims. The CER should document the search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and a critical appraisal of each source. For each identified hazard, I'd discuss how the clinical data supports the safety and performance of the device. The report must also include a justification for why the available data is sufficient to demonstrate conformity with the relevant general safety and performance requirements (GSPRs). Post-market clinical follow-up (PMCF) activities should be planned to address residual uncertainties.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether a clinical investigation is required versus literature alone being sufficient? What are the key differences between a CER for a predicate-based device versus a novel device?

## Q4: A field complaint reports that a medical device's patient-contacting silicone sensor pad is causing skin irritation in several patients. How would you approach the investigation and corrective action process?

**Answer:** I'd follow a structured complaint investigation process aligned with ISO 13485 and 21 CFR Part 820 (or applicable regulations). First, I'd gather all available information: patient demographics, duration of contact, severity of irritation, lot numbers of the affected pads, and any photos or medical reports. I'd also check if similar complaints have been reported from other facilities or regions. Next, I'd assess whether this is an isolated issue or a systemic problem — if multiple lots are affected, it suggests a manufacturing or material issue; if a single lot, it could be a batch contamination. I'd request retained samples from the affected lot for analysis: visual inspection, chemical analysis (e.g., FTIR to check for material changes), and biocompatibility testing (cytotoxicity, irritation, sensitization) per ISO 10993. I'd also review the manufacturing records for that lot — raw material certificates, processing parameters, sterilization records, and any deviations. If the root cause is identified (e.g., a change in raw material supplier, a mold release agent residue, or incomplete curing of the silicone), I'd initiate a corrective and preventive action (CAPA). The CAPA might include updating incoming inspection criteria, modifying the manufacturing process, adding a cleaning step, or changing the material formulation. I'd also assess whether a field safety corrective action (FSCA) is needed — if the irritation is serious, this could involve a recall or field notification. Throughout the process, I'd document findings in the complaint handling system and update the risk management file to reflect the new hazard information.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine the severity level of the complaint and whether it needs to be reported to regulatory authorities? What information would you include in a field safety corrective action notice to customers?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a medical device project. During a design review, the quality manager insists that a new risk control measure must be added to address a hazard with an estimated risk level that the team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a collaborative risk management discussion rather than a confrontation. First, I'd ask the quality manager to walk through their risk assessment in detail — specifically, how they arrived at the severity and probability estimates for that hazard. It's possible they have information or regulatory guidance the team hasn't considered. I'd also review the existing risk control measures to ensure they're properly documented and verified. If the team's assessment shows the residual risk is already acceptable (e.g., severity is negligible or probability is extremely low), I'd suggest we jointly review the ISO 14971 risk acceptability criteria defined in the risk management plan. If the criteria are clear and the risk falls within the acceptable region, I'd document the discussion and the rationale for not adding additional controls. However, if the quality manager has a valid concern — for example, a similar hazard led to a recall in a competitor's device, or a notified body has flagged this type of risk in recent guidance — then I'd acknowledge that and work with the team to find a less schedule-impacting solution. For instance, we might add a software-based control instead of a hardware change, or include the risk in the instructions for use rather than redesigning the hardware. The key is to keep the discussion focused on patient safety and regulatory compliance, not schedule pressure. If we genuinely disagree after a thorough review, I'd escalate to the project sponsor or regulatory affairs manager for a decision, ensuring all perspectives are documented in the design history file.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document this disagreement in the risk management file? What if the quality manager's concern is based on a regulatory expectation that isn't explicitly required by the standard?
