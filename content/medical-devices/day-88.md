# medical-devices — Day 88

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts to their classifications and to the patient connections they represent, because the measurement matrix falls out of that map rather than from a generic checklist. For each applied part I'd identify whether it's BF or CF, whether it's floating or earth-referenced, and whether it's intended for direct cardiac contact — that last point drives the CF designation and the much tighter limits that come with it. Then I'd build the measurement matrix from the standard: patient leakage current from each applied part to earth, patient leakage current between applied parts (including across different classifications), and the appropriate normal-condition and single-fault-condition cases for each. The single-fault cases are where teams get caught out — you have to think about which fault (open earth, open one protective means, applied-part-to-earth short) is credible for each configuration and measure accordingly.

The measurement path itself needs care. I'd use a measuring device that presents the impedance the standard specifies, keep the leads short and well-separated from mains-referenced wiring, and verify the setup with a known current source before trusting any reading — a leakage measurement rig that's silently miswired will happily give you a passing number. For a device with mixed BF and CF parts, I'd also check the isolation between the two applied parts, since a fault that couples one to the other can push a CF part outside its limit even when each part passes individually against earth.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes down to reading the standard's applicability clauses against the actual patient connections, not measuring everything "to be safe." Measuring more than required isn't harmful in itself, but it muddies the verification record and can create confusion about which limit applies to which configuration. I'd document the rationale for each measurement — why it applies, which fault condition it represents, and which limit it's compared against — so the test report is defensible during review.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where an applied part's classification is ambiguous because it's not intended for direct cardiac contact but is used near the heart? What would you do if a CF applied part passed against earth but failed the between-applied-parts measurement?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** I'd start from the harm pathway rather than from the software itself. The question isn't "how bad is this bug" but "can this failure, alone or in combination with other failures or reasonably foreseeable misuse, lead to a hazardous situation that reaches the patient?" That means tracing the failure forward: what does the software do wrong, what does the device then do or fail to do, what does the clinician or patient experience, and what's the clinical consequence. If the chain terminates in an inconvenience, a delayed reading, or a recoverable nuisance, it's a reliability or usability issue. If it can terminate in a missed alarm, a wrong therapy delivery, or a misleading physiological value that a clinician acts on, it's safety-related and belongs in the risk management process.

I'd be careful about two traps. The first is classifying by severity of the *symptom* rather than the *consequence* — a frozen display looks cosmetic until you realize it's the only indication of a critical parameter. The second is treating "the clinician would notice" as a control without evidence; human detection is a legitimate risk control only if it's been validated through usability engineering, not assumed. Where the failure is intermittent or only manifests under specific conditions, I'd also consider whether the detection probability is high enough to rely on.

Once something is judged safety-related, it goes into the hazard analysis with a documented risk control, and the control gets verified like any other — which usually means a specific test that demonstrates the failure is detected, mitigated, or annunciated. The classification decision itself should be recorded with its rationale, because it's exactly the kind of judgment a reviewer will probe later. For borderline cases I'd rather escalate into the risk process and document why the risk was found acceptable than leave it outside the process and have to defend that omission during an audit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that's safety-related only in combination with a second independent failure? Where does the software safety classification under IEC 62304 fit into this decision, and is it the same question?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that has to pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a single problem with two constraints pulling in opposite directions, and resolve it by deciding early which frequency bands belong to which concern. The core tension is that the filtering that helps you pass emissions — series impedance and shunt capacitance at the connector and at noisy sources — is the same filtering that can degrade a signal you care about if it's applied in the wrong place or with the wrong corner frequency. So the first step is to separate the noise sources from the sensitive nodes on paper: switching regulators, digital clocks and their harmonics, motor or actuator drive, and any wireless transmitter are the usual emissions suspects, while the analog front-end and low-level sensor lines are the usual victims. Filtering goes at the source for emissions and at the entry point for immunity, and the sensitive signal path is protected by layout and shielding rather than by loading it with capacitance.

For conducted emissions specifically, I'd plan the front-end filter around the switching frequencies and their harmonics, using a multi-stage approach — a common-mode choke plus X and Y capacitors — and I'd model the filter's interaction with the source and load impedances rather than assuming a textbook response, because a filter that's mismatched can actually amplify at some frequencies. For radiated emissions, the countermeasures are usually about loop area and return paths first, filtering second: a filter can't fix a badly routed clock or a split return plane. I'd also keep the filter's parasitic behavior in mind — component self-resonance means a capacitor stops behaving like a capacitor above a certain frequency, so the physical placement and the choice of dielectric matter as much as the nominal value.

On the signal integrity side, I'd define the passband each signal actually needs and verify the filter doesn't intrude on it, then confirm with measurement rather than simulation alone. The verification plan should include both the compliance measurements and the device's own functional accuracy tests, because passing emissions while degrading a sensor reading is not a pass. If a conflict emerges — say, a filter needed for emissions sits right at the edge of a signal's bandwidth — I'd look for a topology change (differential signaling, a higher-resolution ADC with digital filtering, moving the noisy source) before trying to tune a filter into a corner it can't fit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a common-mode choke and a differential-mode filter for a particular interface? What would you do if a filter that passes emissions causes a phase shift that affects a closed-loop control response?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** I'd structure it so the file is a byproduct of the design work rather than a separate deliverable. That means the hazard analysis is a living artifact that gets updated at each design review, with each hazard traced to the design decision or risk control that addresses it, and each control traced forward to the verification evidence that demonstrates it works. The file's skeleton — hazard identification, risk estimation, risk control, residual risk evaluation, and the overall benefit-risk conclusion — should be populated early, even when the content is thin, because an empty structure invites the "assemble it at the end" failure mode.

Practically, I'd keep the risk analysis linked to the design inputs and outputs so that a change to a design element automatically raises the question of whether the hazard analysis needs updating. A change-control step that asks "does this affect any hazard or control?" is what keeps the file current; without it, the file drifts out of sync with the design and the gap only surfaces at audit. I'd also make the risk file searchable by hazard and by design element, so a reviewer can go from a hazard to its controls and evidence in a couple of steps rather than reconstructing the chain by hand.

The other discipline that matters is recording the *reasoning*, not just the conclusion. A risk estimate with no rationale is unauditable — a reviewer can't tell whether it was considered or guessed. I'd capture the basis for each severity and probability judgment, the alternatives considered for each control, and why the residual risk was accepted. And I'd keep the file's structure aligned with whatever the quality system expects, so the same content serves both the engineering team and the regulatory submission without a reformatting exercise at the end.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard that's identified late, after the design is largely frozen? What's the relationship between the risk management file and the design history file, and where do they overlap?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat the disagreement as a question about the risk estimate rather than about who's right, and try to resolve it on evidence. The first step is to get the quality manager's reasoning on the table: is the concern about the severity estimate, the probability estimate, the detectability, or the completeness of the hazard identification? Often the disagreement dissolves once the basis for each estimate is explicit — sometimes the engineering team has underweighted severity because the harm is rare, and sometimes the quality manager is applying a conservative default that the evidence doesn't support. Either way, the conversation should be about the estimate and its rationale, not about the schedule.

If the evidence supports the engineering team's position, I'd document that rationale explicitly in the risk file — the severity and probability judgments, the basis for them, and why the risk is judged acceptable — so the decision is defensible and the quality manager's concern is on record rather than overridden. If the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, I'd lean toward the more conservative position, because an unresolved risk question is exactly the kind of thing that becomes a finding later, and the cost of a control added now is usually smaller than the cost of defending its absence during review.

On the schedule impact, I'd separate the question of *whether* the control is needed from the question of *how* to implement it. If the control is warranted, there's often a cheaper implementation than the first one proposed — a firmware check instead of a hardware change, a design change that folds into an already-planned revision — and I'd ask the team to find that path rather than treating the schedule as a reason to skip the control. What I'd avoid is letting the schedule decide the risk question, because that inverts the priority order and is hard to defend if the hazard later materializes.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's position was based on a regulatory expectation rather than a technical risk estimate? How would you escalate if the disagreement couldn't be resolved at the design review?
