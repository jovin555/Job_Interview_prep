# medical-devices — Day 85

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The starting point is to read the applied-part definitions and the measurement network requirements from IEC 60601-1 rather than from memory, because the limits and the test circuits differ by classification and by condition. BF applied parts have a patient leakage limit of 100 µA in normal condition and 500 µA in single-fault condition; CF applied parts are an order of magnitude tighter at 10 µA NC and 50 µA SFC. The measurement network itself is defined in the standard — it's essentially a frequency-weighted impedance that approximates the human body, and it must be inserted between the applied part and earth in the specified polarity, with the device powered at nominal mains voltage plus a defined percentage, and with the mains polarity reversed for each measurement.

For the measurement path, I'd build a fixture that lets me switch the measurement network between each applied part and earth without disturbing the device under test, and I'd keep the network's leads short and shielded so that the fixture itself doesn't pick up ambient fields and inflate the reading. I'd also measure with the device in each of its operating modes, because leakage can change when a pump runs, a heater turns on, or a wireless radio transmits — the standard requires the measurement in each mode that could affect the result.

Deciding which measurements are required is a matter of enumerating the applied parts, their classifications, and the conditions. For a device with both BF and CF parts, I'd measure patient leakage from each applied part individually, from all applied parts tied together, and from each applied part to every other applied part where the standard requires it. I'd also measure the earth leakage and the enclosure leakage, because a fault in the mains section can show up as patient leakage through the isolation barrier. The key discipline is to build a matrix of applied part × condition (NC/SFC) × mains polarity × operating mode, and to record which cells are required by the standard and which are informative but not required — that matrix becomes the test procedure and the evidence for the DHF.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the CF applied part is floating and the BF applied part is earth-referenced, how does that change the measurement setup?
- What would you do if a measurement is marginally over the limit only in one operating mode?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow-band failure is almost always a resonance or a coupling path that is tuned to that frequency, so the first job is to characterize it precisely rather than to start changing hardware. I'd confirm the failure is reproducible, note the exact frequency, the field strength at which it appears, the modulation, and the device behavior — does it reset, does it produce a false reading, does it drop a communication link, does it recover on its own? That behavior tells me which subsystem is being disturbed.

Next I'd try to localize the coupling path. I'd repeat the test with the device in different orientations and with the enclosure open versus closed, because a shift in the failure frequency with orientation points to a cable or a slot acting as an antenna, while a stable frequency points to an on-board resonance. I'd then use a near-field probe to scan the board and cables at the failing frequency to find where the energy is actually entering. Common culprits are unshielded sensor cables, a connector whose shield is bonded at only one end, a slot in the enclosure near a high-impedance node, or a trace that forms a loop with a resonant length near a quarter wavelength.

Once I know the path, the fix is usually one of a few things: improve the shield termination so the cable shield is bonded to chassis at the connector, add a common-mode choke or ferrite on the offending cable, add a small RC or feedthrough filter at the point where the disturbance enters the sensitive circuit, or change the layout to shrink the loop area. If the disturbance is getting into the analog front-end, I'd also check whether the front-end's common-mode rejection is adequate at that frequency — sometimes the fix is a better instrumentation amplifier or a differential input rather than more filtering. I'd verify the fix by re-testing at the failing frequency and at the band edges, and I'd re-run the full immunity sweep to make sure the fix didn't create a new failure elsewhere.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish a cable-coupled failure from a direct field-to-trace coupling failure?
- If the fix requires a board respin, how would you decide whether to add a temporary mitigation for the test lab while the respin is in progress?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file should be a living record that grows with the design, not a retrospective summary. I'd start by establishing the risk management plan early — it defines the scope, the responsibilities, the risk acceptability criteria, the methods for hazard identification and risk estimation, and the review points where the file will be updated. That plan is itself part of the file, and it's what an auditor reads first to understand how the rest was produced.

The core of the file is the hazard analysis and the risk control traceability. I'd maintain a hazard analysis that starts from a structured prompt — a checklist or a fault-tree approach — to identify hazards from the device's intended use, its energy sources, its materials, its software, and its use environment. Each hazard gets an estimated severity and probability, a risk control, and a verification that the control was implemented and effective. The traceability matrix links each risk control to the design input it satisfies, the design output that implements it, and the verification evidence. Keeping that matrix current as the design changes is what prevents the file from becoming stale.

The practical discipline is to tie risk management to the design review cadence. Every design review should include a risk management update: new hazards identified, controls added or changed, residual risks re-evaluated. When a design change is proposed, the risk file is one of the first things I'd check — does this change introduce a new hazard, does it invalidate a control, does it change the residual risk? I'd also keep the file's structure aligned with the DHF so that a reviewer can move between them without hunting. The goal is that at any point in the project, someone unfamiliar with the device could open the file and understand what the risks are, how they're controlled, and what evidence supports the controls.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a risk that can't be reduced below the acceptability threshold?
- What's the difference between the risk management file and the risk management report, and when is each produced?

## Q4: How would you approach developing a software test plan for a medical device that uses machine learning for diagnostic decision support, given that IEC 62304 doesn't explicitly address AI/ML?

**Answer:** IEC 62304 gives me the software lifecycle framework — classification, architecture, unit and integration testing, release criteria — but it doesn't tell me how to verify a model whose behavior is learned rather than specified. So I'd use 62304 as the skeleton and add an ML-specific verification layer on top of it, and I'd be explicit in the plan about which requirements come from the standard and which come from the nature of the model.

The first thing I'd do is separate the software into the ML component and the surrounding deterministic software. The deterministic parts — data acquisition, preprocessing, alarm logic, UI, logging — are verified the way any Class B or C software is: requirements-based testing, boundary conditions, fault injection, traceability. The ML component needs a different approach. I'd define the model's intended clinical use and its performance requirements in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and the acceptable operating point, and I'd verify against a held-out test set that was not used in training or tuning. I'd also verify the model's behavior on edge cases — out-of-distribution inputs, missing data, sensor artifacts — because a model that performs well on the average case can fail badly on the cases that matter clinically.

Beyond performance, I'd verify the data pipeline: that the training data is representative of the intended population, that preprocessing at inference matches preprocessing at training, and that the model's inputs are validated before inference. I'd also plan for monitoring in the field, because a model's performance can drift as the patient population or the sensor characteristics change. The test plan would include a post-market performance monitoring component, with defined thresholds that trigger investigation. The honest position is that ML verification is an evolving area, and I'd document the rationale for the approach I chose, the limitations of the evidence, and the residual risk — that documentation is what makes the approach defensible to a reviewer even where the standard is silent.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a model update after the device is on the market — is that a new submission or a change control?
- What would you do if the model's performance on the held-out set met the requirement but its performance on a clinically important subgroup was worse?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a project where the clinical team has requested a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a decision that needs to be made on evidence, not on advocacy, and I'd structure the response so that the clinical team, the program manager, and the quality function all see the same trade-off. The first step is to understand the request precisely: what clinical problem does it solve, how often does that problem occur, what's the harm if it isn't solved, and is there a software-only or labeling-only way to achieve most of the benefit? Often a usability concern can be mitigated by a change to the user interface, the instructions for use, or a training module without touching hardware.

If the change genuinely requires a hardware revision, I'd quantify the impact honestly: the engineering effort, the verification and validation re-work, the regulatory submission delay, and the risk of introducing new issues late in the design. I'd also assess whether the change affects the device's safety or essential performance — if it does, it may be a required change rather than an optional one, and the schedule discussion changes. I'd bring that assessment to a cross-functional review with the clinical lead, the program manager, and quality, and I'd frame the options: implement now and accept the delay, implement in a follow-on revision after the initial submission, or address the underlying need through a non-hardware route.

The decision itself is a business and clinical judgment, not an engineering one, but my job is to make sure it's made with the full picture. If the decision is to defer, I'd document the rationale and the plan for the follow-on revision, and I'd make sure the clinical team understands what they're getting in the interim. If the decision is to implement now, I'd re-baseline the schedule and the risk file, and I'd make sure the change goes through the same design review and verification process as any other change — late changes are exactly when shortcuts get tempting, and that's when they're most dangerous.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the clinical team and the program manager disagreed on the priority?
- If the change is deferred to a follow-on revision, how would you make sure the clinical need isn't lost?
