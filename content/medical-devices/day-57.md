# medical-devices — Day 57

## Q1: How would you approach designing the thermal management for a patient-worn medical sensor that must remain within its safe touch-temperature limit during continuous operation, given that the electronics are sealed in a compact, non-vented enclosure?
**Answer:** Start by treating the touch-temperature limit as a hard design input derived from the applicable standard and the intended contact duration, not as an afterthought. The first step is a thermal budget: estimate total dissipated power from the major contributors — the radio during transmit bursts, the sensor excitation circuitry, the MCU, and any linear regulators or LED drive — and compare that against the surface area available to reject heat. In a sealed, non-vented enclosure the only paths out are conduction through the housing and convection/radiation from the outer surface, so the enclosure material and wall thickness become part of the thermal solution rather than just a mechanical choice. From there the levers are: reduce dissipation at the source (switching regulators instead of linear, duty-cycling the radio, lowering LED current, choosing a lower-power MCU mode), spread the heat so no single hot spot exceeds the limit (thermal vias, copper pours, spreading the high-dissipation parts away from the skin-contact face), and add margin so worst-case ambient plus worst-case duty cycle still passes. I would verify with thermocouples or an IR camera on the contact surface under the worst-case operating mode, at the maximum specified ambient, and confirm against the limit with margin. If the budget doesn't close, the honest answer is that the enclosure or the duty cycle has to change — you can't cheat a touch-temperature limit with firmware alone.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between reducing power dissipation versus increasing the thermal mass or surface area of the enclosure? How would you handle a scenario where the worst-case thermal condition only occurs during a rare combination of radio transmit and sensor excitation?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?
**Answer:** The classification should come out of the risk management process, not from a subjective judgment call in the moment. The starting point is the hazardous situation the failure could contribute to, and whether that situation could lead to harm. A software failure that only degrades convenience, or that produces an annoyance the user can trivially recover from, is a reliability or usability issue. A software failure that could cause the device to display a wrong physiological value, fail to annunciate a valid alarm, or drive an actuator incorrectly is contributing to a hazardous situation and belongs in the risk file. The key discriminator is the sequence: failure → hazardous situation → harm. If any credible path through that sequence exists, it's safety-related and needs a risk control, and the control has to be verified. I would also be careful not to let "it's just a display glitch" reasoning shortcut the analysis — a display glitch on a monitored parameter can absolutely be safety-related if a clinician acts on it. When there's genuine disagreement, the tie-breaker is to document the hazard analysis, the severity and probability estimates, and let the risk management review make the call rather than resolving it informally. The software safety classification under IEC 62304 then follows from that analysis, and it drives the rigor of the software lifecycle activities.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a case where the failure is intermittent and hard to reproduce, making the probability estimate uncertain? How does the software safety classification change the verification activities you would perform?

## Q3: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?
**Answer:** I would treat traceability as a living structure rather than a document produced at the end. The backbone is a requirements management approach where every design input has a unique identifier, every design output that satisfies it references that identifier, and every verification and validation activity references the outputs it exercises. That gives a bidirectional map: from any requirement you can find the evidence that it was met, and from any test you can find the requirement it demonstrates. The practical discipline is to make the traceability matrix a byproduct of normal engineering work — requirements captured in a tool, test cases linked to requirements as they're written, and design reviews checking that the links are complete — rather than a heroic effort before submission. When the design changes, the change control process is what keeps traceability honest: a change to a design input triggers a review of the affected outputs, verification, and validation, and the matrix is updated as part of the change, not after. I would also keep the risk file linked into the same structure, so risk controls trace to the requirements and tests that verify them. The failure mode to avoid is a matrix that looks complete but has drifted out of sync with the actual design, because that's worse than an obviously incomplete one — it gives false confidence during an audit.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a late design change that invalidates a set of already-completed verification tests? What would you do if you inherited a project with a traceability matrix that had significant gaps?

## Q4: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?
**Answer:** I would start from the intended clinical function and the device's mode of action, then work through the IEC 60601-2 series to identify every particular standard whose scope covers any function the device performs. A combination device can fall under more than one particular standard, and the correct approach is to apply all that are relevant rather than picking one. The general standard, IEC 60601-1, applies in all cases, and the collateral standards (like 60601-1-2 for EMC, 60601-1-6 for usability, 60601-1-8 for alarms, 60601-1-11 for home healthcare) apply based on the device's characteristics and environment. Where a particular standard and the general standard both address the same requirement, the particular standard takes precedence for that requirement — it's a more specific application of the general requirement, not a replacement for the whole general standard. Where two particular standards appear to conflict, the resolution is usually to satisfy the more stringent requirement, or to document the rationale for how both are met, and to raise it with the test lab early rather than discovering it during testing. I would also confirm the applicable standards with the regulatory strategy and the test lab before locking the design, because the particular standards can impose requirements that affect hardware and firmware architecture, not just test procedures.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a particular standard imposes a requirement that conflicts with a design decision already made? How would you document the rationale for which standards apply in the design history file?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a project where the clinical team has requested a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?
**Answer:** I would first make sure I understand the request precisely — what clinical problem it solves, how often it occurs, and what the consequence is if it isn't addressed. A usability change framed as a preference and one framed as a patient-safety or use-error concern are very different conversations. Then I would evaluate it against the risk file: does the current design create a use-related hazard that this change would mitigate? If so, the change may be necessary regardless of schedule, and the right move is to escalate it as a risk management decision rather than a schedule decision. If it's a genuine improvement but not safety-driven, I would lay out the options honestly: implement now and accept the schedule impact, defer to a post-launch revision with a documented rationale, or find a lower-cost interim mitigation such as a labeling or training change that addresses the underlying need without a hardware revision. I would bring the clinical team, quality, and regulatory into that discussion rather than deciding unilaterally, because the trade-off spans disciplines. Whatever the outcome, I would document the decision and its rationale so it's defensible later. The thing I would avoid is either silently absorbing the change and blowing the schedule, or reflexively saying no and dismissing a legitimate clinical concern.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the clinical team and the regulatory team disagreed on whether the change was necessary before submission? What would you do if the change could be implemented in firmware rather than hardware, at a smaller schedule cost?
