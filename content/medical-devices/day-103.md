# medical-devices — Day 103

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement matrix, because the required measurements are driven by classification, not by convenience. For each applied part I'd determine whether it's BF or CF, whether it's intended for direct cardiac contact, and whether the device is mains-powered or internally powered — those three facts determine which leakage current limits and which test conditions apply.

For the measurement path itself, the key is that the measuring network (the MD) must be connected between the applied part and earth, or between applied parts, depending on the test. I'd build a dedicated test fixture rather than probing on the bench: a properly characterized measuring device per the standard's Figure 13/14 network, short, well-shielded leads to the applied part, and a way to switch between applied parts without disturbing the setup. The fixture needs to represent the worst-case patient impedance and the correct mains polarity and neutral/line reversal conditions, because leakage current is not symmetric under reversed polarity.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes down to: patient leakage current from each applied part to earth (normal and single-fault), patient leakage current between applied parts of different classifications (this is where BF and CF together matter — the CF part is the more restrictive one), and for CF parts specifically, the tighter limits and the additional single-fault conditions. I'd also check whether the device has any applied part that's not isolated from mains, which changes the analysis entirely.

The practical trap is measuring a CF part with the same setup used for BF and assuming the number is valid — the limits differ and the single-fault conditions differ. I'd document the measurement matrix explicitly in the verification plan so nothing gets skipped, and I'd verify the fixture itself against a known reference before trusting any reading.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where one applied part is BF and another is CF, and the two share a common reference? What would you do if a measurement is marginally over the limit and you suspect the fixture rather than the design?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** I'd start from the hazard, not from the software. The question isn't "is this failure bad?" but "can this failure, in combination with the device's other characteristics, lead to an unacceptable risk to the patient or operator?" So the first step is to trace the failure to a hazardous situation and then to a harm, using the risk management file as the reference. If the failure can't reach a hazardous situation — because of an independent hardware interlock, a watchdog, a plausibility check, or because the function isn't safety-related — then it's a reliability or usability issue and belongs in the defect tracker, not the risk file.

If it can reach a hazardous situation, I'd assess severity and probability: what's the worst credible harm, how likely is the failure mode, and is it detectable before harm occurs? A failure that produces a wrong displayed value that a clinician could act on is very different from a failure that produces a logged value nobody reads in real time. I'd also consider whether the failure is detectable by the user — undetected wrong output is generally more serious than an obvious error message.

The classification then drives the rigor: safety-related failures need formal risk controls, verification that the control is effective, and traceability in the risk file; non-safety issues get normal defect handling and reliability testing. I'd be careful about the "it's just a display glitch" argument — if the display is the clinician's only window into the patient, a display glitch is a safety issue.

I'd also make sure the decision is documented with rationale, because auditors and reviewers will ask why a given failure was or wasn't treated as safety-related, and "we discussed it and decided" without a record is a finding waiting to happen.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that's safety-related only in combination with a second independent failure? Where does the software safety classification under IEC 62304 fit into this decision?

## Q3: How would you approach developing a design verification test plan for a medical device that must measure both temperature and pressure, where the two sensors share a common ADC and multiplexer, and the device must maintain specified accuracy for both parameters simultaneously?

**Answer:** The shared ADC and multiplexer is the crux, so I'd design the plan around the interactions rather than testing each channel in isolation. First I'd characterize the multiplexer: channel-to-channel crosstalk, charge injection, settling time after switching, and on-resistance variation with temperature. Those are the mechanisms by which one channel's measurement can corrupt the other, and they're invisible if you only test one sensor at a time.

The plan would have three layers. Layer one is per-channel accuracy against a traceable reference, swept across the specified range, with the other channel held at a representative value — this establishes the baseline. Layer two is the simultaneous test: both sensors driven across their ranges at the same time, including the worst-case combination (e.g., pressure at full scale while temperature is at an extreme), because that's when settling and crosstalk are worst. Layer three is timing and sequencing: verify that the firmware's channel-switching and settling delays are adequate at the fastest sampling rate the device uses, and that accuracy holds at that rate, not just at a slow bench rate.

I'd also include temperature as a test variable for the electronics themselves — the ADC's own offset and gain drift, and the reference's drift, affect both channels, so the plan needs runs at the temperature extremes of the operating range, not just ambient. And I'd verify the reference voltage and its decoupling under the switching load, since a shared reference is a shared failure mode.

For the acceptance criteria, I'd define accuracy per channel against the device's specified limits, and I'd define a "no interference" criterion — the presence of activity on one channel must not shift the other channel's reading beyond a stated fraction of its error budget. That criterion is what makes the test meaningful rather than just a data collection exercise.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you isolate whether an accuracy failure is the sensor, the multiplexer, or the ADC reference? What would you do if the device passes at slow sampling but fails at the specified rate?

## Q4: How would you approach selecting materials for a patient-contacting sensor housing that must meet both biocompatibility (ISO 10993) and mechanical durability requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a two-sided constraint problem and start by defining the contact category precisely, because ISO 10993 testing is driven by contact duration and nature — surface contact versus mucosal versus indirect, and limited versus prolonged versus long-term. Getting that categorization wrong means either over-testing (expensive, slow) or under-testing (a regulatory problem later). I'd confirm the intended use and contact duration with the clinical and regulatory teams before selecting anything.

On the biocompatibility side, I'd prefer materials with an existing biocompatibility history and a supplier who can provide the relevant test data and a controlled formulation, rather than a novel material that needs a full test battery. I'd also ask about the manufacturing process — mold release agents, colorants, and sterilization method can change the biocompatibility profile of an otherwise acceptable resin, so the material decision has to include the process and the sterilization cycle.

On the durability side, I'd define the mechanical requirements from the use case: drop, crush, repeated flexing at the cable entry, chemical exposure from cleaning agents, and the number of sterilization or disinfection cycles. Then I'd check the candidate material's datasheet properties against those, but I'd verify with actual testing rather than trusting datasheets, because the molded part's properties depend on gate location, wall thickness, and process parameters.

The intersection is where it gets interesting: a material that's mechanically ideal may not be biocompatible, and a biocompatible material may not survive the cleaning regimen. I'd build a shortlist, run coupon-level tests on both axes in parallel, and only then commit to tooling. I'd also involve the supplier early, because material substitution after tooling is expensive.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a material change late in development after biocompatibility testing is complete? What role does the sterilization method play in the material selection?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd resist the temptation to treat this as a schedule argument versus a quality argument, because framed that way nobody wins and the decision gets made on seniority rather than evidence. Instead I'd try to make it an evidence question: what is the actual risk estimate for this hazard, and what's the basis for the disagreement?

First I'd ask the quality manager to walk through their reasoning — sometimes the concern is legitimate and the engineering team has underestimated severity or probability, and the right move is to update the risk estimate. Other times the concern is a precautionary instinct without a hazard path, and the conversation is about documenting why the risk is acceptable. Either way, the risk management file is the place to resolve it, not the design review meeting.

If the estimates genuinely differ, I'd look at what evidence would settle it: test data, field data from similar devices, literature, or a focused analysis. I'd propose getting that evidence rather than voting. If the evidence supports the engineering team's position, I'd document the rationale and the residual risk acceptance, and make sure the quality manager's concern is recorded and addressed in the file — that protects both the decision and the relationship.

If the evidence is ambiguous, I'd lean toward the conservative option but look for the cheapest effective control rather than the most expensive one — sometimes a labeling change, an alarm, or a software check addresses the concern at a fraction of the cost of a hardware change. And if the schedule impact is genuinely significant, I'd escalate the trade-off to the project decision-maker with the options and costs laid out, rather than absorbing it silently.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager has authority to block the design review without the control? How would you prevent this kind of disagreement from recurring on the next project?
