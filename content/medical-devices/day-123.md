# medical-devices — Day 123

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement matrix rather than treating "leakage current" as a single number. The key distinction is that BF and CF parts have different allowable limits and different fault conditions that must be evaluated, so the measurement path has to be able to isolate each applied part individually — you can't just measure the aggregate.

Practically, I'd build a measurement network that lets me connect the measuring device (MD) between any single applied part and earth, and between applied parts, with the rest of the applied parts left floating or connected per the test setup. For a mixed BF/CF device, the CF part will typically drive the tighter limit, so I'd design the fixture so the CF part can be measured in isolation without the BF part's leakage masking or contributing to the reading. That means switched connections rather than a hardwired harness, and careful attention to the MD's own input impedance and the test supply's reference.

Deciding which measurements are required comes down to: (1) the classification of each applied part, (2) whether the part is intended for direct cardiac connection (CF) or not (BF), (3) the number of applied parts and whether they're simultaneously connected to the patient, and (4) normal condition vs. single-fault condition. I'd build a matrix — rows are applied parts, columns are the applicable measurement types (patient leakage, patient auxiliary current, etc.) and conditions — and populate it directly from the standard's clauses. Anything not in the matrix doesn't get measured, which keeps the test plan defensible and avoids over-testing.

- How would you handle a device where the BF and CF parts share a common reference node internally — does that change the measurement approach?
- What would you do if a measurement sits comfortably under the limit but the test house's setup gives a noticeably different reading from yours?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a frequency-partitioning problem from the start, not a filter you bolt on at the end. Conducted emissions live at the lower end (roughly 150 kHz to 30 MHz), radiated at the higher end (30 MHz up), and the signal integrity requirements usually sit somewhere in between or overlap — so the first job is to lay out where each concern lives on the spectrum and make sure the filter topologies don't fight each other.

For conducted emissions, the work is mostly at the power input: a common-mode choke plus X/Y capacitors sized against the leakage-current budget (Y-caps trade off directly against patient/earth leakage, so on a medical device that's a hard constraint, not a free parameter). I'd also look at the switching supply's layout and grounding, because a lot of "conducted emissions failures" are really layout problems — hot loops, poor return paths — that no amount of filtering fully fixes.

For radiated, the levers are different: cable common-mode currents, enclosure seams, and high-di/dt or high-dv/dt nodes. Ferrites on cables, shielding, and slowing edges where the timing budget allows. The signal-integrity tension shows up here — a series termination or ferrite that kills an emission can also degrade an edge or add jitter, so I'd define the SI budget (eye, jitter, setup/hold margin) up front and verify the filter doesn't eat into it.

The discipline I'd insist on is measuring each change against both requirements simultaneously — pre-compliance scans plus SI measurements on the same bench — rather than optimizing emissions and then discovering the link no longer closes timing.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether a ferrite bead or an RC snubber is the right tool for a given emission, given the SI cost of each?
- If the Y-capacitor budget is constrained by leakage current, what other levers do you have for conducted emissions?

## Q3: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I'd start from the device's intended use and clinical claims, not from the product category name, because the particular standards are scoped by function. I'd list every clinical function the device performs, then for each one identify whether a 60601-2-xx standard exists that covers it — monitoring, therapy delivery, and so on — and read each one's scope clause carefully, since scope clauses are where "does this apply to me" is actually answered. A combined device typically ends up subject to more than one particular standard, and possibly to collateral standards (like 60601-1-2 for EMC, 60601-1-6 for usability, 60601-1-8 for alarms) on top.

On conflicts: the general standard and particular standards are written so that the particular standard takes precedence for the specific requirement it addresses, and the general standard fills in everything the particular doesn't cover. So the resolution rule is "particular over general, where they actually conflict" — but the real work is confirming it's a genuine conflict and not two requirements that both apply. I'd document each apparent conflict, the clause references, the resolution, and the rationale, and get it reviewed by regulatory/quality early rather than discovering it during a test-house kickoff. Where the standards genuinely leave a gap or ambiguity, I'd look at how the test lab and notified body expect it to be handled and align with them before testing, so the interpretation is agreed in advance.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if two particular standards impose different, incompatible test conditions for the same subsystem?
- How would you document the standard-applicability decision so it survives an audit years later?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** I'd anchor the decision in the risk analysis rather than in the severity of the symptom. The question isn't "does this failure look bad?" but "can this failure, in the context of the device's intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse, contribute to a hazardous situation?" So the first step is to trace the failure to the hazard it could lead to and the harm that hazard could cause — if the failure can plausibly contribute to patient harm, it's safety-related regardless of how rarely it occurs or how minor the immediate symptom seems.

A few practical tests I'd apply: Does the failure affect a function that the risk file identifies as risk-relevant? Could it defeat or degrade a risk control measure? Could it mislead the clinician — for example, present plausible-but-wrong data that drives a wrong decision? If the answer to any of those is yes, I'd treat it as safety-related and route it through the formal risk process, even if the probability is low. Probability affects the risk estimate, not whether the failure belongs in the safety analysis at all.

Conversely, a failure that only affects convenience, cosmetics, or a non-clinical function — with no credible path to harm — can be handled as a usability or reliability defect through normal defect tracking. The important discipline is documenting the rationale either way, so the classification is defensible and consistent, and revisiting it if the design or intended use changes.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a failure whose safety relevance depends on clinical workflow — for example, whether a nurse would notice a stale display?
- Where does this classification feed into IEC 62304 software safety classification, and how do the two connect?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd resist the temptation to frame it as "engineering vs. quality" and instead get both sides looking at the same risk estimate. The quality manager's concern is legitimate by default — they're accountable for the risk file being defensible — so my first move is to understand what's driving their view: is it the severity of the potential harm, the detectability, an ambiguity in how the hazard was characterized, or a concern about how a reviewer or auditor would read it? Often the disagreement is really about an unstated assumption in the risk estimate rather than about the hazard itself.

From there I'd walk through the risk analysis together: the hazard, the sequence of events, the severity and probability rationale, and whether existing controls already reduce the risk to an acceptable level. If the estimate holds up, I'd document that reasoning explicitly in the risk file so the acceptance is traceable — that often resolves the concern, because the quality manager's real need is a defensible record, not necessarily the extra control. If the estimate doesn't hold up, or if there's genuine uncertainty, I'd take the control seriously and look for the least disruptive way to implement it — sometimes a design change, sometimes a labeling or workflow control, sometimes a verification activity that demonstrates the risk is already mitigated.

On schedule: I'd be honest about the impact and bring it into the decision rather than using it as leverage. If the control is warranted, the schedule adjusts; if it isn't, the documented rationale is the resolution. Either way the outcome is a risk file that reflects a shared, recorded judgment rather than one party overruling the other.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if the quality manager accepted the rationale but a notified body reviewer later questioned it?
- How would you prevent this kind of late-stage disagreement from recurring on the next project?
