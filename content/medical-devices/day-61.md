# medical-devices — Day 61

## Q1: How would you approach designing the analog signal chain for a patient-connected sensor whose signal of interest is very small and sits on a large, slowly varying baseline — for example, a biosensor whose useful signal is a fraction of a millivolt riding on a baseline that can drift by hundreds of millivolts?
**Answer:** The core problem is dynamic range, not just gain, so I'd think about it as a chain rather than a single amplifier. First, I'd separate the baseline from the signal of interest: a high-pass or band-pass stage early in the chain removes the slow drift before it eats into the ADC's usable range, so the later gain stages can be sized for the small signal rather than for the worst-case baseline. Second, I'd choose the topology based on where the noise floor needs to sit — an instrumentation amplifier front end for high common-mode rejection and low input-referred noise, followed by a filtered gain stage, rather than trying to do everything in one op-amp. Third, I'd budget the error sources explicitly: input offset voltage and its drift over temperature, input bias current interacting with source impedance, resistor thermal noise, and reference noise, because at these signal levels the reference and the passives are as important as the amplifier. Fourth, I'd think about the ADC interface — differential input if available, anti-alias filtering ahead of the converter, and a reference with adequate initial accuracy and tempco. Finally, I'd make the design verifiable: define the accuracy budget up front, identify which terms dominate, and plan bench measurements (noise density, offset drift over temperature, CMRR) that can confirm each term rather than only testing the end-to-end number. If the baseline drift is physiological rather than an artifact, I'd also consider whether it should be preserved for the algorithm rather than filtered out, which is a requirements question before it's a circuit question.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide between AC-coupling the signal early versus doing baseline removal digitally after the ADC?
- How would you verify the noise budget on the bench without a full environmental chamber?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device falls under a particular standard in the IEC 60601-2 series, and what would you do if the general standard and a particular standard appeared to conflict?
**Answer:** I'd start from the device's intended clinical function and its risk profile, not from a keyword match on the product name. The particular standards are organized around device types and the hazards specific to them, so the first step is to write down what the device actually does — what it measures, what it delivers, where it's used, and who touches it — and then walk the IEC 60601-2 series index against that description. If the device combines functions, more than one particular standard can apply, and I'd treat that as the expected case rather than an edge case. On conflicts: the general standard and a particular standard are not peers. A particular standard takes precedence for the requirements it explicitly covers, and it may add to, replace, or modify a clause of the general standard. So the resolution is to read the particular standard's scope and its clause-by-clause modifications carefully, document which clause governs for each requirement, and record that reasoning in the compliance matrix. Where a genuine ambiguity remains — for example, two particular standards imposing different test conditions on the same applied part — I'd raise it with the test lab early and, if needed, with the regulatory reviewer, rather than resolving it unilaterally late in the project. The practical output is a standards applicability matrix that maps each requirement to its governing clause and its verification method, kept under change control alongside the DHF.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a device that is a borderline fit for a particular standard — close but not clearly in scope?
- What would you put in the compliance matrix so it stays useful when the design changes?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that has to pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?
**Answer:** I'd treat emissions compliance and signal integrity as one design problem, because the fixes for one often hurt the other. The first principle is to control noise at the source: choose switching regulators and clock frequencies with the harmonic content in mind, slow down fast edges where timing allows, and keep high-di/dt and high-dv/dt loops physically small. The second is to define the return path deliberately — a continuous reference plane, no splits under signals that cross them, and connector pinouts that keep signal returns adjacent to their signals. The third is filtering, applied in layers: local decoupling at each IC, bulk decoupling at the rail entry, and a common-mode choke or filter at the cable or connector interface, since cables are usually the dominant antenna. For conducted emissions, the mains input filter is the main lever, and its layout matters as much as its component values — the filter has to be referenced to a clean ground and placed so that noise doesn't couple around it. For signal integrity, I'd be careful that the same filtering doesn't degrade the signals I care about: differential signaling where possible, controlled impedance on high-speed nets, and filter corner frequencies chosen from the actual signal bandwidth rather than copied from a reference design. Finally, I'd plan pre-compliance testing early, at the board level if possible, so that the filter values and layout can be tuned before the enclosure and cabling are frozen — because the enclosure and cable routing change the radiated result substantially.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether a ferrite bead or a common-mode choke is the right part at a given interface?
- What would you do if the emissions fix that works on the bench degrades the analog accuracy of the sensor front end?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?
**Answer:** I'd treat the risk management file as a living part of the design process, not a deliverable produced for submission. Practically, that means starting it at the concept stage, when the intended use and the foreseeable hazards are first understood, and updating it at each design review rather than in a burst before the submission. The structure I'd use follows ISO 14971's logic: intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse, hazard identification, risk estimation, risk control, verification of the controls, and evaluation of residual risk — with each entry traceable to the design inputs and outputs it touches. The key discipline is that every risk control becomes a design requirement with a verification method, so the risk file and the DHF reference each other rather than duplicating each other. I'd also keep the hazard analysis and the DFMEA linked but distinct, since they answer different questions — one is about what could harm the patient, the other about how a function could fail. To keep it maintainable, I'd assign ownership, version it with the design, and review it at the same cadence as the design reviews, so that a change to the design triggers a review of the affected hazards rather than a scramble at the end. The test of whether it's working is whether a reviewer can follow a hazard from identification through control to verification without asking for a missing link.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a hazard that is identified late, after the design is largely frozen?
- How do you keep the risk file and the DHF from drifting out of sync as the design changes?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?
**Answer:** I'd start by separating the two questions that are getting tangled: whether the hazard is genuinely negligible, and whether the proposed control is the right response to it. On the first, I'd ask the quality manager to walk through the risk estimation with the team — the severity, the probability of occurrence, and the detectability — and check whether the disagreement is about the numbers or about the assumptions behind them. Often the gap is that engineering is reasoning from the design as intended, while quality is reasoning from the design as it could be used or misused, and both perspectives belong in the analysis. On the second, I'd look for whether there's a lower-cost control that addresses the same hazard — a design change, a labeling or training control, or a verification activity — because the hierarchy of controls means the first idea isn't always the proportionate one. If, after that, the team still believes the risk is acceptable and quality still wants the control, I wouldn't simply overrule them; I'd document the disagreement, the rationale on both sides, and the decision, and escalate it through the agreed route rather than letting it sit unresolved. The schedule impact is real, but it's a consequence of the decision, not an argument against making it properly — and a documented, reasoned decision is what protects the project either way.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the control is added, how would you recover the schedule without compromising the verification work?
- How would you record a residual risk that the team accepts but a stakeholder is uncomfortable with?
