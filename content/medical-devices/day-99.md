# medical-devices — Day 99

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard, because the measurement matrix is driven entirely by that classification and by the device's means of protection. The key distinction is that BF and CF parts have different allowable leakage limits — CF is tighter because it's intended for direct cardiac contact — so the same physical measurement setup can pass for one part and fail for another. I'd build a measurement matrix with rows for each applied part and columns for the conditions the standard requires: normal condition, single-fault conditions (open earth, open one means of protection, reversed mains where applicable), and the relevant patient-source configurations (e.g., mains voltage on the applied part for the patient leakage test). Then I'd decide which measurements are actually required by working from the standard's decision tree rather than measuring everything — for example, whether the part is floating or earth-referenced, whether the device is Class I or Class II, and whether the part is B, BF, or CF type all change which tests apply and which limits apply. Practically, the measurement path itself matters: I'd use the standard's specified measuring device (the MD network) rather than a generic multimeter, keep the test leads short and consistent, and verify the setup with a known-good reference before trusting a marginal reading. I'd also document the exact configuration for each measurement in the test report, because leakage current results are only meaningful with the setup fully described — a reviewer needs to be able to reproduce the measurement. Finally, I'd treat any marginal pass as a design concern rather than a test-pass, since leakage current can drift with temperature, humidity, and component tolerances over the life of the device.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a single-fault condition needs to be tested for a given applied part, and how do you document that decision? What would you do if a BF part passes under normal condition but fails under a single-fault condition?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted emissions and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a single co-design problem rather than two separate ones, because the filtering that fixes emissions can easily damage signal integrity if it's added late. I'd start by identifying the noise sources and their frequency content — switching power supplies, digital clocks, motor drives, and any high-speed interfaces — and map those against the emission limits and the frequencies where the device's own signals of interest live. The core tension is that an EMI filter is a low-pass structure, so if the signal of interest and the noise share a band, a simple filter can't separate them; that pushes the solution toward source suppression (layout, grounding, shielding, slew-rate control) rather than filter brute force. For conducted emissions I'd work on the power input path: a properly designed common-mode and differential-mode filter with the filter's ground reference and layout treated as part of the filter, because a good filter schematic on a bad layout behaves like a different filter. For radiated emissions I'd focus on loop areas, cable routing, and shielding, since radiated emissions are largely about current loops and antenna structures rather than component values. On the signal integrity side, I'd define the passband and stopband requirements for each sensitive signal first, then verify the filter doesn't violate them — checking phase margin on feedback loops, settling time on sensor front-ends, and rise time on digital lines. I'd validate the whole thing with pre-compliance scans early, not at the formal test, and I'd keep the filter design modular so a component change during EMC debugging doesn't force a board respin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to fix an emissions problem at the source versus adding filtering? What's your approach when a filter that fixes conducted emissions makes radiated emissions worse?

## Q3: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I'd start from the device's intended use and clinical function, because the particular standards are organized by device type and function, not by product name. For a combined-function device I'd identify each function separately, find the particular standard that governs each one, and then determine whether the device is covered by one standard that already anticipates the combination or whether both apply. The key principle is that the general standard (60601-1) applies always, and particular standards add to or modify it — they don't replace it — so the baseline requirements stay in force and the particular standards layer on top. Where two particular standards both apply and appear to conflict, I'd read them carefully to see whether the conflict is real or apparent: often one standard is more specific about a requirement and the other is silent, in which case the more specific one governs. If there's a genuine conflict, I'd document the conflict, the interpretation I'm applying, and the rationale, and I'd engage the test lab and possibly the regulatory body early rather than discovering the conflict at submission. I'd also check the collateral standards — 60601-1-2 for EMC, 60601-1-6 for usability, 60601-1-8 for alarms, 60601-1-11 for home use — since those apply based on environment and features rather than device type, and a combined-function device often triggers several of them. The output of this exercise is a compliance matrix that maps every applicable requirement to how the device meets it, which becomes the backbone of the verification plan.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a particular standard's requirement is stricter than what the device's clinical function actually needs? What would you do if the test lab and your team disagreed on which particular standards apply?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** I'd structure it so the risk management file is a living part of the design process, not a retrospective artifact. The foundation is a hazard analysis that's built early — from intended use, foreseeable misuse, and the device's architecture — and then maintained as a traceable structure: each hazard links to its causes, the risk estimate, the risk control measure, the verification that the control was implemented, and the residual risk evaluation. The key to keeping it useful is making that traceability bidirectional and queryable, so when a design change happens you can ask "which hazards does this touch?" and "which risk controls does this invalidate?" rather than re-reading a static document. I'd integrate it with the design history file so that risk control verification evidence lives alongside the design verification evidence, and I'd hold risk reviews at the same cadence as design reviews so the file is updated at the point of change rather than at the end. I'd also keep the risk estimates and the acceptance criteria explicit and versioned, because residual risk acceptance is a judgment that needs to be defensible later — a reviewer should be able to see what was known, what was decided, and why. Practically, I'd avoid a single monolithic spreadsheet in favor of a structured tool or a well-indexed set of linked documents, and I'd make sure the file captures the risk management plan up front, since the plan defines the scope, the methods, and the acceptance criteria that everything else is measured against.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design change late in the project that invalidates a previously accepted residual risk? What's the minimum content a risk management file needs to be considered complete and auditable?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a disagreement about risk estimation and acceptance criteria rather than a scheduling argument, because the schedule impact is a consequence, not the root of the disagreement. First I'd make sure we're actually disagreeing about the same thing: I'd ask the quality manager to walk through the hazard, the sequence of events, the severity and probability estimates, and the acceptance criteria being applied, and I'd do the same from the engineering side. Often the gap is that engineering is estimating probability from field experience while quality is applying a conservative worst-case estimate, or the two sides are using different severity scales. If the estimates genuinely differ, I'd resolve it with evidence — fault tree analysis, FMEA, historical data, or a targeted test — rather than by seniority. If after that the hazard still lands in a region where the acceptance criteria require a control, then the control goes in and the schedule conversation happens separately; if it lands in an acceptable region, I'd document the rationale and the acceptance decision so it's defensible to a reviewer. The one thing I wouldn't do is let the schedule pressure drive the risk decision, because that's exactly the failure mode a quality system is designed to prevent. I'd also look for a control that addresses the hazard at lower cost — sometimes a labeling or usability control satisfies the requirement where a hardware change wouldn't be needed — but I'd be honest that this is a design decision, not a way to make the disagreement go away.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's estimate was based on a standard's conservative default rather than device-specific data? How would you escalate if the disagreement couldn't be resolved at the design review?
