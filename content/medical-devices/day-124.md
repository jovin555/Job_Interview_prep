# medical-devices — Day 124

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement matrix, because the required measurements fall out of the classification and the supply configuration rather than from a generic checklist. For a device with both BF and CF applied parts, the CF part carries the tighter limits, so the measurement plan has to treat each applied part individually — you can't average or pool them. The measurement path itself needs to present the correct impedance to the circuit under test: the measuring device (MD) is defined by the standard, and the test setup must connect the applied part to the MD through the specified network, with the device powered in its intended configuration and then repeated under single-fault conditions (e.g., protective earth open, one means of patient protection defeated). I'd decide which measurements are required by working through: patient leakage current from each applied part to earth (normal and single-fault), patient leakage current between applied parts where the standard requires it (especially relevant when a BF and CF part could be touched simultaneously), and the mains-to-applied-part path. The key discipline is to build a measurement matrix — rows for each applied part, columns for normal/single-fault and for each relevant leakage type — and to trace each cell back to the clause that requires it, so nothing is measured "just because" and nothing required is skipped. I'd also verify the test fixture itself doesn't load the circuit: the MD's input impedance and the wiring to the applied part can dominate a low-current measurement, so I'd characterize the setup with a known source before trusting readings near the limit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where the BF and CF applied parts are galvanically connected to each other internally — does that change the measurement matrix? What would you do if a measurement sits within 10% of the limit but technically passes?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is a strong clue that something in the device is resonant or that a specific coupling path becomes efficient at that frequency — it's rarely a broadband susceptibility problem. I'd first confirm the failure is genuinely frequency-specific and not an artifact of the test setup: repeat with the device in a different orientation, with cables dressed differently, and with the field probe in a different position, because a single-frequency failure can be a standing-wave or cable-resonance effect in the chamber rather than the device itself. If it reproduces, I'd characterize the coupling path: is the disturbance entering through an enclosure seam, a cable (patient cable, power cable, display interconnect), or a PCB trace acting as an antenna? A quick way to localize is to repeat the test with cables replaced by shielded or shortened variants, and with the enclosure temporarily taped or shielded at suspect seams — if the failure moves or disappears, you've found the path. On the PCB, I'd look for structures whose physical length corresponds to a half- or quarter-wavelength at the offending frequency, and for high-impedance nodes (sensor inputs, reference pins, reset lines) that are poorly bypassed. Resolution usually falls into: improving shielding/grounding at the seam or connector, adding or resizing ferrites and feed-through capacitors on the offending cable, tightening decoupling on the susceptible node, or adding firmware-level filtering/digital debouncing if the disturbance is corrupting a sampled signal rather than causing a hard fault. I'd verify the fix at the exact frequency and at the band edges, and re-run the full sweep to make sure the fix didn't shift the problem elsewhere.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a cable-resonance effect from a genuine device susceptibility in the chamber? If the fix is a firmware filter, how do you demonstrate it doesn't compromise the device's specified response time?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file stays useful only if it's built as a living traceability structure rather than a retrospective narrative. I'd set it up at project kickoff with the hazard analysis as the spine: each hazard links forward to the risk estimate, the risk control measure, the verification evidence that the control was implemented and effective, and the residual risk evaluation — and each of those links is a live reference, not a copy. The practical discipline is that the risk file is updated at the same cadence as design reviews, so every design change triggers a risk review question: does this change introduce a new hazard, alter an existing risk estimate, or invalidate a control? I'd keep the hazard log in a tool that supports linking (even a well-structured spreadsheet with stable IDs works if disciplined), so that when a design output changes, you can query which hazards and controls are affected. Two things make the file auditable at the end: stable identifiers that never get reused, and a change history that shows the file evolved with the design rather than being back-filled. I'd also separate the risk management plan (what will be done, by whom, when) from the risk management report (the summary at the end) so the plan can be revised without rewriting the whole file. The test of a good file is whether an auditor can pick any hazard and follow it to evidence in a few minutes — if that trace is hard to follow, the file isn't doing its job regardless of how complete it looks.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard that's identified late, after the design is largely frozen — where does it enter the file and how do you keep the trace coherent? What's the minimum set of links you'd insist on for every risk control?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means a software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation, so the verification has to demonstrate both that the requirements are correctly implemented and that the software behaves safely under fault and abnormal conditions — not just that it works in the nominal case. I'd structure verification around the software requirements specification: every requirement gets a verification method (test, analysis, inspection, or review) and evidence, with bidirectional traceability so you can show both "every requirement is verified" and "every test traces to a requirement." For Class C specifically, I'd emphasize: unit-level verification of safety-related functions (with coverage analysis appropriate to the classification), integration testing of the interfaces between software items, and system-level testing that exercises the safety-related functions in the context of the device. Beyond nominal testing, I'd include fault-injection and abnormal-condition testing — watchdog behavior, memory corruption, sensor faults, timing overruns — because the standard's concern is failure modes, not just functional correctness. I'd also verify the software architecture against its design description, confirm the risk control measures implemented in software are effective (linking back to the risk file), and check that the development process artifacts — configuration management, problem resolution, change control — are consistent with the classification. The release decision should be gated on a documented verification summary that maps evidence to requirements and to risk controls, with any open anomalies assessed for safety impact before release.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide how much structural coverage analysis is enough for a Class C item? How do you handle a safety-related requirement that can only be partially verified by test and partly by analysis?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a disagreement about the risk estimate and the acceptability criteria, not as a scheduling fight, because the quality manager is accountable for the risk file and dismissing the concern outright would undermine that. First I'd get the disagreement onto paper: what is the hazard, what is the team's severity and probability estimate, what is the quality manager's, and where exactly do the estimates diverge? Often the gap is in probability — engineering may be reasoning from bench experience while quality is reasoning from worst-case use or from a broader population — and that's a productive conversation. I'd bring the risk acceptance criteria into the room: if the team's estimate genuinely places the risk in the acceptable region per the agreed criteria, that's the argument to make, with evidence. If the quality manager still wants the control, I'd evaluate the control on its merits — does it actually reduce the risk meaningfully, is it verifiable, and what does it cost in schedule and complexity? Then I'd look for the smallest effective version of the control rather than accepting or rejecting the largest one. If we still disagree after that, I'd escalate through the defined path (design review chair, then management with risk acceptance authority) rather than letting it stall, and I'd document the decision and rationale either way. The schedule impact is real, but it's a consequence of the decision, not an argument against considering the risk — I'd rather absorb a documented delay than ship with an unresolved risk disagreement.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's concern is valid but the proposed control introduces a new hazard of its own? How would you keep the engineering team's morale intact if the decision goes against their recommendation?
