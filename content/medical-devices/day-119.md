# medical-devices — Day 119

## Q1: How would you approach designing a battery-backed subsystem so that a device's real-time clock and its logged physiological data survive a full main-battery removal, without introducing a new single point of failure into the safety architecture?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating the two concerns, because they have different criticality and different failure modes. The RTC needs a tiny, long-lived backup source — typically a coin cell or a supercapacitor — sized against the worst-case time the device could sit unpowered between uses, with derating for self-discharge and temperature. The logged data needs non-volatile storage that is written atomically, so a power loss mid-write cannot leave a half-updated record. Those are different problems and I'd design them independently.

For the RTC, the key design question is what happens when the backup source itself fails or is depleted. The device must be able to detect that the time is no longer trustworthy — a validity flag set on first power-up and cleared only by a deliberate time-set action — and it must have a defined behavior when time is invalid: either refuse to timestamp, or timestamp with an explicit "time uncertain" marker, rather than silently logging plausible-but-wrong times. That's a risk-management decision as much as a hardware one, because time-stamped data may feed clinical decisions.

For the data, I'd use a journaling or copy-on-write scheme with a checksum per record and a commit marker written last, so a torn write is detectable and discardable on recovery. I'd also bound the write frequency and consider wear leveling if the storage is flash.

On the "new single point of failure" concern: the backup source must not be able to take down the main function. That means isolating it — a diode-OR or load switch so a shorted backup cell cannot drag the main rail down — and making sure the RTC and storage recovery paths are exercised by test, including the case where the backup is dead and the case where power is lost mid-write. I'd verify both with fault injection rather than assuming.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether an invalid timestamp should suppress an alarm or merely annotate it? What test would convince you the atomic-write scheme actually works under a real brownout rather than a simulated one?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device's software needs Class A, B, or C under IEC 62304, and what would you do if the hardware and software leads disagreed on the classification?

**Answer:** The classification follows from the risk analysis, not from the software team's intuition. The question IEC 62304 asks is: can a software failure contribute to a hazardous situation, and if so, is the resulting harm non-serious (Class B) or serious/death (Class C)? Class A is reserved for software that cannot contribute to a hazardous situation at all. So the correct process is to start from the hazard analysis, trace each hazardous situation back to whether software could cause or fail to prevent it, and let that drive the class.

In practice the disagreement usually comes from two people reasoning about different things. The hardware lead may be thinking "the hardware watchdog catches it," and the software lead may be thinking "the software drives the alarm, so it's Class C." Both may be partly right. The resolution is to make the argument explicit and documented: for each hazardous situation, what external risk control (hardware interlock, independent watchdog, mechanical limit) reduces the software's contribution, and is that control itself credited in the risk file? If a hardware control is credited, the software class can legitimately drop — but only if the control is genuinely independent and its own failure is analyzed.

If the two leads still disagree after that, I'd escalate to the risk management owner and, if needed, the quality/regulatory function, because this is a risk-acceptance question, not an engineering preference. I'd also note that the conservative choice is usually cheaper than the argument: classifying higher than strictly necessary costs documentation effort, while classifying too low and being wrong costs a re-submission. I'd frame the decision that way and let the risk file record the rationale either way.

**Possible follow-ups:** If a hardware watchdog is credited as a risk control, what does that imply about how the watchdog itself must be developed and verified? How would you handle a case where the classification changes mid-project because a new hazard is identified?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2 while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a single problem with two constraints pulling in opposite directions, and resolve it by frequency separation and by putting the filters where they do the most good with the least signal damage.

The first move is to understand the noise sources and their spectra before adding any components. Switching power supplies, digital clocks, and motor drives are the usual suspects, and each has a characteristic frequency range. Conducted emissions are dominated by lower-frequency differential-mode noise on the power leads plus common-mode noise; radiated emissions are driven by high-frequency currents on cables and loops acting as antennas. Knowing which source maps to which limit tells you whether you need a differential-mode filter, a common-mode choke, shielding, or layout changes — and layout is usually the cheapest and most effective fix.

For the power input, I'd design a staged filter: a common-mode choke and X/Y capacitors at the connector, then local decoupling at each load, with the filter's corner frequency chosen to sit below the switching fundamental but above the load transients the supply must deliver. The trap is over-filtering — too much inductance or capacitance can cause stability problems with the supply's control loop or inrush issues, so I'd check the filter against the supply's loop response, not just its attenuation curve.

For signal integrity, the rule is to keep the filter out of the signal band. Analog front-ends get their own local filtering close to the sensor, referenced to a clean analog ground, and I'd keep the digital return currents physically away from the analog region so the emissions filter doesn't become a coupling path. If a shared filter would compromise a sensitive measurement, I'd rather add a dedicated low-dropout regulator or an isolation barrier than compromise the analog path.

Finally, I'd verify iteratively — pre-compliance scans early, then targeted fixes — rather than designing the filter once and hoping. Emissions problems are almost always found empirically, and the design should leave room for a ferrite bead or capacitor that can be added or removed without a board spin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a common-mode choke belongs on the power input or on the signal cable? What would you do if the filter that fixes emissions also degrades a sensor's noise floor below its accuracy spec?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes on the bench is the most informative clue — it means the discrepancy is context-dependent, so my first job is to characterize what's different between the field and the bench. I'd resist the temptation to assume the sensor is wrong; a consistent offset in one direction usually points to a systematic physical or procedural cause rather than random drift.

I'd work through the plausible causes in order of likelihood. First, thermal: is the sensor actually measuring what the clinician thinks it's measuring? A patient-contacting sensor has a thermal path to the patient, but also to the device housing, the ambient air, and the clinician's hand. If the sensor is poorly coupled to the patient or is being read before it has equilibrated, it will read toward ambient or toward the device's own self-heating. Self-heating from the electronics is a real effect in a sealed enclosure and would show up as a positive offset that disappears when the device is tested in still air on a bench. Second, procedural: is the reference thermometer being used correctly, and are the two measurements taken at the same site and time? A reference reading at a different body site can legitimately differ. Third, calibration: the bench check may only verify the electronics against a simulated sensor input, not the full sensor-plus-thermal-path chain, so a sensor or assembly issue could pass the electronic check.

To separate these, I'd want field data: the actual readings, the ambient conditions, how long the device had been running, and where on the patient it was placed. I'd try to reproduce the offset in the lab by running the device in a controlled thermal environment with a known reference and by testing the sensor's response time and self-heating. If the offset reproduces only in the enclosure or only after warm-up, that points to thermal design; if it reproduces only with a particular sensor batch, that points to a component or assembly issue.

The corrective action depends on the root cause, but the investigation has to be documented as a formal complaint investigation with the risk file updated if the offset could affect clinical decisions. I'd also check whether the device's accuracy specification and its instructions for use adequately describe the required equilibration time and placement, because a usability or labeling gap is a legitimate root cause.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a test that distinguishes sensor self-heating from a genuine patient-temperature difference? If the root cause turns out to be an instructions-for-use gap rather than a hardware fault, what does the corrective action look like?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard the engineering team considers negligible, while the schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a disagreement about risk acceptability, not about engineering facts, and try to resolve it on those terms rather than by arguing about the schedule. The quality manager is usually applying a defensible principle — if a hazard is identified, it should be evaluated and either controlled or explicitly accepted with rationale — and dismissing it as "negligible" without documentation is exactly the kind of gap that causes problems later.

My first step is to get the disagreement into the risk file where it can be examined. What is the hazard, what is the sequence of events, what is the severity and probability, and what does the risk matrix say? Often the engineering team and the quality manager are estimating probability differently, and putting the estimate on paper with its assumptions exposes whether "negligible" is actually supported or is an intuition. If the risk file already covers this hazard with a documented rationale for acceptance, I'd show that. If it doesn't, the quality manager has a point regardless of the schedule.

If the risk estimate genuinely supports acceptance, I'd propose documenting the acceptance with rationale and moving on, and I'd ask the quality manager what evidence would change their mind. If they still want a control, I'd look for the cheapest effective one — sometimes a labeling or software change is far cheaper than a hardware change, and the risk reduction may be achievable without the schedule hit the team is worried about. If the only effective control is expensive, I'd bring the trade-off to the project decision-maker with the risk file in hand, because accepting schedule risk to avoid a documented hazard is a decision above my pay grade and should be made explicitly, not by default.

What I would not do is let the disagreement stall the review. I'd record it as an open action with an owner and a date, and keep the rest of the review moving.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality manager's requested control would itself introduce a new hazard? What would you do if the project decision-maker overruled the quality manager and accepted the risk — what would you want documented?
