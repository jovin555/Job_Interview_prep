# medical-devices — Day 62

## Q1: How would you approach designing the analog front-end for a patient-connected biosensor whose signal of interest is a fraction of a millivolt riding on a baseline that can drift by hundreds of millivolts, while keeping the design verifiable against a specified accuracy limit?
**Answer:** The core problem is dynamic range: the signal of interest is tiny relative to the baseline and any common-mode or offset error, so the first decision is where to remove the baseline — in the analog domain, the digital domain, or both. A common approach is a high-impedance instrumentation amplifier front-end with good CMRR to reject common-mode, followed by AC coupling or a high-pass stage to strip the slowly varying baseline before the signal reaches the ADC. That lets you apply gain to the small signal without saturating on the baseline. The trade-off is that AC coupling introduces a high-pass corner, which distorts very low-frequency content and can cause baseline wander if the corner is set too high; if the clinical parameter genuinely has DC or near-DC content, you may need a different strategy such as a servo/DC-restore loop or a higher-resolution ADC with enough headroom to digitize the full baseline and remove it digitally. On the digital side, you'd want the ADC resolution and reference stability to be adequate for the post-gain signal, and you'd want to characterize offset, gain, and noise so the error budget is traceable to the accuracy specification. For verifiability, I'd define the accuracy limit as an error budget broken into front-end gain/offset error, noise, drift over temperature and time, and ADC contributions, then design a test that injects known small signals on known baselines and confirms the end-to-end error stays within budget. I'd also make sure the design can be calibrated or trimmed if the uncalibrated error is too large, and document which errors are calibrated out versus which must be bounded by design.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between AC coupling and a digital baseline-removal approach for a specific signal? How would you verify the front-end's noise performance in a way that's meaningful for the accuracy spec?

## Q2: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?
**Answer:** I'd start by mapping each clinical function to its likely particular standard, using the scope clause of each candidate standard rather than assuming from the device's marketing category. A combined-function device can fall under more than one particular standard, and the correct approach is to identify all that apply and then determine how they interact. Where two particular standards both apply, you generally have to satisfy both; where they appear to conflict, the resolution is usually that the more specific or more stringent requirement governs, but that's a judgment you confirm with the test lab and, if needed, the regulatory body rather than deciding unilaterally. The general standard (60601-1) and collateral standards (like 60601-1-2 for EMC, 60601-1-6 for usability, 60601-1-8 for alarms) apply on top of the particulars, and the particulars typically modify or add to the general requirements rather than replace them. Practically, I'd build a requirements matrix listing each applicable clause, its source standard, and how the combined device satisfies it, flag any clauses where the two functions impose different limits, and document the rationale for how each conflict was resolved. That matrix also becomes the backbone of the test plan and the DHF traceability, so it's worth doing carefully up front rather than discovering a gap during testing.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a particular standard's requirement seems to make a feature of the other function unusable? Who would you involve in resolving an apparent conflict between two applicable standards?

## Q3: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?
**Answer:** The goal is that any design input can be traced forward to the output that satisfies it and the verification evidence that confirms it, and any output or test can be traced back to the input or requirement that justifies it. I'd set up the traceability structure early, before the design is frozen, because retrofitting it later is where projects get into trouble. Practically that means a requirements management approach — whether a dedicated tool or a controlled spreadsheet — where each design input has a unique identifier, each design output references the inputs it addresses, and each verification/validation activity references the outputs and inputs it covers. The key discipline is that traceability is updated as part of the change process, not as a separate documentation exercise: when a requirement changes, the change record should identify the affected outputs, tests, and risk items, and those get updated in the same change. For maintainability, I'd keep the traceability matrix generated or derived from the source data where possible rather than hand-maintained, so it can't silently drift out of sync. I'd also link traceability to risk management, so risk control measures trace to the requirements and verification that demonstrate their effectiveness. During design reviews, I'd use the matrix as a check: are there inputs with no verification, outputs with no parent input, or tests that verify nothing? Those gaps are the ones that cause problems at audit or submission.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a late design change that invalidates a set of already-completed verification tests? What would you do if you inherited a project with poor traceability partway through?

## Q4: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?
**Answer:** A narrow-band, reproducible failure is a strong clue that something in the device is resonant or that a specific coupling path is efficient at that frequency — it's usually not a broad susceptibility problem. I'd first characterize the failure precisely: at what field strength and modulation does it occur, what is the actual malfunction (reset, corrupted reading, communication dropout, display artifact), and does it depend on device orientation, cable routing, or the state of the device. That tells me whether the coupling is into a cable (common-mode current on an I/O or power lead), into an enclosure seam or aperture, or directly into a trace or component on the board. Then I'd try to localize it: does the failure persist with cables shortened or rerouted, with a ferrite clamped on a specific cable, with the enclosure partially shielded, or with a specific subsystem disabled? That's a fast way to identify the dominant path. Once localized, the fix depends on the mechanism — a cable-borne issue might be addressed with common-mode chokes, improved cable shielding or grounding, or filtering at the connector; a board-level resonance might need layout changes, added filtering, or a change in the affected circuit's impedance. I'd also check whether the frequency corresponds to a known resonance — a cable length, a slot dimension, or an LC in the design — because understanding the mechanism makes the fix more robust than trial-and-error. Throughout, I'd keep the fix aligned with the device's own signal integrity and safety requirements, since a filter that fixes immunity can degrade signal quality or leakage performance.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a fix is robust enough to survive production variation rather than just passing the one test? What role does the test lab's setup play in whether a narrow-band failure is real or an artifact?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a disagreement about risk acceptability, not about whether the quality manager is being unreasonable, and I'd work the process rather than argue positions. First, I'd make sure we're actually looking at the same risk estimate — often the disagreement comes from different assumptions about severity, probability of occurrence, or detectability, and getting those explicit can either resolve the gap or clarify exactly where we differ. I'd ask the quality manager to walk through their reasoning and the harm scenario they're concerned about, and I'd walk through the engineering team's basis for the lower estimate. If the estimates still differ, the right move is to apply the organization's risk acceptability criteria consistently: if the risk falls in an acceptable region per the defined criteria, the decision to add a control is a judgment call that should be documented with rationale; if it's in a region where controls are required, then the schedule impact doesn't change the obligation. I'd also consider whether there's a lower-cost control that addresses the concern — sometimes the disagreement is really about the specific proposed measure, not about whether any control is needed. If we still can't agree, I'd escalate through the defined path (design review board, risk management lead, or management) rather than letting it stall, and I'd document the decision and its rationale either way. The schedule pressure is real, but it can't be the deciding factor on a safety question — the honest framing to the team is that we either justify the risk as acceptable or we control it, and we do that on the merits.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality manager's concern was based on a harm scenario the engineering team believed was not physically possible? What would you do if the risk acceptability criteria themselves were ambiguous or not well defined?
