# medical-devices — Day 98

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The first step is to read the applied-part definitions and the measurement requirements from the general standard rather than working from memory, because the limits and the measurement conditions differ by classification and by whether the part is intended for direct cardiac contact. CF-type parts carry the tightest patient leakage limits, BF-type parts are less restrictive, and the measurement network and the conditions under which you apply it (normal condition versus single-fault condition, and which fault) are prescribed. So the design of the measurement path starts with the standard, not with the instrument.

Practically, the measurement path needs to be able to inject the specified test voltage and measure the leakage current through the specified measuring device (the standard's patient leakage measurement network) between the applied part and earth, and between applied parts where required. The key design considerations are: the path must not itself load or bypass the isolation barrier being measured; the return path must be well-defined so you're measuring the current you think you're measuring; and the setup must be repeatable so that a marginal pass doesn't flip to a fail on a different day or a different operator. For a device with both BF and CF parts, you need to be able to isolate each applied part individually — you can't measure a combined leakage and attribute it correctly. That usually means a test fixture or a harness that lets you connect the measurement network to one applied part at a time while the others are left in their normal connection state, and a documented procedure for which parts are connected, floating, or referenced during each measurement.

Deciding which measurements are required is a matter of mapping the standard's clauses to the device's actual applied-part configuration and its intended use. You enumerate each applied part, its classification, whether it's intended for direct cardiac contact, and then list every measurement the standard requires for that combination under normal and single-fault conditions. That enumeration becomes the test matrix. The trap is assuming that because a part is "just a sensor" it doesn't need the CF-level treatment — the classification follows the intended application and the risk analysis, not the connector type. I'd also cross-check the matrix against the risk management file, because a hazard that the standard doesn't explicitly measure may still need a verification activity.

A second practical point: the measurement path and the device's own isolation design have to be considered together. If the isolation barrier is marginal, no amount of careful measurement will make it pass; the measurement just tells you where you stand. So I'd want the leakage measurement to be planned early enough that a failing result still leaves time to change the barrier, the Y-capacitor selection, or the creepage, rather than discovering it at the formal test house.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a given applied part should be classified as BF or CF when the intended use is ambiguous? What would you do if a measurement passes at the test house but fails when repeated in your own lab?

## Q2: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes calibration on return is the most important clue, because it tells you the discrepancy is probably not a simple offset error in the sensor or its electronics — it's something about the field condition, the measurement setup, or the way the reading is interpreted. So I'd resist the temptation to "fix the calibration" and instead treat it as a discrepancy investigation with two parallel threads: what's different about the field measurement, and what's different about the returned device's state.

On the field side, I'd want to understand the reference method precisely. Where is the reference thermometer placed relative to the device's sensing element? A patient-contacting temperature sensor and a reference thermometer rarely measure the exact same point, and a consistent offset can come from thermal gradients, from the sensor's thermal mass and time constant, from the reference being measured at a different site, or from the sensor being influenced by the patient's position or by an adjacent heat source. I'd also ask whether the discrepancy is present at the moment of measurement or develops over time, and whether it's consistent across patients or concentrated in a subset. A consistent offset across many patients points toward a systematic difference in measurement site or method; a scattered offset points toward device variability or handling.

On the device side, "passes calibration" needs to be unpacked. What does the calibration check actually verify — a single-point check at one temperature, a multi-point check, a check of the electronics only with a simulated sensor input, or a check with the actual sensing element? If the calibration procedure substitutes a simulated input for the real sensor, it verifies the electronics but not the sensor or its thermal contact. I'd want to see the returned device's as-found data, the calibration procedure, and whether the check covers the temperature range and the conditions of use. I'd also look at whether the returned device's sensor shows any signs of drift, contamination, or mechanical damage that a bench check might not exercise.

Then I'd try to reproduce the field condition: measure the device against a traceable reference at multiple temperatures, with the sensor in a setup that mimics patient contact, and compare the device's reading to the reference at the same physical location. If the discrepancy reproduces, it's a device or design issue; if it doesn't, it's more likely a field measurement-method or use-condition issue, which is still a real problem because clinicians are making decisions on that reading.

The corrective action depends on which thread explains it. If it's a measurement-site or method difference, the fix may be in the instructions for use, the sensor placement guidance, or the device's displayed value and its stated accuracy. If it's a device issue that only appears in the field, it may be a handling, cleaning, or environmental effect that the bench check doesn't capture, and the fix is in the design or the labeling. Either way, I'd document the investigation, the as-found and as-returned data, and the rationale for the conclusion, because this is exactly the kind of complaint that a regulator or a notified body may ask about later.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a genuine device drift from a difference in measurement site? What would you do if the discrepancy only appears in a subset of devices and you can't reproduce it on any returned unit?

## Q3: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes over the course of a project?

**Answer:** I'd treat traceability as a living structure that's created alongside the design, not a matrix that's assembled at the end. The practical way to do that is to give every design input a stable identifier the moment it's captured, and to require that every design output, verification activity, and validation activity reference the input identifiers it addresses. If the identifiers are stable and the references are mandatory, the traceability is a byproduct of doing the work rather than a separate documentation exercise.

The structure I'd aim for is: design inputs (user needs and the derived technical requirements, each with an ID and a rationale), design outputs (specifications, drawings, software requirements, each linked to the inputs it satisfies), verification (each test or analysis linked to the output and input it verifies, with the result and the acceptance criterion), and validation (linked to the user needs and the intended use). The risk management file sits alongside this and cross-references the same identifiers, so a hazard maps to the requirement that controls it and to the verification that confirms the control. That cross-linking is what makes the DHF useful during a design review or an audit — you can start from a hazard and walk to the evidence, or start from a test and walk back to the requirement it satisfies.

The hard part is change. When a design input changes, the traceability has to show what changed, why, and which outputs and verifications are affected. I'd handle that with a change control process that requires the change request to identify the affected input IDs, and a review step that confirms every downstream artifact has been updated or explicitly assessed as unaffected. The traceability matrix should be regenerated or reviewed at each design review, not just at the end, so that gaps are caught while there's still time to close them. A matrix that's only reviewed at the end tends to reveal orphaned requirements and untested outputs too late.

I'd also keep the granularity sensible. Too coarse and the matrix is useless for impact analysis; too fine and it becomes a maintenance burden that people stop updating. The right level is usually the requirement or the test case, not every line of code. And I'd make the tooling do as much of the linking as possible — a requirements management tool or a structured spreadsheet with enforced ID references — because manual cross-referencing is where traceability breaks down.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design input that's discovered late and has no corresponding output yet? What would you do if you found an orphaned design output with no traceable input during a design review?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The decision should be driven by the risk analysis, not by intuition about how bad the failure "feels." The question I'd ask is: if this failure occurs, what is the resulting hazardous situation, and what is the severity of the harm that could follow? If the failure can contribute to a hazardous situation with unacceptable severity or probability, it's safety-related and needs a risk control and the associated software rigor. If it can't — because the failure is detected and handled, because the device fails safe, or because the consequence is limited to loss of a convenience feature — then it's a usability or reliability issue and should be handled through those processes, but it still needs to be documented and assessed.

The practical method is to trace the failure mode through the software architecture to the hazardous situation. That means understanding what the software function does, what happens when it fails, whether there's an independent means of detecting or mitigating the failure, and whether the failure can propagate to a safety-critical function. A failure in a data-logging task that has no path to the therapy or monitoring function is different from a failure in the same task that starves the safety-critical task of CPU time or corrupts shared memory. So the classification depends on the architecture and the failure's reach, not just on the function's nominal purpose.

I'd also be careful about the "it's just a usability issue" conclusion, because usability problems can be safety-related when they lead a clinician to take an unsafe action. A confusing alarm or a misleading display can contribute to a hazardous situation just as surely as a numerical error. So the assessment has to consider the user as part of the system, and the risk analysis should capture use errors as hazards in their own right.

Once classified, the rigor follows: safety-related failures get the software safety classification, the architectural mitigations, the verification depth, and the traceability that the standard requires; non-safety failures get handled through the normal defect and reliability processes. The important discipline is to make the classification decision explicitly and document the rationale, so that it can be reviewed and challenged. A failure that's quietly reclassified as "not safety-related" because the schedule is tight is exactly the kind of decision that a design review or an audit should catch.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a failure that's safety-related only in combination with another independent failure? What would you do if the risk analysis and the software team disagreed on whether a particular failure mode is safety-related?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd start by making sure we're actually disagreeing about the same thing, because "negligible" and "needs a control" are often shorthand for different assessments of the same hazard. I'd ask the quality manager to walk through the hazard, the hazardous situation, the severity and probability estimates, and the basis for their concern, and I'd ask the engineering team to do the same for their "negligible" assessment. Very often the disagreement turns out to be about a missing failure mode, a different assumption about the use environment, or a different reading of the risk acceptability criteria — and once that's surfaced, the path forward is clearer.

If the disagreement is genuine, I'd anchor the discussion in the risk management plan and the acceptability criteria that were agreed at the start of the project. The question isn't whether the team "feels" the risk is negligible; it's whether the estimated risk, against the agreed criteria, is acceptable. If the quality manager believes the estimate is wrong, the productive move is to challenge the estimate with evidence — a different probability basis, a missed sequence of events, a relevant field data point — rather than to argue about the conclusion. If the estimate holds up against the criteria, then the case for adding a control is weaker, and the conversation shifts to whether the criteria themselves should be revisited, which is a management decision, not an engineering one.

If the quality manager's concern is legitimate but the proposed control is expensive, I'd look for a control that addresses the hazard at lower cost — a design change that's cheaper than the proposed one, a labeling or training control, or an architectural change that makes the hazardous situation less likely. The goal is to reduce the risk to an acceptable level, not necessarily to implement the specific control that was first proposed. That reframing often dissolves the schedule conflict, because the expensive control was one option among several.

If we still can't agree, I'd escalate through the defined path rather than let it stall the review. That means documenting both positions, the evidence for each, and the decision that's needed, and taking it to the design review board or the appropriate management authority. I'd also make sure the decision and its rationale are recorded in the risk management file, because whichever way it goes, the file needs to show that the hazard was considered and the decision was made deliberately. The one thing I wouldn't do is let the schedule pressure quietly override the quality manager's concern without a documented decision — that's the outcome that causes problems later, either in an audit or in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the quality manager's concern turned out to be based on a misunderstanding of the design? What would you do if the escalation authority sided with the quality manager and the schedule impact was unavoidable?
