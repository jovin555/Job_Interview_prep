# medical-devices — Day 96

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** I'd start by mapping the applied parts and their classifications against the standard's measurement matrix, because the required measurements fall out of the classification, not the other way around. For each applied part I'd identify whether it's BF or CF, whether it's floating or earth-referenced, and whether it's intended for direct cardiac contact — that last point drives the CF designation and the tighter leakage limits. Then I'd build a measurement plan covering the standard's conditions: patient leakage current from each applied part to earth, patient leakage current between applied parts (including across the BF/CF boundary, which is easy to overlook), and patient auxiliary current where the standard requires it, each under normal condition and the applicable single-fault conditions.

The practical design question is the measurement network itself. The standard defines a specific measuring device — a passive network with defined input impedance — and the measurement must be made with the device powered in its intended configuration, including any patient simulator or equivalent load. I'd design a fixture that lets me switch the measurement network between each applied part and the reference point without disturbing the setup, because hand-moving probes between measurements introduces variability and makes repeatability hard to defend in a test report. For a device with multiple applied parts, I'd also think carefully about what "simultaneously connected" means for the worst case — leakage paths can add, so the configuration that maximizes total patient current may not be the one you'd intuitively test first.

Deciding which measurements are actually required comes down to reading the particular standard alongside the general standard. The general standard gives the framework and limits; a particular standard may add, remove, or modify measurements for that device type. I'd document the rationale for each measurement included and each one deliberately excluded, because a reviewer will ask why a given configuration wasn't tested. Where a measurement is borderline or the configuration is ambiguous, I'd rather measure it and record the result than argue it's out of scope.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where an applied part can be connected to the patient while another applied part is simultaneously connected to a different patient circuit? What would you do if a particular standard's measurement requirement seemed to contradict the general standard's?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow, reproducible band is actually good news — it points to a resonance or a specific coupling path rather than a broad susceptibility problem, so I'd treat it as a signal-path investigation rather than a shielding problem. First I'd characterize the failure precisely: sweep finely around the band to find the exact center frequency and bandwidth, and note whether the malfunction tracks the field strength (suggesting a linear coupling mechanism) or has a threshold (suggesting a nonlinear junction like a semiconductor being rectified). I'd also check whether the frequency shifts when the device's enclosure, cable routing, or orientation changes — if it moves, I'm looking at a resonant structure; if it's rock-steady, I'm looking at something being demodulated.

Next I'd identify the coupling path. The usual suspects are cables acting as antennas — patient cables, power cords, USB — and the enclosure seams or apertures. I'd do a quick experiment: ferrite clamps on each cable in turn, then a temporary shield over sections of the enclosure, and see which one moves or eliminates the failure. That narrows it to conducted-in-on-a-cable versus radiated-into-the-box. If it's a cable, the fix is usually common-mode choking and better cable termination; if it's the enclosure, it's seam bonding or aperture treatment.

On the circuit side, I'd look at what's actually malfunctioning. If the failure is a reset or a communication error, I'd probe the affected net during the exposure — a scope with a field probe or a differential probe on the suspect line will often show the rectified envelope. A common finding is that a high-impedance node, an unbypassed reference, or a reset line with insufficient filtering is picking up the demodulated signal. The fix is usually local: tighten the loop area, add or resize decoupling, add a small series element plus capacitor on the affected line, or improve the ground reference at the IC. I'd verify the fix at the exact failing frequency and then re-sweep the whole band to make sure I haven't just moved the problem.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a fix that genuinely solves the susceptibility and one that merely shifts the resonant frequency outside the test band? What documentation would you want to capture from this investigation for the EMC test report and the risk file?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The failure mode I'd design against is the risk file that gets written backwards from a finished design — it looks complete but it's really a rationalization, and it falls apart under audit because the hazards don't connect to real design decisions. So I'd structure it so that risk management is a live input to design, not a retrospective output.

Concretely, I'd start with the intended use and the foreseeable misuse, because those define the harm space. From there I'd build a hazard list organized by harm rather than by component — that keeps it clinically meaningful and prevents the file from becoming a component-by-component checklist that misses system-level hazards. Each hazard gets an estimated severity and probability, and crucially, each risk control gets traced forward to a specific design element and a specific verification activity. That traceability is what makes the file auditable: for any risk control, I can point to the requirement it implements and the test that confirms it works.

The structural discipline that keeps it maintainable is treating the risk file as a set of linked registers rather than a monolithic document — hazards, risk controls, verification evidence, and residual risk evaluations, each with stable identifiers so that when a design changes I update the affected entries rather than rewriting the file. I'd also build in a review cadence: risk review at each design gate, with the file as a standing agenda item, so that new hazards surface while there's still time to act on them. And I'd make sure the risk file explicitly feeds the design inputs — a risk control that isn't reflected in a requirement is a control that won't get verified.

One thing I'd be deliberate about is the residual risk section. It's tempting to declare everything acceptable and move on, but the file is more defensible if the residual risk evaluation shows the reasoning — including where a risk was reduced as far as reasonably practicable and why further reduction wasn't justified. That's the part auditors and reviewers actually read closely.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a hazard that's identified late in the project, after the design is largely frozen? How do you keep the risk file consistent with the DHF when a design change ripples through multiple requirements?

## Q4: A clinical stakeholder requests a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a decision to be made with evidence rather than a yes/no to be negotiated. The first thing I'd do is separate the clinical need from the proposed solution — the stakeholder is asking for a specific change, but the underlying need may be addressable in more than one way. So I'd get clear on what clinical problem the change solves, how often it occurs, and what the consequence is if it isn't addressed. That reframes the conversation from "can we do this" to "what's the best way to meet this need given where we are."

Then I'd quantify the actual cost of the request honestly — not just the hardware revision, but the ripple: re-verification, re-validation, EMC and safety retesting, updated risk file, updated DHF, and the regulatory timeline impact. I'd also look for lower-cost paths: can the need be met in firmware, in a labeling or training change, in an accessory, or in a follow-on revision? Sometimes the clinical need is real but the hardware change is one of several ways to meet it, and the cheapest path is often not the one originally proposed.

With that in hand, I'd bring it to a structured decision with the clinical, quality, regulatory, and program stakeholders — presenting the options with their trade-offs rather than advocating for one. The key question I'd put on the table is whether the change addresses a safety or effectiveness gap that would block approval, or an enhancement that could be deferred. If it's the former, the schedule impact is simply the cost of a correct device and we plan around it. If it's the latter, deferring to a follow-on revision with a documented rationale is usually the right call, provided the clinical stakeholder understands and agrees.

Whatever the outcome, I'd document the decision and its rationale in the design history — a late change that's rejected without a record looks like an oversight, while a documented evaluation shows the team considered it deliberately.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the clinical stakeholder escalated the request above you? What would you do if the change was small in hardware terms but triggered a full re-test cycle because of how the device is certified?

## Q5: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware meets IEC 62304 requirements for a Class C software safety classification?

**Answer:** Class C means a software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation with unacceptable risk, so the verification approach has to demonstrate that the software is correct by design and by test, not just that it works in the nominal case. I'd structure it around the standard's required activities and make sure each one produces evidence that's traceable.

The foundation is the software requirements and architecture. For Class C, the architecture needs to show how the software is decomposed and how the safety-critical portions are separated from non-critical ones — segregation, defensive design, and where relevant, independence between channels. I'd want the architecture to make the safety argument visible: which components implement safety functions, what protects them from interference by other components, and how failures are detected and handled. That architecture then drives the detailed design and the unit-level verification.

Verification itself I'd build in layers. At unit level, I'd aim for structural coverage appropriate to Class C — statement and branch coverage at minimum, and I'd look at whether the standard's expectations for the classification push toward MC/DC for the safety-critical units. At integration level, I'd verify the interfaces and the fault-handling behavior — what happens when a component returns bad data, when a task overruns, when a communication link drops. At system level, I'd verify the safety requirements end to end, including the abnormal and fault cases, not just the happy path. Throughout, I'd keep requirements-to-test traceability so that every safety requirement has verification evidence and every test maps back to a requirement.

Two things I'd pay particular attention to for Class C. First, the problem resolution process — every defect found in verification needs to be recorded, evaluated for its safety impact, and its resolution verified, because the standard expects a closed loop. Second, the tool and configuration management: the build that's tested must be the build that's released, with the toolchain and its qualification or justification documented. For Class C, an unreproducible build is a verification gap.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you justify the level of structural coverage you chose if a reviewer challenged it? How would you handle a safety-critical unit that's difficult to test at unit level because of its hardware dependencies?
