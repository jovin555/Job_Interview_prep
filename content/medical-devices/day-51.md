# medical-devices — Day 51

## Q1: How would you approach designing the creepage and clearance distances on a medical PCB that has a mains-referenced primary side, a secondary side, and a patient-connected applied part, where the applied part must be isolated from mains by two means of patient protection (2×MOPP)?

**Answer:** I'd start by classifying the insulation barriers rather than jumping straight to a number. The requirement is set by the standard, not by preference: for a patient-connected part that must be isolated from mains by 2×MOPP, the barrier between the mains primary and the patient circuit has to be treated as two independent means of protection, each of which must individually satisfy the applicable creepage, clearance, and dielectric strength requirements for the working voltage and the pollution degree of the environment. So the first step is to draw the isolation diagram — mains primary, secondary (SELV), and the applied part — and mark exactly which barrier each conductor pair crosses. That diagram drives everything downstream.

Next I'd pull the actual limits from the standard's tables, using the correct inputs: working voltage across the barrier (not just the nominal mains voltage — I'd use the peak or RMS value the standard specifies, including any transient overvoltage category), pollution degree (typically 2 for a device in a home or clinical environment, but I'd confirm against the intended use environment), material group of the PCB laminate (CTI rating), and altitude if the device is rated for use above 2000 m, since clearance derates with altitude. I'd also check whether the standard allows any reduction for the specific construction — for example, a solid insulated barrier or a conformally coated assembly can change the calculation, but I would not assume a reduction without confirming it's permitted.

On the layout side, the practical work is: keep the creepage path along the surface as long as the table requires, which often means a slot or routed cutout in the PCB rather than just a wide gap, because a slot breaks the surface tracking path and lets you meet creepage in less board area. Clearance is the shortest path through air, so it's set by the physical gap and by any components or hardware that bridge it — I'd check that no screw, heatsink, or connector pin reduces the clearance below the required value. I'd also verify that the isolation barrier is continuous across the whole board, including under connectors and around mounting holes, because a single violation anywhere defeats the barrier.

Finally, I'd treat this as a design-verification item, not just a layout preference: the creepage/clearance distances get measured and documented, and the barrier gets dielectric-strength tested (hi-pot) at the specified test voltage. If the design uses a reinforced or double-insulation approach, I'd make sure the test and the documentation reflect that the two means of protection are genuinely independent — a single component or a single layer of insulation can't count as both.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle the case where a slot in the PCB is not practical because of mechanical or EMI constraints?
- What changes if the device is rated for operation at 3000 m altitude?

## Q2: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around a specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow-band failure is a strong hint that something in the device is resonant or that a specific coupling path is efficient at that frequency, so I'd treat it as a targeted investigation rather than a broad EMC overhaul. First I'd characterize the failure precisely: at what field strength does it start, is it amplitude-dependent or frequency-dependent, does it correlate with a particular operating mode, and what exactly is the malfunction — a reset, a sensor reading shift, a communication dropout, a display artifact? The nature of the malfunction usually points at the victim circuit.

Then I'd try to localize the coupling path. The main candidates are: radiated coupling into a cable or harness acting as an antenna, coupling into an unshielded loop on the PCB, coupling through an enclosure seam or connector, or conduction along a power or signal line that then radiates internally. A useful technique is to reproduce the failure on the bench with a signal generator and a near-field probe or a small antenna, then move the probe around the board and cables to see which area is most sensitive. If the failure follows a specific cable, that cable is the antenna; if it follows a specific IC or trace, that's the victim. I'd also check whether the frequency corresponds to a resonance — a cable length, a trace length, or an LC network — because that tells me whether the fix is to detune the structure or to harden the victim.

For resolution, the options depend on the mechanism. If it's cable coupling, I'd look at common-mode chokes, ferrites, or improved cable shielding and grounding, and I'd check that the cable's return path is well-defined. If it's PCB coupling, I'd look at loop area reduction, ground plane integrity, and whether the victim trace runs parallel to a noisy one. If it's a power rail, I'd look at decoupling and filtering at the point of entry to the sensitive circuit. If it's the enclosure, I'd look at seam gasketing or aperture size relative to the wavelength. In all cases I'd verify the fix by re-testing at the failing frequency and at the band edges, and I'd confirm the fix doesn't degrade emissions performance, since filtering and shielding choices often trade one against the other.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether to fix the victim circuit or the coupling path?
- What would you do if the fix worked on the bench but the failure reappeared at the test lab?

## Q3: How would you approach building the traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, and how would you keep it maintainable as the design changes?

**Answer:** I'd treat traceability as a living structure that's built alongside the design, not assembled at the end. The backbone is a requirements hierarchy: user needs and clinical requirements at the top, then design inputs derived from them, then design outputs (the actual specifications, schematics, firmware requirements, test methods) that satisfy each input, then verification evidence showing each output meets its input, and validation evidence showing the finished device meets the user needs. Each item gets a unique identifier, and the links between them are explicit — a design input traces down to the outputs that implement it and up to the user need it serves.

The practical discipline is to define the identifier scheme and the linking convention early, before there's a large body of documents to retrofit. I'd use a requirements management tool if one is available, but a well-structured spreadsheet or database can work if the linking is consistent and the review process is enforced. The key is that every design input has at least one output and at least one verification method, and every output traces back to at least one input — orphan inputs and orphan outputs are both red flags that a reviewer will catch.

For maintainability, the important thing is change control. When a design input changes, the change should trigger a review of the affected outputs and verification evidence, and the traceability links should be updated as part of that change, not afterward. I'd also make traceability a standing item in design reviews, so gaps are caught while the design is still fluid rather than during a submission review. And I'd keep the verification and validation evidence linked to the specific revision of the output it tested, because a test result against an obsolete revision is not evidence for the current design.

One thing I'd be careful about is not conflating verification and validation. Verification answers "did we build it right" — does the output meet the input. Validation answers "did we build the right thing" — does the device meet the user need in the intended use environment. They use different evidence and often different test populations, and the traceability structure should keep them distinct while still linking both back to the user needs.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a design input that is satisfied by a supplier's component specification rather than your own design output?
- What would you do if a verification test was performed against an earlier revision and the design has since changed in a way that shouldn't affect the result?

## Q4: A clinical stakeholder asks for a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?

**Answer:** I'd start by separating the clinical need from the proposed solution. The stakeholder is describing a problem — something about the current design is hard to use or creates a risk of misuse — and the hardware revision is one possible fix, not necessarily the only one. So the first move is to understand the underlying need: what task is difficult, who is affected, how often, and what's the consequence of getting it wrong. That framing often opens up options that don't require a hardware change, such as firmware behavior, labeling, training, or a workflow change, and it also tells me whether the issue is a genuine safety or usability concern or a preference.

If the need is real and the only viable fix is a hardware change, I'd assess the impact honestly rather than arguing from schedule. That means: what's the scope of the change, does it affect any verified or validated item, does it touch a safety-related function, does it require re-testing of EMC, safety, or usability, and does it change the regulatory submission content? I'd bring that assessment to the decision-makers — the project lead, quality, regulatory, and the clinical stakeholder — with the trade-offs laid out, because the decision to delay a submission is a business and regulatory call, not an engineering one alone.

I'd also look for a staged approach. Sometimes the full change can be deferred to a post-market revision while a smaller mitigation addresses the immediate risk — for example, a firmware guard, an additional alarm, or a revised instruction — and that mitigation gets documented as an interim control. If the change is genuinely required for safety, though, I wouldn't try to defer it; the right answer is to make the change and accept the schedule impact, because shipping a device with a known usability hazard is not a trade-off worth making.

Throughout, I'd keep the risk management file current: if the change addresses a hazard, the risk control and its verification need to be documented, and if the change is deferred, the residual risk and the rationale need to be recorded and accepted by the appropriate authority.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the clinical stakeholder and the regulatory lead disagreed on whether the change was necessary?
- What would you do if the change was small but touched a safety-related function?

## Q5: How would you approach verifying that a device's firmware correctly handles a sensor that intermittently produces readings that are within the valid range but physiologically implausible — for example, a pressure spike that is numerically possible but inconsistent with the patient's condition?

**Answer:** The core challenge is that the reading passes the range check, so a simple bounds test won't catch it. I'd approach it as a plausibility problem rather than a validity problem, and build the verification around the distinction between "in range" and "consistent with the physiological context."

First I'd define what plausibility means for the specific signal. That usually involves rate-of-change limits (a pressure can't rise faster than a physiological maximum), cross-parameter consistency (a pressure spike that isn't accompanied by a corresponding change in a related signal is suspect), and temporal consistency (a single-sample spike surrounded by normal values is more likely artifact than real event). The exact criteria come from the clinical requirements and the sensor's physical characteristics, and they need to be documented as design inputs so the verification has something to test against.

For the test strategy, I'd use a combination of injected and recorded data. Injected data lets me control the exact scenario — a single-sample spike, a sustained but implausible level, a spike that coincides with a real event — and confirm the firmware's response is deterministic. Recorded data from the intended use environment lets me check that the plausibility logic doesn't reject real events, which is the failure mode that matters most clinically: a filter that suppresses a genuine physiological event is worse than one that lets an artifact through. I'd also test the boundary cases explicitly — a reading exactly at the rate-of-change limit, a reading that's implausible in isolation but consistent with a real event, and a sequence of small implausible readings that individually pass but collectively indicate a problem.

I'd also verify the response behavior, not just the detection. When the firmware flags an implausible reading, what does it do — discard it, hold the last valid value, flag it for the clinician, trigger an alarm? Each of those has different safety implications, and the verification needs to confirm the behavior matches the requirement. And I'd check that the plausibility logic itself doesn't create a new failure mode, such as a state where the device is waiting for valid data and stops monitoring.

Finally, I'd make sure the test cases are traceable to the risk analysis. If the hazard is "clinician acts on an implausible reading," the verification needs to show the firmware prevents that, and the evidence needs to be in the DHF.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide the rate-of-change limit without suppressing genuine fast physiological events?
- How would you verify the plausibility logic doesn't introduce a new failure mode, such as a stuck state?
