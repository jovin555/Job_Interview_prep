# medical-devices — Day 26

## Q1: How would you approach designing a test strategy for verifying that a medical device's firmware correctly handles a sensor that intermittently provides valid but erroneous readings — for example, a pressure sensor that occasionally reports a spike that is within the valid range but physiologically impossible?

**Answer:** This is fundamentally a signal-validation problem that needs to be addressed at multiple layers. First, I'd establish a clear definition of "physiologically plausible" for the parameter — this should come from clinical input and be documented as a design input requirement. That definition typically includes both absolute bounds (e.g., pressure can't exceed a certain value) and rate-of-change limits (e.g., pressure can't change by more than X mmHg per second), because a single out-of-range value is easier to detect than a gradual drift.

For the test strategy, I'd structure it in three tiers. Tier one is unit-level testing of the validation algorithm itself: feed it synthetic data streams with known artifacts — spikes, dropouts, step changes, slow drifts — and verify it correctly flags or rejects each type. This is where you can exhaustively cover edge cases deterministically. Tier two is integration testing with the actual sensor driver and real hardware: inject faults at the register or bus level (e.g., corrupt I2C reads, momentarily float the sensor power rail) to verify the firmware's error-handling paths work end-to-end. Tier three is system-level testing with a hardware-in-the-loop setup that can simulate realistic physiological waveforms with superimposed artifacts, run for extended durations, and verify that the device's displayed values and alarms remain correct.

A key design decision is what the firmware should do when it detects an erroneous reading: reject it and hold the last valid value, flag it as suspect, or trigger an alarm. That decision should be driven by the risk analysis — for a monitoring device, silently discarding data could be dangerous if the sensor is actually failing, so the device should typically indicate reduced confidence in the measurement. I'd also verify that the validation logic itself can't be defeated by a specific artifact pattern — for example, a spike that occurs at exactly the sampling rate and aliases into a plausible waveform. Finally, I'd ensure the test cases are traceable back to the risk management file, so each identified failure mode has a corresponding verification activity.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide between rejecting a single erroneous sample versus applying a filter that smooths the signal, given that filtering can mask real physiological changes?
- How would you verify that the validation algorithm doesn't introduce clinically significant latency in detecting a genuine critical event?

## Q2: How would you approach developing a design verification test plan for a medical device that must operate correctly during and after exposure to a defibrillation pulse, given that the device is intended for use in a hospital environment where defibrillation may occur?

**Answer:** This is governed by IEC 60601-1 and the relevant particular standards — for monitoring devices, IEC 60601-2-25 or similar — which specify defibrillation protection requirements. The key distinction is between a device that is *intended* to withstand defibrillation (e.g., a patient monitor that remains connected during defibrillation) and one that must simply survive it without damage. The test plan needs to reflect the intended use case.

First, I'd review the applicable standard to confirm the test waveform (typically a specified voltage/energy pulse applied between the defibrillation electrodes and the device's applied parts) and the pass/fail criteria — which usually include both no damage to the device and no hazard to the patient or operator. The test plan would then cover three phases: before, during, and after the pulse. Before the pulse, the device should be operating normally and recording baseline data. During the pulse, the device may reset or temporarily lose function, but it must not create a safety hazard — for example, it must not deliver energy back through the patient connection. After the pulse, the device must either automatically recover to normal operation or require a safe, clearly indicated reset procedure, and it must not have sustained damage that could compromise future safety.

The test setup is critical: you need a defibrillation pulse generator, a calibrated test fixture that simulates the patient, and appropriate measurement equipment to verify that no hazardous voltage or current appears at the device's accessible parts during the pulse. I'd also test the device's behavior when the defibrillation pulse is applied while the device is in different operating states — normal monitoring, alarm condition, battery-powered versus mains-powered — because the failure modes can differ. Finally, I'd verify that any components specifically added for defibrillation protection (e.g., spark gaps, gas discharge tubes, series resistors) are properly rated and that the PCB layout provides adequate creepage and clearance to prevent arcing. The test results need to be documented with enough detail to demonstrate compliance to a notified body, including photographs of the setup and waveforms captured during testing.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you verify that the device's firmware correctly handles a reset that occurs during the defibrillation pulse, particularly regarding data that was being logged at the time?
- What would you do if the device passes the defibrillation test but you discover that a component in the protection circuit is operating beyond its absolute maximum ratings?

## Q3: During a design review for a medical device that uses a wireless link to transmit physiological data to a display unit, the firmware engineer proposes using an unacknowledged broadcast protocol to simplify the design, while the clinical team requires that data loss be detectable and that the display unit indicate when data is stale. How would you approach this trade-off?

**Answer:** The clinical requirement is non-negotiable — if the display unit can't distinguish between fresh and stale data, a clinician could make a decision based on outdated information. The question is how to meet that requirement with minimal protocol complexity. An unacknowledged broadcast protocol can still support staleness detection if the transmitter includes a sequence number and timestamp in each packet, and the receiver monitors the expected packet rate and sequence continuity. If packets are lost, the receiver can detect the gap and display an indicator that data is stale or incomplete. This approach doesn't require acknowledgments or retransmissions, so it preserves the simplicity the firmware engineer wants.

However, I'd push the discussion further: what is the clinical consequence of a lost packet? If the device is monitoring a critical parameter and a packet is lost, is it acceptable to simply indicate staleness, or does the system need to trigger an alarm? That's a risk-based decision. For a monitoring device where the display is the primary interface, a brief staleness indicator might be acceptable, but if the data is used for alarm generation, you need a more robust mechanism — either acknowledgments with retransmission, or a redundant path (e.g., the display unit also monitors the raw signal quality). I'd also consider the RF environment: in a hospital, wireless links can be congested, and a broadcast protocol with no retransmission might result in frequent data gaps that degrade clinical usability. A middle-ground approach is a lightweight acknowledgment for critical data (e.g., alarm events) while using unacknowledged broadcast for routine waveform data.

The design review should focus on defining the clinical requirements precisely: what is the maximum acceptable data gap, what should the display show during a gap, and what happens if the gap persists? Once those are defined, the protocol choice becomes an engineering trade-off rather than a disagreement. I'd also require that the protocol be tested under realistic RF interference conditions to verify that the staleness detection works as intended and that the display's warning is noticeable and unambiguous.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you verify that the staleness indicator is effective in practice, given that clinicians may not notice a subtle display change during a busy shift?
- What additional design considerations would you raise if the wireless link is also used for firmware updates or configuration changes?

## Q4: How would you approach developing a design verification test plan for a medical device that must measure both temperature and pressure, where the two sensors share a common ADC and multiplexer, and the device must maintain specified accuracy for both parameters simultaneously?

**Answer:** The shared ADC and multiplexer introduce a coupling mechanism between the two measurement channels, so the test plan must specifically verify that the accuracy of one channel is not degraded by the presence of a signal on the other channel. This is a classic crosstalk or channel-to-channel isolation problem. I'd start by reviewing the ADC's datasheet for the specified channel-to-channel isolation and settling time, and then design tests that stress the worst-case conditions: a large signal on one channel while a small signal is being measured on the other.

The test plan would include several categories. First, baseline accuracy testing: each channel is tested independently against a reference standard across the full specified range, with the other channel held at a fixed mid-range value. Second, crosstalk testing: one channel is driven to its maximum specified input while the other channel measures a small, precisely known signal — this is repeated with the roles reversed. The measured error on the "quiet" channel must remain within its specified accuracy. Third, multiplexer settling time testing: the ADC is switched between channels at the maximum scan rate, and the measurement error is characterized as a function of the time between channel switches. This is particularly important if the two sensors have very different source impedances — a high-impedance pressure sensor might not settle as quickly as a low-impedance temperature sensor.

I'd also include a test where both signals are varied simultaneously to verify that the device's reported values track the true values correctly — for example, a temperature ramp combined with a pressure step change. The test plan should specify the reference equipment (calibrated temperature bath and pressure standard), the number of test points, and the acceptance criteria for each test. Finally, I'd verify that the firmware's sampling sequence and any digital filtering don't introduce additional coupling — for example, if the firmware averages multiple samples, it should discard the first sample after a channel switch to allow for settling. The results should be documented in a way that demonstrates the device meets its accuracy specification under worst-case simultaneous measurement conditions.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine the maximum multiplexer scan rate that still meets the accuracy specification, and how would you verify this over the device's operating temperature range?
- What would you do if crosstalk testing reveals that the pressure channel is affected by the temperature channel's signal, given that the PCB layout is already finalized?

## Q5: You're leading a project where a field complaint reports that a medical device's rechargeable battery is swelling after 6-12 months of use in a hospital setting. The device is used for continuous patient monitoring and the battery is not user-replaceable. How would you approach the investigation and corrective action process?

**Answer:** Battery swelling is a serious safety issue — it can indicate internal short circuits, overcharging, or thermal runaway, and it poses a fire risk in a patient-care environment. The first priority is patient safety: I'd immediately assess whether any devices in the field present an imminent hazard. If the swelling is severe or there are any reports of heat, odor, or leakage, I'd recommend a field safety corrective action (FSCA) to remove affected devices from service or quarantine them. If the swelling is cosmetic and contained, I'd still issue guidance to clinical staff on what to look for and how to report it.

For the investigation, I'd structure it as a formal root-cause analysis following a systematic methodology like 8D or 5-Whys. The key questions are: Is the swelling caused by the battery cell itself, the charging circuit, the device's thermal environment, or the usage pattern? I'd start by collecting data: how many devices are affected, what's the commonality (e.g., specific serial number range, manufacturing date, battery lot), and what's the usage profile (e.g., how often is the device on charge, what's the ambient temperature in the hospital). I'd also retrieve affected devices and examine them — measure the battery voltage and internal resistance, inspect the charging circuit for signs of overvoltage or overcurrent, and check the device's firmware logs for charging behavior.

The investigation should also include a review of the battery's specification and the charging algorithm. Common causes of swelling include overcharging (charging voltage too high or termination current too low), charging at elevated temperatures, or a defective cell batch. I'd compare the device's charging profile against the battery manufacturer's recommended profile and verify that the firmware's charge termination criteria are correct. If the battery is charged continuously while the device is on mains power, the charging algorithm must handle the transition between charge and standby correctly — a failure to terminate charging can cause gradual overcharge over months.

Once the root cause is identified, the corrective action depends on the cause. If it's a firmware issue, a software update might be sufficient. If it's a hardware issue, a PCB revision or component change might be needed. If it's a cell defect, the battery supplier needs to be involved and a new lot qualified. In all cases, I'd update the risk management file to reflect the new hazard and the implemented risk controls, and I'd develop a verification plan to confirm the corrective action resolves the issue — for example, accelerated life testing of the revised charging algorithm. Finally, I'd ensure that the complaint handling process captures the outcome and that any FSCA is executed according to regulatory requirements.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine whether the swelling is a safety hazard requiring an immediate field action versus a cosmetic issue that can be monitored?
- How would you verify that the corrective action is effective, given that the failure mode takes 6-12 months to manifest in the field?
