# medical-devices — Day 127

## Q1: How would you approach designing the enclosure and internal layout of a handheld medical device so that a single mechanical drop cannot simultaneously compromise the electrical safety clearances and the patient-connected circuitry inside?

**Answer:** I'd treat drop survival and electrical safety as two requirements that must be satisfied by the same physical design, not as separate problems solved in sequence. The starting point is to define the credible mechanical event — drop height, orientation, and surface per the applicable standard or the product's use environment — and then map where energy and deformation will go. The layout principle is to keep the isolation barrier and the patient-connected front-end away from the regions most likely to deform: corners, edges, and the battery compartment. Isolation slots and creepage distances should not run along a seam or a thin wall that can flex or crack on impact.

Structurally, I'd design the enclosure so that impact energy is absorbed by sacrificial features — ribs, crush zones, compliant mounts for the PCB — rather than transmitted into the board. The PCB itself should be mounted on compliant standoffs with controlled standoff height, and heavy components (battery, transformers, large inductors) should be mechanically restrained so they can't shear off and migrate across a clearance gap. A loose battery or a detached heatsink landing across the mains-to-secondary barrier is a classic post-drop safety failure.

For the internal layout, I'd keep a defined "keep-out" band around the isolation barrier that no component, wire, or fastener can enter even under deformation, and I'd route patient-connected cabling so that a drop cannot pull it taut against a sharp internal edge. Fasteners and screws need retention features so they don't become projectiles inside the enclosure.

Verification is where this comes together: drop testing followed by a full repeat of the dielectric strength and leakage current tests, plus a visual and X-ray or cross-section inspection of the barrier region. Passing the drop and passing hi-pot separately is not sufficient — the question is whether the device still passes hi-pot *after* the drop. I'd also do a teardown review of the drop-tested units to confirm the barrier geometry is intact, because a device can pass hi-pot while having a marginal clearance that will degrade in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a cracked enclosure after drop is a cosmetic issue or a safety issue? What would you do if the drop test passed but the post-drop hi-pot margin had narrowed significantly?

## Q2: How would you approach deciding whether a device's software needs Class A, B, or C under IEC 62304, and how would you handle a disagreement between the hardware and software leads on the classification?

**Answer:** The classification is driven by hazard analysis, not by the software team's preference or by how complex the code is. The logic is: identify the hazardous situations the device can contribute to, determine which of those could result in harm, and then ask whether software could contribute to that hazardous situation. If software cannot contribute to any hazardous situation, Class A. If software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation that is not a serious injury, Class B. If software failure could contribute to a hazardous situation that could result in death or serious injury, Class C.

The key discipline is that the classification follows from the risk analysis and the device's intended use, and it should be documented with a rationale that traces back to specific hazards. A monitoring device that drives an alarm but delivers no therapy is a common point of confusion: the absence of therapy does not automatically mean Class A or B, because a missed or false alarm on a monitored physiological parameter can itself lead to serious harm. That's why the classification has to be argued from the hazard analysis rather than from a rule of thumb about "does it treat the patient."

When the hardware and software leads disagree, I'd bring it back to the hazard analysis as the shared source of truth. The disagreement usually comes from one person reasoning about the software in isolation and the other reasoning about the system. I'd walk through the specific hazardous situations together, ask "could a software failure contribute to this?", and let the answer drive the class. If the two leads still disagree after that, the decision needs to be escalated to the risk management owner and documented — including the rationale for the chosen class and the dissenting view — because the classification drives the rigor of the entire software lifecycle, and an under-classification is a compliance and safety problem. I'd also note that when in doubt, the conservative choice is usually the defensible one, but it should be a reasoned conservative choice, not a default.

**Possible follow-ups:** What artifacts would you expect to see that justify a Class B versus Class C decision? How does the classification change your verification and unit test coverage expectations?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2 while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** I'd start from the noise sources and the coupling paths rather than from a filter topology. The first step is to identify the dominant emissions sources — switching power supplies, clock and data lines, motor or actuator drive — and their frequency content, then map how that energy can leave the enclosure: conducted along cables and the mains cord, or radiated from cables acting as antennas and from board-level loops.

For conducted emissions, the filter belongs at the point where the cable enters the enclosure, and its effectiveness depends as much on the impedance of the return path as on the component values. A common mistake is to design a good differential-mode filter and neglect common-mode impedance, which is often what actually fails. I'd design the filter with the cable's actual common-mode impedance in mind, keep the filter's input and output physically separated so it doesn't couple around itself, and reference the filter ground to the chassis at a single well-defined point.

For radiated emissions, the biggest wins are usually layout and return-path discipline before any filter component is added: minimizing loop areas on switching nodes, keeping high-speed traces short and referenced to a continuous plane, and avoiding splits in the return plane under signals. Cable-level ferrites and common-mode chokes help, but they're treating the symptom if the board is radiating efficiently.

The signal-integrity tension is real: a filter that's aggressive enough to kill emissions can also round off edges, add jitter, or attenuate the signal of interest. So I'd define the signal-integrity budget first — what edge rate and amplitude the receiver actually needs — and then design the filter to meet emissions without eating into that margin. Where the two conflict, the resolution is usually to move the noise source or improve the return path rather than to filter harder. I'd verify with pre-compliance scans early, because discovering the filter is insufficient at the test house is expensive.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between a ferrite bead and a common-mode choke for a given interface? What would you do if the filter that passes emissions also degrades a sensor's accuracy?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?

**Answer:** The fact that it passes calibration on the bench is the most important clue — it means the discrepancy is context-dependent, so the investigation has to reproduce the field conditions rather than re-verify the bench condition. I'd start by characterizing the complaint: how large is the offset, is it consistent across units or isolated, does it depend on the patient, the environment, or the duration of use, and is the reference thermometer itself validated and used correctly.

Then I'd work through the plausible mechanisms systematically. Thermal: is the sensor self-heating, or is it picking up heat from the patient's body or from adjacent electronics in a way that the bench calibration doesn't reproduce? Contact: is the sensor's thermal contact with the patient different in real use — placement, adhesion, insulation from clothing — than in the calibration fixture? Reference: is the clinical reference measuring at a different site or with a different time constant, so the two readings are legitimately different rather than one being wrong? Environmental: is there an ambient temperature or airflow difference between the ward and the calibration lab?

I'd also consider whether the calibration fixture itself is masking the problem — for example, if it holds the sensor in a well-controlled bath, it may not exercise the same thermal path as a patient-contacting probe. A useful step is to compare the returned unit's behavior in a fixture that mimics the clinical mounting, and to instrument a unit in the field if that's feasible.

The corrective action depends on root cause: if it's a thermal-path or self-heating issue, the fix might be in the sensor mounting, the excitation scheme, or a compensation model; if it's a reference or usage issue, the fix might be in the instructions for use or in clinician training. Either way, I'd want the investigation to distinguish "the device is wrong" from "the device and the reference are measuring different things," because those lead to very different corrective actions.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a test that separates sensor self-heating from a genuine measurement error? What would you do if the offset only appeared in a subset of units?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard the engineering team considers negligible, while the schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd start by making sure we're actually arguing about the same thing. "Negligible" is a judgment about risk, and risk is a product of severity and probability — so the first move is to get the hazard, the severity, and the probability estimate on the table explicitly, along with the basis for the engineering team's estimate. Often the disagreement dissolves once the assumptions are visible: the quality manager may be assuming a higher severity or a more credible sequence of events than the engineering team is, or vice versa.

If the estimates genuinely differ, I'd treat that as a risk management question rather than a schedule question. The risk management file should have a defined acceptability criterion, and the decision should be made against that criterion, not against the schedule. If the hazard falls below the criterion with a documented rationale, that's a legitimate basis for not adding the control — but it has to be documented, and the quality manager's concern has to be addressed in the record rather than overruled.

If the hazard is genuinely borderline, I'd look for a control that addresses the concern at lower cost than the one proposed — sometimes the quality manager's underlying worry can be mitigated by a design change, a labeling change, or a verification activity rather than the full control measure. That's often where the real solution is.

On the schedule: I'd be honest that the impact is real and quantify it, but I wouldn't let schedule be the deciding argument. The right framing is "here's the risk, here's the criterion, here's the cost of the control, here's the cost of not doing it" — and then escalate to whoever owns the risk acceptance decision if we can't converge. What I would not do is quietly drop the concern or quietly accept a control I think is unnecessary; both create problems later, either in the audit or in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the quality manager's concern turned out to be valid but the proposed control was the wrong one? How would you document a decision not to add a control so that it holds up in an audit?
