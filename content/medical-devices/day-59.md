# medical-devices — Day 59

## Q1: How would you approach determining the required creepage and clearance distances for a medical PCB that has a mains-referenced primary side, a secondary side, and a patient-connected applied part, where the applied part must be isolated from mains by two means of patient protection (2×MOPP)?
**Answer:** I'd start by classifying the circuit into its insulation domains: the mains-referenced primary, the secondary (SELV) side, and the patient-connected applied part, then map out every insulation barrier between them and what level of protection each barrier must provide. The key is that the requirement is driven by the *worst-case* voltage and the *type* of protection needed, not just the nominal working voltage. For a 2×MOPP barrier between mains and an applied part, I need two independent means of patient protection in series, each individually rated for the full MOPP requirement — so a single barrier, however thick, doesn't satisfy it; you need two separate insulation systems, which can be two separate physical barriers or one barrier plus another independent one (e.g., reinforced plus basic, or two basics).

For the actual distances, I'd work from the applicable standard's tables (IEC 60601-1 Clause 8 and its annexes), which give creepage and clearance as a function of working voltage, pollution degree, material group (CTI), and altitude. I'd determine the pollution degree of the environment (typically 2 for most medical equipment, higher if conductive contamination is possible), pick the material group from the PCB laminate's CTI rating, and apply altitude correction if the device is rated above 2000 m. Clearance is driven mainly by the transient/impulse voltage the barrier must withstand (which ties back to the dielectric strength test voltage), while creepage is driven by the steady-state working voltage and the pollution degree. I'd also account for the fact that creepage and clearance are measured along the *shortest path* — so slots, cutouts, and conformal coating can change the effective distance, and I'd verify that the layout actually achieves the intended path rather than assuming a straight-line measurement.

Practically, I'd build a small insulation coordination table into the design review: for each barrier, list the required protection level, the working voltage, the pollution degree, the material group, the resulting minimum creepage and clearance, and the actual layout value with margin. I'd also flag any place where a component (optocoupler, isolation transformer, digital isolator, or capacitor) forms part of the barrier, because its own creepage/clearance and its rated isolation voltage have to meet the same requirement — a barrier is only as good as its weakest element. Finally, I'd make sure the test plan for dielectric strength and patient leakage current is written against these same barriers, so the design intent and the verification evidence line up.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the required creepage distance can't be met in the available board area without a major layout change? How does conformal coating affect the creepage requirement, and when would you rely on it?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a medical device that must pass both conducted emissions and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a system-level problem rather than a "add a filter at the end" problem, because conducted and radiated emissions are coupled: a poorly controlled return path that causes conducted noise can also radiate, and a filter that fixes conducted emissions can create a resonance that makes radiated emissions worse. I'd start by identifying the noise sources and their frequency ranges — switching power supplies, motor drives, digital clocks, and high-speed interfaces are the usual suspects — and then decide, for each, whether the right fix is at the source, along the path, or at the boundary.

At the source, I'd reduce the noise where it's cheapest: slow down switching edges where timing allows, use spread-spectrum or dithering on clocks if the device can tolerate it, keep loop areas small in switching converters, and choose switching frequencies that don't sit on top of sensitive bands or the measurement bands of interest. Along the path, I'd focus on return current control — a solid reference plane, no splits under high-speed traces, and careful connector pinouts so that return currents aren't forced to detour. At the boundary, I'd design the input filter as a proper multi-stage filter: a common-mode choke plus X and Y capacitors for the mains input, with the Y-capacitor value chosen to satisfy both the emissions target and the leakage current limit (which is the classic tension in medical designs — more Y-capacitance helps emissions but hurts leakage current). I'd also add local filtering on each I/O and power port, because cables are the most common radiation path.

For signal integrity, the constraint is that the filter must not degrade the signals it's protecting. That means keeping filter component parasitics low, placing filters close to the connector or the noise source, and verifying that the filter's insertion loss doesn't eat into the signal's bandwidth or introduce group-delay distortion that matters for the application. I'd simulate the filter with realistic source and load impedances, then verify on the bench with a pre-compliance scan before going to the test lab, because pre-compliance lets you iterate cheaply. Finally, I'd document the rationale — which noise source each filter element addresses, and what the trade-off was — because that's what makes the design defensible in a review and repeatable in production.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you resolve a conflict between the Y-capacitor value needed for emissions and the leakage current limit? What would you do if a filter that passes conducted emissions makes radiated emissions worse?

## Q3: How would you approach classifying a device's software under IEC 62304, and what would you do if the hardware and software leads disagreed on the classification?
**Answer:** I'd start from the standard's definition rather than from intuition: the software safety classification is driven by the *possible* consequence of a software failure, not by how likely that failure is or how complex the software is. Class A applies when a software failure cannot contribute to a hazardous situation; Class B when it can contribute to a hazardous situation that is not a serious injury; Class C when it can contribute to a hazardous situation that could result in death or serious injury. The classification is about the *hazard*, so the first step is to trace from the software's functions to the hazards in the risk analysis — if a software function is involved in a hazardous situation, the classification follows from the severity of that situation, and if the software is not involved in any hazardous situation, it can be Class A.

The subtlety is that "contribute to a hazardous situation" includes software that doesn't directly control therapy but does something the safety function depends on — for example, a monitoring device that displays a physiological parameter and generates alarms. If a software failure could cause a missed alarm or a wrong displayed value that a clinician acts on, that can contribute to a hazardous situation even though the software isn't delivering therapy. So I'd resist the temptation to classify by "does it control a pump or not" and instead ask what the software's output feeds into and what happens downstream if it's wrong.

If the hardware and software leads disagreed, I'd treat it as a risk-analysis question rather than a turf question. I'd ask each to walk through the specific hazard(s) they think the software can contribute to, and then check those against the risk management file. If the disagreement persists, the tie-breaker is the risk analysis and the clinical/quality function, not seniority — because the classification drives the rigor of the software lifecycle (architecture, detailed design, unit verification, integration testing), and getting it wrong in either direction is a real problem: too low and you under-verify a safety-relevant function; too high and you impose cost and schedule that the risk profile doesn't justify. I'd document the rationale for the chosen classification in the software development plan, including the hazards considered, so the decision is auditable and revisitable if the design changes.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would the classification change if the software's only safety role were to detect a hardware fault and shut down? What evidence would you need to justify a Class A classification for software in a device that has alarms?

## Q4: How would you approach investigating a field complaint where a device's patient-contacting temperature sensor reads consistently higher than a reference thermometer used by clinical staff, but the device passes its calibration check when returned to the manufacturer?
**Answer:** The fact that it passes calibration at the manufacturer but reads high in the field is the key clue — it points to something about the field conditions or the measurement setup that isn't reproduced on the bench, rather than a simple offset error in the sensor. I'd structure the investigation around "what's different between the field and the bench," and I'd resist the urge to conclude the clinician is measuring wrong until I've ruled out the device-side explanations.

First, I'd characterize the complaint precisely: is the offset consistent across patients and users, or does it cluster? Is it always the same direction and magnitude, or does it vary? Does it correlate with a particular probe, a particular device serial number, or a particular clinical environment? That tells me whether I'm looking at a design issue, a manufacturing issue, or a use issue. Then I'd look at the measurement chain: the sensor element, the probe-to-device interface (connector, cable, compensation), the signal conditioning, and the algorithm that converts the raw signal to a temperature. A common cause of "reads high in the field but calibrates fine" is a thermal path issue — the sensor is measuring its own self-heating or the temperature of the probe housing rather than the patient, and that error only shows up when the probe is in contact with a patient in a real clinical setup rather than in a calibration bath. Another common cause is a reference-method mismatch: the clinician's reference thermometer may be measuring a different site (e.g., oral vs. axillary vs. skin) or may have its own offset, so the "discrepancy" is partly a comparison-method issue.

I'd also check whether the device's calibration procedure itself is masking the problem — for example, if calibration is done at a single point or at a temperature far from the clinical range, a gain error or a non-linearity could pass calibration but show up at body temperature. And I'd check the connector and cable, because a poor contact or a cable with the wrong compensation can introduce a consistent offset that changes with handling. I'd want to reproduce the field condition as closely as possible: same probe, same cable, same contact geometry, same ambient conditions, and compare against a calibrated reference at the same site.

If I found a design or manufacturing cause, I'd move into the corrective action process — containment (identify affected units), root cause, corrective action, and verification that the fix actually resolves the field condition, not just the bench condition. If I found a use or reference-method cause, the action might be labeling, training, or an IFU clarification rather than a hardware change — but I'd still document it through the complaint system because it's a signal about usability. Either way, I'd keep the risk analysis in the loop, because a temperature reading that's consistently off by a clinically meaningful amount can affect treatment decisions.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design a bench setup that reproduces the field condition without access to the clinical environment? If the root cause turned out to be the clinician's reference thermometer, would you still treat it as a complaint requiring corrective action?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a project where the clinical team has requested a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?
**Answer:** I'd start by making sure I understand the request precisely — what clinical problem it solves, what the current design does that's inadequate, and how the clinical team would prioritize it against the other things competing for the same schedule. "Usability change" can mean anything from a label tweak to a fundamental rework of the user interface, and the response depends heavily on which it is. I'd also want to know whether this is a nice-to-have or something that affects safe and effective use, because those two cases have very different weight in a medical device context — a usability issue that could lead to a use error is a risk-management matter, not just a schedule matter.

Then I'd evaluate the actual cost and risk of the change, not just the headline "three months." A hardware revision late in development typically means: re-spinning the board, re-running verification and validation, re-doing EMC and safety testing, updating the DHF and risk file, and possibly re-doing clinical evaluation if the change affects the clinical performance. The schedule impact is usually larger than the engineering time alone because of test lab queues and regulatory review. I'd also check whether the change can be scoped down — sometimes the clinical need can be met with a firmware change, a labeling change, or a change that's deferred to a post-market update, which avoids the hardware revision entirely.

With that analysis in hand, I'd bring it back to the clinical team and the project stakeholders as a decision, not a yes/no. I'd lay out the options: (a) make the change now and accept the schedule slip, (b) make a reduced version now that addresses the core clinical need, (c) defer the change to a follow-on release and document it as a known limitation, or (d) reject it if the clinical benefit doesn't justify the risk and cost. I'd give a recommendation with reasoning, but I'd make sure the decision is made by the right owner — if it's a safety-relevant usability issue, quality and regulatory need to be in the room; if it's a preference, the product owner can decide. Throughout, I'd keep the risk file and the design history current, because whatever we decide, the rationale needs to be traceable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the clinical team escalated the request above you? What would you do if the change were small in engineering effort but required a full re-test because it touched a safety-related function?
