# medical-devices — Day 70

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device that has both BF-type and CF-type applied parts, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The starting point is to read the applied-part classifications against the standard rather than assume them. BF and CF applied parts have different limits and different fault conditions that must be evaluated, so the first task is to build a matrix: for each applied part, list its classification, whether it is intended for direct cardiac contact, and which single-fault conditions the standard requires you to simulate. That matrix drives the test plan, and it also forces an early conversation with the systems and regulatory leads, because the classification is a design decision with real cost implications — CF generally demands tighter isolation and lower leakage, which cascades into the isolation barrier, the power architecture, and the connector design.

For the measurement path itself, the key is to replicate the standard's measurement network faithfully. Patient leakage current is not measured with a simple ammeter across the applied part; it is measured through the specified measuring device (the RC network defined in the standard) referenced to earth, with the device under test in the correct configuration for each test — normal condition and each applicable single-fault condition. So the practical design work is: define a test fixture that lets you switch the measuring network between the applied parts and the reference points without disturbing the setup, keep the mains supply clean and isolated so you are not measuring your own test bench, and make sure the earth reference is unambiguous. A common mistake is to leave a second ground path in place — through a scope probe, a USB cable, or a signal generator — which shunts current away from the measuring network and gives a falsely low reading.

Deciding which measurements are required comes down to three filters. First, classification: a BF part and a CF part have different limits, so they cannot share a single pass/fail number. Second, patient connection: an applied part that is not patient-connected in normal use may still need evaluation under single-fault conditions if the standard treats it as such. Third, intended use: a part that is only ever touched by the operator is an operator leakage concern, not a patient leakage concern, and mixing the two leads to over-testing in one place and under-testing in another. I would document the rationale for each included and excluded measurement in the test plan, because that rationale is exactly what a reviewer will ask for later.

If a measurement comes back elevated, I would not immediately reach for a design change. I would first confirm the measurement itself — check the fixture, the reference, and whether any unintended ground path exists — because a large fraction of "failures" at this stage are setup artifacts. Only once the measurement is trustworthy would I trace the current path: isolation barrier capacitance, Y-capacitors across the barrier, the applied-part front-end's reference, and any path through the enclosure or a cable shield.

## Q2: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I would treat this as a scoping exercise first and a compliance exercise second. The general standard (60601-1) and the collateral standards (60601-1-x, covering EMC, usability, alarms, and so on) apply to essentially everything. The particular standards (60601-2-x) are function-specific, so the question is: which clinical functions does this device actually perform, and which of those functions has a dedicated particular standard? For a combined monitor-and-therapy device, that usually means two or more particular standards apply simultaneously, and the device must satisfy all of them.

The practical way to build that list is to work from the intended use statement and the clinical claims, not from the engineering architecture. A device that monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy is doing two things that a clinician would describe separately, and each of those descriptions maps to a standard. I would also check the scope clause of each candidate standard carefully, because some particular standards explicitly exclude devices that combine functions, or defer to another standard for the combined case. That scope clause is where most of the ambiguity lives.

Conflicts between the general standard and a particular standard are resolved by a simple hierarchy: the particular standard takes precedence for the aspects it covers, and the general standard fills in everything the particular standard does not address. The particular standard is written as a set of additions, modifications, and replacements to the general standard, so reading it as a standalone document is a mistake — you have to read it against the general standard to see which clauses it modifies. Where two particular standards both apply and appear to conflict, the resolution is usually to meet the more stringent requirement, but I would not make that call unilaterally. I would document the conflict, propose an interpretation, and get it reviewed by the regulatory and quality functions, because the interpretation becomes part of the compliance rationale and needs to be defensible.

The output of this exercise is a compliance matrix: each applicable clause, which standard it comes from, how the device meets it, and where the evidence lives. That matrix is what makes the combined-function device tractable, because it turns a vague "which standards apply?" question into a concrete, auditable list.

## Q3: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow-band failure is a strong clue, because it points to a resonance or a specific coupling path rather than a broad susceptibility problem. The first thing I would do is characterize the failure precisely: at what frequency does it start and stop, how wide is the band, does it depend on the field orientation, and does it depend on the cable configuration or the device's operating state? That characterization tells you whether you are looking at a structural resonance (enclosure, cable, or PCB), a component-level susceptibility (a specific IC or sensor), or a coupling path that only becomes efficient at that frequency.

The next step is to localize the coupling path. I would use a near-field probe to scan the board and cables while injecting at the failing frequency, and I would also try the reverse — inject a signal at the failing frequency and look for where it appears on the board. If the failure correlates with a specific cable, that suggests the cable is acting as an antenna and the current is being conducted into the enclosure. If it correlates with a specific area of the PCB, that suggests a trace or a component is resonating. Shielding the enclosure temporarily, or adding a ferrite to a cable, is a quick way to test a hypothesis without committing to a redesign.

Once the path is identified, the fix is usually one of a few things. If it is a cable coupling issue, common-mode chokes, ferrites, or improved cable shielding and grounding at the connector can help. If it is a PCB resonance, the fix might be a layout change — shortening a trace, changing a return path, adding a ground stitch — or a local filter on the affected net. If it is a component-level susceptibility, a decoupling or filtering change at that component's supply or input may be enough. The important discipline is to fix the coupling path rather than to blanket the design with filters, because filters add cost, board area, and their own failure modes, and they can mask a problem that will reappear at a different frequency.

I would also verify the fix at the failing frequency and at the band edges, and then re-run the full immunity sweep, because a change that fixes one band can shift a resonance into another. And I would document the root cause and the fix in the design history, because immunity failures that are "fixed" without a documented mechanism tend to come back.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish between a failure caused by conducted coupling through a cable and one caused by direct field coupling into the enclosure?
- If the fix requires a PCB layout change late in the project, how would you assess the schedule and re-test impact?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The decision should be driven by the risk management process, not by intuition about how bad the failure "feels." The starting question is: what is the hazardous situation that this failure could lead to, and what is the severity of the harm that could result? If the failure can contribute to a hazardous situation with non-negligible severity, it is safety-related and needs to be captured in the risk file with a risk control. If it cannot — because the failure is detected and annunciated before it can affect the clinical function, or because the function it affects is not safety-related — then it is a usability or reliability issue and belongs in a different track.

The practical way to make that call is to trace the failure through the software architecture to the clinical function it affects. A failure in a data-logging routine that only affects the completeness of a record is different from a failure in the same routine if that routine also feeds a display that a clinician uses to make a decision. The same code can be safety-related or not depending on what it feeds, so the classification has to be done at the level of the function, not the module.

I would also apply the software safety classification from IEC 62304 as a cross-check. If the software item is classified as contributing to a hazardous situation, then failures in it are safety-related by definition, and the classification should already be documented. If a failure surfaces that does not fit the existing classification, that is a signal that either the classification was wrong or the failure has been mischaracterized — either way, it needs to go back through the risk process rather than being quietly filed as a bug.

The behavioral discipline here is to resist the temptation to downgrade a failure because fixing it is expensive or because it is intermittent. Intermittent failures are often the most dangerous, because they are the hardest to detect and the easiest to dismiss. I would rather over-classify and then justify a lower classification with evidence than under-classify and discover later that a safety-related failure was treated as a cosmetic issue.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a failure that is safety-related in one operating mode but not in another?
- What evidence would you want before accepting a lower classification for a failure that was initially flagged as safety-related?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I would start by making sure we are actually disagreeing about the same thing. "Negligible" is a judgment, and the quality manager and the engineering team may be using different inputs to reach it — different severity assumptions, different probability estimates, or different views of what the standard requires. So the first move is to get the hazard, the estimated risk, and the basis for that estimate on the table in a form both sides can examine. If the engineering team's "negligible" is based on a probability estimate that the quality manager has not seen, or vice versa, the disagreement may resolve itself once the assumptions are shared.

If the disagreement persists, I would separate two questions: is the risk control required by the standard or by the company's risk policy, and is it required by our own risk analysis? If it is required by the standard or by policy, then the schedule impact is not really a factor in whether to do it — it is a factor in how to do it, and the conversation shifts to sequencing and resourcing. If it is not required by either, then the question is whether the residual risk is acceptable, and that is a decision the risk management process should make, not the loudest voice in the room. I would ask the quality manager to document the rationale for the control, and I would ask the engineering team to document the rationale against it, and then take both to the risk review.

On the schedule question, I would be honest that the impact is real and that it needs to be weighed, but I would not let schedule be the deciding factor for a safety-related control. The right framing is: if we add this control, what does it cost, and what does it buy? If the cost is a schedule slip and the benefit is a documented reduction in a hazard that a reviewer might question, that is often a trade worth making, and it is better to make it deliberately now than to be forced into it later by a reviewer or a field issue.

The leadership part is to keep the disagreement about the risk, not about the people. I would make sure the quality manager's concern is treated as legitimate — it usually is, even when the engineering team disagrees with the conclusion — and I would make sure the engineering team's analysis is heard rather than overridden. If we still disagree after that, I would escalate to the risk management board or the appropriate decision-maker with both positions documented, rather than letting the decision be made by whoever is more persistent.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the risk control the quality manager wants is technically impractical to implement?
- What would you do if the same disagreement recurred on a later project with the same quality manager and the same engineering team?
