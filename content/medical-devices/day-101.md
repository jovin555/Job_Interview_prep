# medical-devices — Day 101

## Q1: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware correctly handles a sensor that intermittently produces readings within the valid range but physiologically implausible — for example, a pressure spike that is numerically possible but inconsistent with the patient's condition?

**Answer:** The core problem is that a range check alone is insufficient: the value is inside the sensor's valid window, so the firmware's normal validation passes it through. I'd approach this in layers. First, I'd define what "physiologically implausible" means in testable terms — rate-of-change limits (a pressure can't jump by a large amount in one sample period given the mechanical and fluidic time constants), cross-parameter consistency (a pressure spike that isn't accompanied by any correlated change in flow or another channel), and persistence criteria (a real physiological event has a plausible rise and decay, whereas a glitch is often a single sample). These become explicit, documented requirements rather than an implicit "the firmware should be smart about it." Second, I'd build the test strategy around injecting those specific patterns: single-sample spikes, step changes, slow ramps that exceed physiological slew rates, and correlated vs. uncorrelated multi-channel anomalies. The test harness should be able to replay recorded or synthetic sensor streams deterministically so the same anomaly can be run against every firmware build. Third, I'd verify the *response* is defined and safe — does the firmware reject the sample, hold the last good value, flag the channel as suspect, raise a technical alarm, or fall back to a degraded mode? Each of those is a design decision that needs a requirement and a test. I'd also test the boundaries: an anomaly that is genuinely physiological but extreme should *not* be rejected, so the test set needs true-positive and false-positive cases. Finally, I'd make sure the anomaly handling is itself observable — logged with enough context that a field investigation could reconstruct what happened — and that the logging path can't be starved by the anomaly handling.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the rate-of-change threshold without either missing real events or rejecting valid ones? Where would this plausibility checking live — sensor driver, signal processing layer, or application logic — and why?

## Q2: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I'd start from the device's intended use and clinical function, not from its marketing description, because the particular standards are scoped by function. I'd enumerate each distinct clinical function the device performs and map each to its candidate 60601-2 part — a monitoring function points toward the relevant monitoring standard, a therapy function toward the therapy-specific standard, and so on. Then I'd read the scope clause of each candidate carefully: some particular standards explicitly exclude or defer to others, and some apply only when the function is the primary intended purpose. Where two particular standards both apply, the general rule is that the particular standard takes precedence over the general standard for the specific requirements it covers, and the general standard fills in everything the particular doesn't address. Where two *particular* standards appear to conflict, I'd look for the more stringent requirement as the baseline, document the conflict explicitly, and — if it's genuinely ambiguous — raise it with the test lab or a regulatory consultant early rather than discovering it at test time. The output of this exercise is a compliance matrix: every clause of the general standard and each applicable particular standard, mapped to how the device complies and where the evidence will come from. That matrix drives the test plan and becomes part of the DHF. I'd also flag combination devices to the regulatory pathway early, since the applicable standard set affects the submission.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if a particular standard's requirement is technically incompatible with the device's intended clinical use? How would you document the rationale for a standard you concluded does *not* apply?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the filtering strategy for a device that must pass both conducted and radiated emissions limits under IEC 60601-1-2, while also meeting its own signal integrity requirements?

**Answer:** The tension is that emissions filtering and signal integrity pull in opposite directions: an aggressive low-pass filter on a signal line cleans up high-frequency emissions but can also degrade the edge rates and bandwidth the signal needs. I'd approach it by separating the problem into power and signal domains. On the power input, the conducted emissions problem is usually dominated by the switching supply and its input current harmonics, so I'd start with a well-designed input filter — common-mode choke, X and Y capacitors sized against the leakage current budget (which is a hard constraint on a medical device, since Y-capacitance directly adds to earth leakage), and a differential-mode stage — and verify it with pre-compliance scans rather than assuming the filter model is accurate. On the signal side, I'd first try to solve emissions at the source: slow the edge rates where the timing budget allows, use series termination or ferrites on lines that leave the board, and keep high-speed traces short and referenced to a solid return plane so the loop area — and therefore the radiated field — stays small. The key discipline is to define the signal integrity requirement first (what bandwidth, what edge rate, what jitter budget the receiver actually needs) and then filter only up to that requirement, not beyond it. I'd also plan for the fact that filtering is often a symptom fix: if a board radiates badly, the root cause is usually return-path discontinuity or a cable acting as an antenna, and a filter on the connector may just move the problem. Pre-compliance scanning early, with the actual enclosure and cables, is what keeps this from becoming a late-stage surprise.

**Possible follow-ups:** How does the medical leakage current limit constrain your choice of Y-capacitors in the input filter? How would you tell the difference between a filter that's fixing the problem and one that's just masking a layout issue?

## Q4: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The failure mode I'd design against is the risk file that gets written retrospectively to match a design that's already done — it's not auditable because the reasoning was reconstructed, and it's not useful because nobody consulted it while making decisions. To avoid that, I'd make the risk management file a living artifact with a clear structure: a hazard analysis that's traceable to specific design inputs and architecture elements, a risk estimation and evaluation record per hazardous situation, risk control measures each linked to the design output that implements them and the verification evidence that proves they work, and a residual risk evaluation with an explicit acceptance decision against defined criteria. The critical discipline is the linkage: every risk control should point to a requirement, and every requirement that mitigates a risk should point back to the hazard. That bidirectional traceability is what makes the file auditable and what makes it useful — when a design change comes in, you can query which hazards it touches. I'd also run the risk review as a recurring design-review agenda item rather than a one-time event, so new hazards surface while there's still time to address them cheaply. And I'd keep the file's structure aligned with the DHF so the two don't drift apart. The test of a good risk file is whether a reviewer who wasn't on the project can follow the reasoning from hazard to control to evidence without asking questions.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a risk control that turns out to be ineffective during verification — what does the file record? How do you keep the risk file in sync when the design changes late in the project?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard that the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a disagreement about risk evaluation criteria, not about whether the quality manager is being unreasonable — and I'd want to resolve it on the merits rather than by seniority or schedule pressure. First, I'd ask the quality manager to walk through their reasoning: what hazardous situation they're envisioning, what severity and probability they're assigning, and against what acceptance criteria. Often the disagreement is that engineering is estimating probability from the design's intended behavior while quality is estimating it from a broader field-use or misuse scenario — and one of those is usually the more defensible reading. Second, I'd check whether the risk evaluation criteria themselves are documented and agreed; if the team is arguing about a hazard without a shared acceptance threshold, that's the real gap, and fixing it resolves this case and future ones. Third, I'd separate the risk decision from the schedule decision: the schedule impact is a real constraint, but it shouldn't be the reason a risk control is dropped — if the control is warranted, the conversation becomes about how to implement it cost-effectively (a design change, a labeling/instruction control, a manufacturing test) rather than whether to do it. If after that discussion there's still genuine disagreement, I'd escalate to the design review board or the responsible regulatory/quality authority with both positions documented, because a risk acceptance decision needs to be made by someone with the authority to own it. What I would not do is let the schedule argument silently override a documented safety concern — that's exactly the kind of decision that looks bad in an audit and worse in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the quality manager's concern is based on a scenario the team believes is physically impossible — how do you resolve that? How would you document a risk acceptance decision where engineering and quality disagreed?
