# medical-devices — Day 65

## Q1: How would you approach designing the patient leakage current measurement path for a device with multiple applied parts of different classifications, and how would you decide which measurements are actually required?

**Answer:** The starting point is to map each applied part to its classification under IEC 60601-1 — Type B, BF, or CF — because that classification, combined with whether the part is intended for direct cardiac contact, determines which leakage current limits apply and under what conditions. The measurement path itself has to be built so that the measuring device (typically an MD or a purpose-built leakage current meter with the correct input impedance and frequency response per the standard) sits in the right place in the circuit: for patient leakage current, between the applied part and earth, with the device powered from mains at nominal voltage plus a defined percentage, and with the supply polarity reversed for each measurement. For multiple applied parts, you measure each one individually and then in combination, because the standard treats the sum of leakage currents across simultaneously connected parts as a separate consideration. The practical design work is in the fixture: you need a way to connect the meter to each applied part without disturbing the patient circuit, and you need to be able to switch between normal condition and single-fault condition (open earth, open one supply conductor) cleanly. I'd build the fixture so that the applied part connections are broken out to a patch panel, with the meter and the fault switches in a defined topology, and document that topology in the test procedure so the measurement is repeatable. The decision about which measurements are required comes from the standard's table of applicable limits for the device's classification and intended use — not from convenience. If an applied part is BF and the device is Class I, you're measuring patient leakage in NC and SFC, and you're also checking that the sum across parts doesn't exceed the limit for the worst-case configuration. I'd also verify the measurement setup itself before trusting any result: a known resistor to earth to confirm the meter reads what it should, and a check that the fixture's own leakage is negligible compared to the limit being tested.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where the sum of leakage currents across multiple applied parts is within the individual limits but the combined value is close to the overall limit?
- What would you do if the measurement is marginal and you need to decide whether it's a real design issue or a measurement artifact?

## Q2: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions, and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** The first step is to define the device's intended clinical function precisely, because the particular standards are keyed to function and intended use, not to the device's marketing category. A device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy will typically fall under more than one particular standard — for example, a monitoring particular standard and a therapy-delivery particular standard — and both apply unless one explicitly excludes the other. I'd start by listing every function the device performs, then for each function identify the candidate particular standards by reading their scope clauses, which state what they cover and what they don't. Where two particular standards both apply, the general standard (60601-1) provides the baseline, and each particular standard modifies or adds to it. Conflicts are usually resolved by the principle that the more specific requirement governs, but you have to read the particular standards carefully because some of them explicitly state how they interact with the general standard and with each other. If there's genuine ambiguity — for example, two particular standards give different test conditions for the same parameter — the resolution is to document the interpretation, cite the clauses, and where necessary seek clarification from the test lab or the regulatory body before testing, not after. I'd also build a compliance matrix that maps each clause of each applicable standard to the design feature or test that addresses it, so the interaction between standards is visible and auditable. The trap to avoid is assuming that because a device is "mainly" one thing, only one particular standard applies; the combination of functions is exactly what makes the standard set larger, not smaller.

- How would you handle a situation where a particular standard's test method is impractical for your device's form factor?
- What would you do if a particular standard was published after your design was frozen?

## Q3: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file should be a living record that grows with the design, not a retrospective compilation. I'd structure it around the ISO 14971 process steps: risk management plan, hazard identification, risk estimation, risk evaluation, risk control, verification of risk control effectiveness, and evaluation of residual risk, with an overall risk acceptability conclusion. The key to keeping it useful is to make each entry traceable in both directions — from hazard to risk control to verification evidence, and from a design change back to the hazards it might affect. Practically, that means maintaining a hazard log or risk register as a controlled document from the start, with each hazard having a unique identifier, a description of the hazardous situation, the harm it could lead to, the severity and probability estimates, the risk control measure, and the evidence that the control was implemented and effective. When a design decision changes — a component swap, a firmware behavior change, a new use case — the risk file should be reviewed for impact as part of the change control process, not left until the next design review. I'd also keep the file's structure aligned with the DHF so that a reviewer can move between them without re-deriving the relationships. The audit test is simple: can someone who wasn't on the project pick up the file and, for any hazard, find the control and the evidence that the control works, and for any design change, find the hazards it touched? If the answer is no, the file has become a document rather than a process. I'd also make sure the risk management plan defines the acceptability criteria up front, because retrofitting criteria after the fact is a common source of audit findings.

- How would you handle a hazard that's identified late in the project, after the design is largely frozen?
- How do you decide when a residual risk is acceptable versus when it needs further control?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the analog front-end for a patient-connected biosensor whose signal of interest is a fraction of a millivolt riding on a baseline that can drift by hundreds of millivolts, while keeping the design verifiable against a specified accuracy limit?

**Answer:** The core problem is dynamic range and baseline rejection, and the design has to solve both without compromising the ability to verify the result. I'd start by characterizing the signal and the baseline separately: what's the bandwidth of the signal of interest, what's the bandwidth of the baseline drift, and where do they overlap? If the signal is at higher frequency than the drift, a high-pass or band-pass stage can remove most of the baseline before the signal reaches the ADC, which relaxes the ADC's dynamic range requirement. If they overlap, you can't filter the baseline out without filtering the signal, so you need either a higher-resolution ADC or a baseline-tracking scheme — for example, a servo loop that subtracts a slowly updated estimate of the baseline, or a chopping/modulation approach that moves the signal away from the baseline's frequency band. The front-end topology matters: an instrumentation amplifier with good common-mode rejection is usually the first stage, followed by filtering and gain, with the ADC's reference and input range chosen so that the signal occupies a meaningful fraction of the full scale. For verifiability, I'd define the accuracy limit in terms of a test that can actually be performed — for example, injecting a known signal at the input and measuring the output across the specified baseline range and temperature range — and I'd make sure the design's error budget is broken down into contributions (input offset, gain error, noise, ADC quantization, reference drift) so that each can be verified or bounded. The design should also include a calibration or self-test path if the accuracy limit is tight enough that component tolerances alone won't meet it. The trap is designing for the signal in isolation and then discovering that the baseline drift saturates the front-end or eats the ADC's resolution; the error budget has to account for the baseline range, not just the signal.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide between a higher-resolution ADC and an analog baseline-subtraction stage?
- How would you verify the front-end's accuracy when the baseline drift is difficult to reproduce in the lab?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and during a design review the quality manager insists on adding a risk control measure for a hazard the engineering team considers negligible. The schedule impact would be significant. How would you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a disagreement about risk estimation and acceptability, not as a personality conflict, and I'd try to resolve it on the merits before it becomes a schedule argument. The first step is to make sure both sides are actually talking about the same hazard and the same estimates: what's the hazardous situation, what's the harm, what severity and probability are being assumed, and on what basis? Often the disagreement comes down to different assumptions about probability — the engineering team may be assuming a failure rate from lab experience, while the quality manager may be considering the installed base, the use environment, or a worst-case user population. I'd ask for the evidence behind each estimate and see whether the gap is factual or a difference in risk acceptability criteria. If the criteria are defined in the risk management plan, that's the reference point: if the estimated risk falls below the acceptability threshold, the control isn't required by the plan, but the quality manager may still be raising a legitimate concern about whether the estimate is right. If the estimate is uncertain, the honest answer may be that the control is cheap insurance, and the schedule impact has to be weighed against that. If the estimate is well-supported and the risk is genuinely negligible, I'd document the rationale for not adding the control, note the dissenting view, and move on — but I'd also look for a lower-cost way to address the concern, such as a design change that doesn't require a hardware revision or a test that demonstrates the risk is as low as estimated. What I wouldn't do is either dismiss the quality manager's concern or accept the control without understanding it, because both set a bad precedent. The decision should be recorded with the reasoning, so that if the hazard ever materializes, the file shows it was considered.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What would you do if the quality manager's concern was based on a regulatory requirement you weren't aware of?
- How would you handle a similar disagreement if it were the clinical team rather than quality raising the concern?
