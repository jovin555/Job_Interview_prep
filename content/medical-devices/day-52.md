# medical-devices — Day 52

## Q1: How would you approach designing the isolation scheme for a medical device whose patient-connected sensor front-end must be galvanically isolated from a mains-powered host, where the isolation barrier also has to pass a low-level analog signal and a bidirectional digital control link?

**Answer:** I'd start by defining what the barrier actually has to accomplish before picking parts. The barrier has two jobs that are often conflated: safety isolation (meeting the applicable means of patient protection for the applied part, with the creepage, clearance, and dielectric withstand that implies) and functional isolation (keeping the host's noise, ground offsets, and fault currents out of a sensitive measurement). Those can pull in different directions, so I'd write down the required withstand voltage, the working voltage across the barrier, and the pollution degree/overvoltage category the environment implies, then let those drive the physical spacing rather than the other way around.

For the signal path, the key decision is where to digitize. If the analog signal is low-level and high-impedance, crossing the barrier in the analog domain means either an isolation amplifier or a modulated carrier through a transformer or capacitive isolator — both add offset, gain, and nonlinearity that have to be characterized and budgeted into the measurement error. Digitizing on the patient side and crossing digitally usually gives a cleaner error budget, because the isolator only has to be transparent to a logic signal, not preserve analog fidelity. The trade-off is that you now need isolated power on the patient side, which means a DC-DC converter that itself becomes part of the barrier and a source of switching noise near the front-end.

For the digital link, I'd pick a isolator family rated for the required withstand and working voltage, and pay attention to things that are easy to overlook: common-mode transient immunity (a defibrillation or ESD event can couple a fast transient across the barrier), propagation delay and channel-to-channel skew if the link is timing-sensitive, and whether the part's certification (e.g., a recognized component with a defined isolation rating) actually covers the way I'm using it. I'd also think about the failure mode — a single isolator failure shouldn't be able to bridge the barrier in a way that defeats the protection, which is part of why the standard asks for two means of protection rather than one robust one.

Physically, I'd keep the barrier as a clearly defined region on the board with no traces crossing it except through the intended isolation components, maintain the required creepage and clearance on both sides including under conformal coating assumptions, and verify the layout against the isolation component's own creepage requirements — the component rating is only valid if the board doesn't undercut it. Then I'd plan the verification: dielectric strength (hi-pot) at the type-test level, and a production routine test if the standard or risk analysis calls for it, plus a functional check that the measurement still meets accuracy with the barrier in place.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide between an analog isolation amplifier and digitizing on the patient side, if the sensor's full-scale output is only a few millivolts?
- What would you check in the layout to confirm the isolation component's rated creepage is actually achieved on the board?

## Q2: How would you approach classifying a device's software under IEC 62304, and what would you do if the hardware and software leads disagreed on the classification?

**Answer:** I'd start from the standard's own logic rather than from intuition about how "important" the software feels. The classification hinges on the worst-case harm that could result from a software failure, assuming the software is the only thing that fails — you don't get to credit external risk controls when deciding the class, though you do get to credit them when deciding how much rigor the controls need. So the first step is to trace each software item to the hazardous situations it could contribute to, using the risk analysis, and ask: could a software failure lead to death or serious injury (Class C), non-serious injury (Class B), or no injury (Class A)? The classification is per software item or architecture segment, not necessarily one label for the whole product, so a device can have a Class C monitoring path and Class A housekeeping code.

The common disagreement is exactly the one where a device monitors physiology and alarms but doesn't deliver therapy. One side argues "it doesn't touch the patient, so it can't hurt anyone" and lands on Class A; the other argues that a missed or false alarm can lead to harm and lands on Class B or C. I'd resolve it by going back to the hazard analysis rather than debating the label: what hazardous situation does a missed alarm contribute to, and what's the severity of the harm if that situation isn't caught? If the answer is "clinician misses a deteriorating patient and the outcome is serious," that's a Class C argument regardless of the fact that the device doesn't actuate anything. The absence of therapy delivery lowers the *direct* risk but doesn't automatically lower the class.

If the disagreement persists, I'd treat it as a risk-management question and bring it to the risk management file: document both positions, the hazard analysis that supports each, and get the quality/regulatory function to make the call with the evidence in front of them. The cost of over-classifying is real — more documentation, more testing, more traceability — but the cost of under-classifying is a submission finding or, worse, a field issue that the process wasn't rigorous enough to catch. I'd rather argue it out on paper early than discover it during an audit.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the classification ends up as Class C for one software item, how does that change your unit and integration testing approach for that item versus the rest of the codebase?
- How would you document the classification decision so it's defensible if a reviewer challenges it?

## Q3: A device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow-band, reproducible failure is a gift compared to an intermittent one, because it points at a resonance rather than a broadband coupling problem. I'd start by characterizing the failure precisely: sweep the band in small steps to find the exact center frequency and the bandwidth over which it fails, and note the field strength and modulation at which it appears. That tells me whether I'm looking at a high-Q resonance (narrow, sharp) or a broader coupling path, and whether the threshold is close to the test level or well below it.

Then I'd try to localize the coupling path. The usual suspects are: a cable or trace acting as an unintentional antenna at that frequency (a quarter-wave or half-wave resonance on a harness, a long unshielded lead, a board trace), a resonant structure in the enclosure or a cavity, or a component — often an op-amp input, a comparator, or a reset/enable line — that's demodulating the RF into a baseband disturbance. I'd probe with a near-field probe or a current probe to see where the energy is actually going, and I'd try the cheap experiments first: does it still fail with the enclosure open? With a cable rerouted or shortened? With a ferrite clamped on a specific harness? Each of those narrows the path.

Once I've localized it, the fix is usually one of a few families. If it's a cable resonance, I'd address it at the source with common-mode chokes, a different cable routing or length, or a shield terminated properly at both ends depending on the grounding scheme. If it's a trace or component, I'd look at filtering and layout: a small series resistor or ferrite plus a capacitor right at the sensitive pin, a ground plane that gives the return current a low-impedance path, and keeping the sensitive node's loop area small. If it's a firmware-visible symptom — a spurious reset, a corrupted reading — I'd also look at whether the firmware is doing anything to make it worse, like polling a pin that's momentarily indeterminate, or lacking a debounce or plausibility check that would reject a transient.

I'd be careful not to fix it by adding a filter that also degrades the signal I care about, and I'd re-verify at the full test level with margin, not just at the point where it stopped failing. A fix that works at exactly the test level with no margin is a fix that will fail in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you tell the difference between a cable resonance and a component-level susceptibility problem?
- If the fix requires a board change, how would you decide whether to do a respin or a rework/modification for the next test round?

## Q4: How would you approach building and maintaining traceability between design inputs, design outputs, verification, and validation in a design history file, especially as the design changes?

**Answer:** I'd treat traceability as a living structure, not a document assembled at the end, because the end-of-project version is always stale and always missing the changes that happened in the last few months. The backbone is a unique identifier for each design input, each design output, each verification activity, and each validation activity, with explicit links in both directions — input to output, output to verification, input to validation — so I can answer both "what verifies this requirement?" and "what requirement does this test cover?" without hunting.

The practical discipline is to make the traceability matrix a byproduct of the work rather than a separate task. When a requirement is written, it gets an ID. When a design output is created, it references the input ID it satisfies. When a test is written, it references the output and input it verifies. If that's enforced in whatever tool the team uses — a requirements management tool, or even a disciplined spreadsheet with a change log — the matrix stays current because it's updated as part of the change, not as a separate cleanup.

For changes, the key is that a change to a design input has to ripple: it should trigger a review of the affected outputs, a determination of whether existing verification still covers the change, and a decision about whether re-verification or re-validation is needed. That's a change-control question as much as a traceability question, and I'd want the change record to point at the affected IDs so the ripple is visible. The failure mode I'd guard against is a change that gets made in the design but never propagates to the matrix, which is exactly the kind of gap a reviewer will find.

I'd also keep the distinction between verification (did we build it right, against the spec) and validation (did we build the right thing, against the user need) clear in the structure, because they trace to different things — verification to design inputs, validation to user needs and intended use — and conflating them is a common source of confusion in a DHF.

## Q5: You're the lead engineer, and a supplier delivers a batch of PCBs where incoming inspection finds a nonconformance in a critical isolation area — the supplier says it's within tolerance and will pass hi-pot. How would you handle it?

**Answer:** I'd separate two questions that are easy to merge: is the supplier's claim about hi-pot true, and does passing hi-pot actually mean the boards are acceptable? Those aren't the same thing. Hi-pot is a go/no-go dielectric strength test at a defined voltage and duration; it doesn't necessarily catch a marginal reduction in creepage, a partially cured material, or a condition that degrades over time under humidity or thermal cycling. So even if the supplier is right that the boards pass hi-pot today, that doesn't settle whether they meet the design's requirements for the life of the device.

My first move would be to get the facts on the nonconformance: what exactly is out of spec, by how much, against which drawing or specification, and what does the supplier's own inspection data show. I'd want the measurement method and the sampling to be comparable to ours, because "within tolerance" disputes often come down to different measurement techniques or different acceptance criteria. If the nonconformance is in a critical isolation area, I'd treat it as a risk-management input, not just a quality disposition — the question is what hazard it could contribute to and whether the existing risk controls still hold.

Then I'd decide the disposition based on evidence, not on schedule pressure. Options are typically: accept with justification (if the deviation is genuinely inconsequential to safety and performance, documented and approved), rework or screen (if a test can reliably sort good from bad), or reject and require the supplier to correct the process. For a critical isolation area, I'd lean toward not accepting on the basis of a single hi-pot pass, because the failure mode I'm worried about is the one that shows up later. If I did consider acceptance, I'd want additional evidence — maybe extended hi-pot, maybe a sample subjected to humidity or thermal conditioning, maybe a cross-section — and I'd want the quality and regulatory functions to sign off, because this is the kind of decision that has to be defensible in the DHF.

I'd also address the process side: a nonconformance in a critical area is a signal about the supplier's process control, so I'd want a corrective action request and a plan to verify the fix, not just a disposition of this batch. And I'd communicate the schedule impact honestly rather than letting the pressure to ship drive a decision that the evidence doesn't support.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What additional evidence would you want before accepting a deviation in a critical isolation area?
- How would you decide between screening the batch and rejecting it outright?
