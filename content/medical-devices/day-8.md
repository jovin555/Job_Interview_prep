# medical-devices — Day 8

## Q1: During IEC 60601-1 testing, your medical device fails the earth leakage current test with a measurement exceeding the 500 µA limit for permanently installed equipment. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving this?

**Answer:** I would start by isolating the leakage path. Earth leakage current typically flows through the mains filter's Y-capacitors, through parasitic capacitance between primary and secondary sides of the power supply, or through insulation breakdown. First, I'd measure leakage with the mains filter disconnected to see if the Y-capacitors are the primary contributor — if so, I'd verify they're appropriately rated and consider reducing their capacitance values if the application allows, while being careful not to compromise conducted emissions performance. If the leakage persists, I'd check the power supply's transformer inter-winding capacitance; a shielded transformer with a Faraday shield connected to protective earth can significantly reduce this path. I'd also inspect the PCB layout for any unintended coupling between mains and earth traces. If the device has a metal enclosure, I'd verify the bonding impedance between all exposed conductive parts and the earth terminal is below 0.1 Ω as required. Finally, I'd check that no insulation breakdown exists by performing a dielectric strength test at reduced voltage first to avoid damaging components.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you balance reducing Y-capacitor values for lower leakage current against the need for adequate EMI filtering? What if the device is Class II (double-insulated) and has no protective earth connection — how does the leakage current limit change?

## Q2: How would you approach developing a design history file (DHF) for a medical device that is an evolution of a previously cleared device, rather than a completely new product?

**Answer:** For an iterative design, I would start by performing a gap analysis between the predicate device's existing DHF and the new design's requirements. The key is to identify which design inputs have changed — for example, a new sensor, modified user interface, or updated power requirements — and which remain identical. For unchanged subsystems, I can reference existing verification and validation documentation rather than recreating it, but I need to clearly document the rationale for why those elements don't require re-verification. For changed elements, I would create new design input specifications, trace them through design output documentation, and execute targeted verification testing. The risk management file needs particular attention: I would update the hazard analysis to reflect any new failure modes introduced by the changes, and re-evaluate existing risk controls that might be affected. The design review process should explicitly compare the new design against the predicate to ensure all changes are captured. I would also create a change impact assessment document that maps each modification to the affected DHF sections, ensuring nothing is overlooked during regulatory submission.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle software changes under IEC 62304 when the device uses a modified version of the predicate's firmware? What documentation would you need to create versus what you could reference from the original DHF?

## Q3: A medical device you're developing uses a rechargeable Li-ion battery pack that must be replaceable by clinical staff. How would you approach the design to meet IEC 60601-1 requirements for battery compartments and user-replaceable power sources?

**Answer:** I would address several specific requirements. First, the battery compartment must prevent access by the patient — typically this means requiring a tool to open, or placing it in a location that's only accessible to service personnel. The contacts must be designed to prevent short circuits if a conductive object is inserted, which often means recessed contacts or using a connector rather than spring terminals. For the battery itself, I need to ensure the cell balancing and protection circuitry (overcurrent, overvoltage, undervoltage, overtemperature) is on the battery pack assembly, not relying on the device's main PCB. The charging circuit must comply with the battery manufacturer's specifications and include redundant temperature monitoring. From a risk management perspective, I would analyze failure modes like reverse polarity insertion, which requires mechanical keying or a protection diode, and thermal runaway, which may require a venting path and flame-retardant enclosure materials. The device should also gracefully handle battery removal during operation — either by maintaining power through a backup supercapacitor or by safely shutting down and preserving patient data. Finally, I'd verify the battery compartment meets ingress protection requirements appropriate for the clinical environment, typically IP22 or higher for cleaning.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you design the charging circuit to prevent overcharging while the device is in use? What IEC 60601-1-2 immunity considerations apply to the battery management system's communication bus?

## Q4: You're the lead engineer on a medical device project. During a design review, the clinical team requests a change that would improve usability but would require a significant hardware revision and delay the regulatory submission by three months. How would you evaluate and respond to this request?

**Answer:** I would approach this systematically rather than making an immediate decision. First, I'd ask the clinical team to document the usability issue formally, including how frequently it occurs, whether it could lead to use errors that cause patient harm, and what specific improvement they're proposing. I'd then evaluate the request against the design inputs and risk management file — if the current design has an acceptable residual risk per ISO 14971, the change may not be strictly necessary from a safety perspective. However, if the usability issue could lead to use errors that create hazardous situations, it becomes a risk management concern that must be addressed. I would then work with the hardware team to scope the actual impact: is there a partial implementation that addresses the core concern without the full three-month delay? For example, could we modify firmware or the user interface to mitigate the issue while deferring the hardware change to the next revision? I would present these options to the project steering committee with a clear trade-off analysis: the clinical benefit and risk reduction versus the regulatory delay and potential competitive impact. Ultimately, if the change addresses a genuine patient safety concern, I would recommend proceeding, but if it's purely a convenience improvement, I would advocate for capturing it in the next design iteration and proceeding with the current submission timeline.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you document this decision in the design history file? What if the clinical team escalates the request to senior management over your recommendation?

## Q5: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's wireless communication (e.g., Bluetooth Low Energy) meets both the IEC 60601-1-2 immunity requirements and the wireless coexistence requirements for a hospital environment?

**Answer:** This requires addressing two distinct but related standards. For IEC 60601-1-2 immunity, the wireless module is part of the device under test during radiated RF immunity testing at 80 MHz to 2.7 GHz at 3 V/m for a life-supporting device or 10 V/m for a device used in a high-frequency medical treatment environment. I would ensure the wireless communication doesn't cause the device to enter an unsafe state during these tests — for example, if the link drops, the device should default to a safe mode rather than continuing with stale data. This means implementing a communication watchdog that detects link loss and triggers an appropriate response, such as an alarm and a safe-state transition. For wireless coexistence, I would follow AAMI TIR69 or similar guidance to characterize the device's behavior in the presence of other wireless technologies common in hospitals — Wi-Fi on 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, other Bluetooth devices, Zigbee, and potentially medical telemetry systems. I would perform testing where the device's wireless link is stressed by co-located interferers at various power levels and frequencies, measuring metrics like packet error rate, latency, and reconnection time. The acceptance criteria should be tied to the device's risk analysis: for a monitoring device that logs data locally and transmits periodically, a brief link interruption may be acceptable, while for real-time alarm transmission, the system needs robust retry mechanisms and possibly a secondary communication path. I would also verify that the wireless transmission doesn't cause self-interference with sensitive analog measurement circuits on the same PCB, which often requires careful antenna placement and shielding during the layout phase.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine the appropriate Bluetooth connection interval and data throughput for a device that monitors multiple physiological parameters? What testing would you do to verify the wireless link doesn't interfere with other medical devices in the same room?
