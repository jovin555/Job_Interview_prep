# medical-devices — Day 129

## Q1: How would you approach designing the mains-to-secondary isolation barrier on a medical PCB where the secondary side also connects to a patient-applied part, and how do you decide whether the barrier needs one or two means of patient protection?

**Answer:** The key is to first classify the applied part and the overall means of protection the standard requires, then design the barrier to that classification rather than picking a barrier and hoping it qualifies. For a patient-connected applied part, the relevant question is whether the patient connection is isolated from mains by one means of patient protection (1×MOPP) or two (2×MOPP), which depends on the applied part type and the risk of the patient being connected to other equipment. Once that's fixed, the barrier is designed as a physical structure: a defined creepage/clearance gap across the PCB, a specified solid insulation thickness through the board or through an isolation component, and a rated isolation barrier device (transformer, optocoupler, digital isolator, or isolated DC-DC) whose working voltage and test voltage meet the requirement. I'd treat the barrier as a single continuous boundary drawn on the layout — everything crossing it must be a rated isolation component, and nothing else (no traces, no copper pours, no mounting hardware) may bridge it. For 2×MOPP, that means either two independent barriers in series or a single component rated for the full reinforced requirement, and the creepage/clearance is calculated for the higher of the two. I'd also verify the barrier against the dielectric strength test voltage and confirm the isolation components' datasheets state the correct rating for the working voltage and pollution degree, because a component that's "isolated" but only rated for functional isolation is a common and dangerous mistake.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a barrier that must also pass a low-level analog signal, where an isolation amplifier's own accuracy and drift become part of your error budget? What documentation would you keep to demonstrate the barrier meets the required means of protection during a design review?

## Q2: How would you approach verifying that a medical device's firmware correctly handles a situation where a sensor intermittently produces readings that are within the valid range but physiologically implausible — for example, a pressure spike that is numerically possible but inconsistent with the patient's condition?

**Answer:** The core problem is that a range check alone won't catch this — the value is inside the sensor's valid window, so the firmware has to reason about plausibility over time and context, not just instantaneous bounds. I'd approach it in layers. First, define what "implausible" means in testable terms: rate-of-change limits (a physiological signal can only move so fast), cross-parameter consistency (a pressure spike that doesn't correlate with any other channel), and persistence (a single sample versus a sustained trend). Then design the test strategy around those definitions rather than around the sensor's raw range. I'd build a test harness that can inject synthetic sensor streams — including the specific adversarial cases: a single-sample spike, a spike that decays over a few samples, a spike that coincides with a real event, and a spike at the boundary of the plausibility window — and verify the firmware's response for each: does it reject, flag, hold the last good value, raise an alarm, or pass it through? The verification has to confirm not just that the bad value is caught but that the *correct* behavior happens and that legitimate fast transients aren't falsely rejected. I'd also verify the behavior is deterministic and documented, because "the algorithm usually catches it" is not a verifiable claim. Finally, I'd trace each test case back to the risk analysis entry that motivated the plausibility check, so the verification is defensible as risk control verification, not just functional testing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the rate-of-change limit without clinical input, and what would you do if the clinical team couldn't give you a number? How would you test that the plausibility logic itself doesn't create a new failure mode, like masking a genuine rapid deterioration?

## Q3: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I'd start from the device's intended use and clinical function, not from its marketing category, and map each distinct function to the particular standard that governs it. A combined device often falls under more than one particular standard, and the correct approach is to apply all that are relevant rather than picking one. The general standard (60601-1) plus collateral standards (60601-1-2 for EMC, 60601-1-6 for usability, 60601-1-8 for alarms, 60601-1-11 for home use, etc.) apply on top of the particular standards. Where a particular standard and the general standard appear to conflict, the particular standard generally takes precedence for the specific requirement it addresses — but I'd verify that reading against the standard's own scope clause and the applicable regulatory guidance rather than assuming, because "particular overrides general" is a default, not an absolute. The practical method is to build a requirements matrix: rows for each clause/requirement, columns for each applicable standard, and a note on which governs and why. That matrix becomes the compliance plan and the traceability backbone for the test program. I'd also flag any requirement where the two standards impose different test conditions or limits, because those are the cases most likely to cause a surprise at the test lab, and resolve them with the lab and the regulatory reviewer early rather than at submission.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a device where one function is a monitoring function and the other is a therapy function, and the two particular standards specify different alarm philosophies? What would you do if a notified body disagreed with your reading of which particular standard applies?

## Q4: How would you approach a situation where, during system integration testing, a device intermittently fails in a way that could plausibly be caused by either a firmware timing issue or a hardware signal-integrity problem, and the two discipline leads are each convinced it's the other's area?

**Answer:** The first move is to stop the debate from being about opinion and make it about evidence. I'd frame the problem as "we don't yet know which domain owns this, and our job is to design the experiment that tells us," which reframes both leads as collaborators on a diagnosis rather than defendants. Concretely, I'd want to characterize the failure precisely: when does it occur, what's the trigger, is it correlated with temperature, load, a specific firmware build, a specific board, a specific cable or fixture? Then I'd design a set of discriminating tests — for example, swap the firmware build on the same hardware, swap the hardware on the same firmware, instrument the suspect signal with a scope or logic analyzer to see whether the physical signal is clean at the moment of failure, and add firmware-side timestamping to see whether the timing violation precedes or follows the signal anomaly. The point is to find a test whose outcome is only consistent with one hypothesis. I'd also set a time-box: if the discriminating tests don't resolve it within a defined window, escalate to a joint debug session with both leads in the same room looking at the same capture, because remote finger-pointing wastes more time than a shared session. Throughout, I'd keep the tone neutral — the goal is a correct root cause, not a winner — and I'd document the investigation so the eventual fix is traceable to evidence.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the discriminating test showed the failure only occurs when both a particular firmware build and a particular board revision are combined, so neither domain alone explains it? How would you prevent this kind of cross-domain ambiguity from recurring on the next project?

## Q5: How would you approach structuring a risk management file so that it stays useful and auditable throughout a project, rather than becoming a document assembled at the end?

**Answer:** The file has to be a living part of the design process, not a deliverable produced after the fact. I'd structure it so that risk analysis is created and updated at the same cadence as design work: hazards are identified during concept and architecture, risk controls are defined as design inputs, and verification of those controls is captured as design outputs — so the risk file and the design history file reference each other continuously rather than being reconciled at the end. Practically, that means a single traceable structure: each hazard links to its foreseeable sequence of events, its risk estimate, the risk control measure, the verification evidence that the control is effective, and the residual risk evaluation. I'd keep the risk estimates and the control verification in the same record so a reviewer can follow the chain without hunting across documents. I'd also build in regular review points — risk reviews at design milestones, and a standing agenda item at design reviews to ask "does this change introduce or retire a hazard?" — so the file stays current as the design evolves. The test of a good file is whether someone who wasn't on the project can pick it up, follow any hazard from identification to residual risk acceptance, and see the evidence at each step. If they can't, the file has become a compliance artifact rather than a risk management tool, and that's the failure mode to avoid.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a design change late in the project that invalidates a risk control you'd already verified — what does the file update look like? How would you keep the risk file from becoming so large that reviewers can't navigate it?
