# medical-devices — Day 80

## Q1: How would you approach designing the analog front-end for a patient-connected biosensor whose signal of interest is a fraction of a millivolt riding on a baseline that can drift by hundreds of millivolts, while keeping the design verifiable against a specified accuracy limit?

**Answer:** The core problem is dynamic range versus resolution: the baseline drift is orders of magnitude larger than the signal, so a single DC-coupled gain stage would either saturate or waste most of the ADC's codes on the drift. I'd start by separating the two concerns architecturally. First, establish a high-impedance, low-bias-current instrumentation front-end so the sensor isn't loaded and the electrode offset doesn't get converted into error. Then handle the baseline with either AC coupling (a high-pass corner set well below the lowest signal frequency of interest) or an active baseline-restoration loop — a servo that senses the slow average and feeds a correction back into the amplifier chain. The trade-off is that AC coupling is simple and drift-immune but removes any true DC diagnostic content, while a servo preserves DC but adds a control loop that itself has to be stable and cannot inject noise into the signal band.

For the signal band itself, I'd set gain so the maximum expected signal-plus-residual-offset uses most of the ADC's input range without clipping under worst-case tolerance, and pick an ADC whose noise floor and effective resolution comfortably beat the accuracy spec with margin — because the accuracy budget has to absorb sensor tolerance, reference tolerance, gain error, and drift over temperature, not just the converter. I'd also think hard about the reference: a noisy or drifting ADC reference directly corrupts the measurement, so a low-drift reference with good decoupling close to the reference pin matters as much as the amplifier choice.

Verifiability is the part people under-plan. I'd define the accuracy limit as a testable statement — over what signal range, temperature range, and time — and then build a verification setup that can actually inject a known small signal on a known large offset. That usually means a calibrated signal source plus a way to sweep the baseline independently, so I can characterize gain, offset, linearity, and drift separately rather than as one lumped number. If I can't decompose the error sources, I can't tell whether a failing unit is a design margin problem or a component problem. I'd also want a noise measurement (RMS in-band) as a separate verification item, since noise and accuracy are different failure modes. Finally, I'd make sure the front-end has a defined behavior when the sensor is disconnected or the baseline goes out of range — saturating gracefully rather than producing a plausible-looking wrong value.

## Q2: How would you approach determining which IEC 60601-2 particular standards apply to a device that combines two clinical functions — for example, a device that both monitors a physiological parameter and delivers a therapy — and how would you resolve conflicts between the general standard and the particular standards?

**Answer:** I'd start from the device's intended clinical function and its classification, not from the marketing description, because the applicable particular standard is driven by what the device actually does to or for the patient. The first step is a structured review of the intended use statement and the risk analysis to enumerate every clinical function, then map each function to the IEC 60601-2-xx standard that governs that function type. A combined-function device often maps to more than one particular standard, and that's expected — the standards are written to be applied together where functions overlap.

The harder question is conflicts. In principle, a particular standard takes precedence over the general standard for the specific requirements it addresses, and the general standard fills in everything the particular standard doesn't cover. But in practice you hit cases where two particular standards impose different test conditions or different limits for what looks like the same parameter. My approach is to first confirm whether it's a genuine conflict or just two standards addressing different aspects — often it's the latter, and reading the scope and the rationale clauses resolves it. If it's a genuine conflict, I'd document the interpretation, consult the standard's guidance or a test lab that has seen the combination before, and where necessary raise it with the regulatory body or a notified body early rather than discovering it at test time. The key is to make the interpretation explicit and traceable in the compliance documentation, because an auditor will ask why you applied one limit and not the other.

I'd also build the standards applicability matrix early in the project and treat it as a living document. It feeds directly into the test plan, the risk file, and the design inputs, so getting it wrong late is expensive. And I'd flag any function that has no clearly applicable particular standard — that's a signal to look harder at whether the function is genuinely novel or whether it falls under a standard I haven't considered.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you document a standards interpretation decision so it survives an audit?
- What would you do if a particular standard's test method isn't feasible for your device's form factor?

## Q3: During IEC 60601-1-2 immunity testing, a device passes radiated RF immunity at most frequencies but shows a reproducible malfunction in a narrow band around one specific frequency. How would you approach diagnosing and resolving it?

**Answer:** A narrow-band, reproducible failure is actually a gift compared to an intermittent one, because it points to a resonant or tuned mechanism rather than a broadband susceptibility. The first thing I'd do is characterize the failure precisely: at what field strength does it start, how narrow is the band, does it depend on device orientation, cable routing, or the state of the device (idle vs. active monitoring), and what exactly is the malfunction — a reset, a corrupted reading, a communication dropout, a display glitch? The nature of the malfunction tells me which subsystem is coupling.

The frequency itself is a strong clue. If it corresponds to a cable length resonance (a cable acting as an antenna at a quarter or half wavelength), the fix is usually at the cable — ferrites, shielding, twisting, or re-routing. If it corresponds to an internal trace or a slot in the enclosure, it's a layout or mechanical issue. If it corresponds to a clock harmonic or a switching regulator frequency, the coupling may be into a specific node rather than a structural resonance. I'd use a near-field probe to localize where the energy is entering and where it's being rectified into a disturbance, and I'd compare against a baseline with the enclosure open versus closed to separate radiated coupling from conducted coupling along cables.

Resolution follows the mechanism. For cable coupling, common-mode chokes and improved cable shielding or grounding. For enclosure coupling, adding shielding, changing seam or vent geometry, or improving the ground plane continuity. For coupling into a sensitive node, local filtering, a better reference, or a layout change to shrink the loop area. I'd also consider whether the firmware can be made more robust to the disturbance — for example, a watchdog or a plausibility check that recovers cleanly — but I'd treat that as defense in depth, not as the primary fix, because the requirement is that the device not malfunction, not just that it recover.

The important discipline is to fix the mechanism, not the symptom. Adding a ferrite that happens to shift the resonance out of the test band without understanding why is fragile — it may reappear at a different frequency, in a different orientation, or in production with a different cable supplier. I'd want to be able to explain the coupling path before I sign off on the fix.

- How would you distinguish a cable resonance from an enclosure resonance experimentally?
- When is a firmware robustness measure an acceptable part of the EMC fix versus a workaround?

## Q4: How would you approach deciding whether a given software failure in a medical device should be classified as a safety-related failure requiring formal risk controls, versus a non-safety usability or reliability issue?

**Answer:** The classification should be driven by the risk analysis, not by intuition about how bad the failure "feels." The starting question is: what is the hazardous situation, if any, that this failure could lead to, and what is the severity of the harm that could result? A software failure that produces an incorrect displayed value is only a safety issue if that incorrect value could lead a clinician to a harmful action or could mask a condition that requires intervention. A software failure that causes a cosmetic glitch or a nuisance log entry is a reliability or usability issue unless it degrades a safety function.

So the process is: take the failure, trace it to the device function it affects, then trace that function to the hazards in the risk file. If the failure can contribute to a hazardous situation, it's safety-related and needs to be handled under the software safety classification and the risk control process — which means it needs a risk control measure, verification that the measure is effective, and traceability through the design history file. If it can't contribute to a hazardous situation, it's still worth fixing, but it's handled as a quality or reliability defect rather than a safety-critical one.

The subtle cases are the ones where the failure is only hazardous in combination with another failure, or only under a specific use scenario. That's where the risk analysis has to consider reasonably foreseeable sequences, not just single faults. A failure that's benign alone but removes a layer of protection when a second fault occurs is safety-related. I'd also be careful about the "it's just a display issue" trap — in a monitoring device, the display is often the primary means by which the clinician detects a deteriorating patient, so a display failure can be safety-related even though it doesn't touch the therapy.

The other discipline is consistency. If two similar failures get classified differently, that's a sign the criteria aren't well defined. I'd want the classification criteria written down and applied uniformly, and I'd want the software safety classification of the overall item to be justified against the risk file rather than asserted. When there's genuine disagreement — say, between a quality lead and a firmware lead — the resolution is to go back to the hazard analysis and let the severity and probability drive it, and to document the rationale either way.

- How would you handle a failure that is only hazardous in combination with a second, independent fault?

## Q5: You're the lead engineer on a project where the clinical team has requested a usability change late in development that would require a hardware revision and push the regulatory submission out by several months. How would you evaluate and respond to the request?

**Answer:** My first move is to separate the clinical need from the proposed solution. The clinical team is telling me something about how the device is used that I need to understand, but the specific change they've proposed may not be the only way — or even the best way — to meet that need. So I'd sit down with them and the product owner to understand what problem they're actually trying to solve, how often it occurs, and what the consequence is if it isn't solved. That conversation often reveals that the need can be met with a firmware change, a labeling change, a training change, or a smaller mechanical modification, any of which might avoid the full hardware revision.

If the need genuinely requires the hardware change, then it becomes a trade-off decision that belongs with the program leadership, not with engineering alone. My job is to lay out the options honestly: do it now and accept the schedule impact; do it in a follow-on revision after the current submission; or find a partial mitigation that addresses the most important part of the need now and defers the rest. I'd quantify the schedule and cost impact as concretely as I can, and I'd also flag the regulatory implications — a late hardware change can affect the design history file, the verification already completed, and potentially the validity of any testing already done, so the true cost is usually larger than just the redesign.

I'd also bring the risk perspective. If the usability issue could lead to a hazardous situation — a misread, a mis-set parameter, a delayed response — then it's not purely a schedule trade-off; it may need to be addressed before submission regardless of cost, or the risk file needs to justify why it can wait. If it's a convenience or efficiency issue, the calculus is different.

Whatever the decision, I'd want it documented with the rationale, because a late change that gets deferred needs a clear record of why, and a late change that gets accepted needs a clear record of the impact. And I'd want the clinical team to feel heard — the worst outcome is that they raise a real concern, get told "no" without explanation, and stop raising concerns.

- How would you decide whether a usability issue is severe enough to justify delaying submission?
- What would you do if the clinical team and the program leadership disagreed on the priority?
