# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 100

## Q1: How would you approach bringing up a high-speed serial link between two FPGAs across a backplane where the link trains successfully at a reduced rate but fails to establish at the target line rate?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a channel-margin problem and work from the physical layer upward. First, I'd confirm the failure is genuinely rate-dependent rather than configuration-dependent — verify the reference clock is within the transceiver's required phase-noise and frequency-tolerance window at both ends, and confirm the PLL/MMCM settings and line-rate configuration match the actual reference frequency. Then I'd look at the channel itself: insertion loss at the Nyquist frequency of the target rate, connector and via discontinuities, and whether the equalization settings (CTLE, DFE, pre-emphasis) are appropriate for the measured loss profile. A link that trains at half rate but not full rate usually means the channel loss or crosstalk exceeds what the current EQ can compensate. I'd pull an eye diagram or use the transceiver's internal eye-scan/PRBS error counter to quantify margin, then adjust TX pre-emphasis and RX equalization, and if margin is still thin, address the board — via stub back-drilling, connector selection, or reference-plane continuity. I'd also rule out crosstalk from adjacent lanes by testing with neighbors quiet versus active.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a channel-loss problem from a reference-clock jitter problem using only on-chip instrumentation? What would you check first if the link trains at full rate on the bench but fails in the system chassis?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the clocking architecture for an FPGA design that must distribute several unrelated frequencies to different logic blocks while keeping jitter low for a sensitive high-speed interface?
**Answer:** I'd start by classifying each clock by its requirements: frequency, jitter/phase-noise budget, and whether it needs to be related (synchronous) to another clock. The sensitive high-speed interface gets priority — its reference should come from a clean source with a dedicated clock management tile, and I'd avoid routing it through shared MMCM/PLL resources that also serve noisy logic, since cascaded or shared PLLs can accumulate jitter. For unrelated frequencies, I'd use separate PLL/MMCM instances or dedicated oscillator inputs rather than trying to derive everything from one source, because integer/fractional synthesis of unrelated rates tends to compromise phase noise. I'd also plan the global versus regional clock network usage deliberately — global networks for clocks that fan out widely, regional/banked resources for clocks confined to a small region, to avoid exhausting global resources and to reduce unnecessary distribution loss. Finally, I'd document the clock domain relationships and constraints up front so the timing and CDC analysis downstream is grounded in the actual architecture rather than discovered late.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether two clocks can share a PLL without violating the sensitive interface's jitter budget? What are the risks of deriving a clock through a cascade of two MMCMs?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design that passes timing analysis and simulation but produces occasional single-bit errors in a data path only when the board is placed in a system with other high-power switching supplies nearby?
**Answer:** The correlation with nearby switching supplies points strongly at a noise/EMI coupling mechanism rather than a logic bug, so I'd separate the two hypotheses early. First, I'd confirm the errors are truly data-path errors and not memory or link errors by adding on-chip error detection or a signature/checksum at key pipeline stages, and by capturing the failing pattern. Then I'd test the coupling hypothesis directly: vary the proximity and load of the offending supplies, add temporary shielding or ferrite filtering, and see whether the error rate changes. On the board side I'd look at return-path continuity, decoupling effectiveness on the affected rails, and whether sensitive traces run near the aggressor. On the FPGA side I'd check whether the failing path has adequate timing margin — a design that "passes timing" at nominal but has thin margin can fail when supply noise shifts delays — so I'd review the worst-case slack and consider whether the operating conditions (voltage/temperature) are being modeled. I'd also rule out clock jitter induced by the noise, since jitter eats margin on the same paths.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the errors originate in the FPGA fabric versus an external interface? What board-level changes would you prioritize if you confirmed radiated coupling?

## Q4: How would you approach choosing between a global clock buffer and a regional/banked clock resource for a block of logic that runs at a modest rate, when the device's global clock resources are nearly exhausted?
**Answer:** I'd first confirm the block genuinely needs a clock network resource at all versus being clocked from an already-distributed clock, since the cheapest fix is often reusing an existing clock. If it needs its own clock, I'd evaluate the fan-out and physical spread of the logic: a regional/banked buffer is appropriate when the loads are confined to a small region and the skew tolerance is loose, which is usually true for modest-rate logic. The trade-offs are that regional resources have more limited reach and skew control, so if the logic is spread across the die or has tight intra-clock skew requirements, forcing it onto a regional buffer can create timing problems that a global buffer would have avoided. I'd also check whether the modest-rate block can tolerate the slightly higher skew and whether the tool can legally place the loads within the regional buffer's coverage. If neither fits, I'd consider whether the block can be restructured to share a clock with an existing domain, or whether a clock-enable scheme on an existing global clock is cleaner than adding a new network.
**Possible follow-ups:** What timing risks appear when logic clocked by a regional buffer spans more of the die than the buffer can reach? How would you verify after implementation that the regional clock choice didn't introduce skew problems?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA board, and a junior engineer proposes a stack-up that you believe will cause impedance and crosstalk problems for the high-speed lanes, but they've built a simulation that shows it working and are confident. How do you handle the situation?
**Answer:** I'd treat it as a shared investigation rather than an authority call, because the engineer has done real work and the simulation may be revealing something I'm missing — or it may be missing something the simulation can't capture. First I'd ask what the simulation models: does it include the actual stack-up dielectric properties, the reference-plane transitions, the connector, and crosstalk from adjacent aggressors, or is it an idealized single-trace model? Often the gap is that a 2D or simplified model looks fine while the real stack-up has thin dielectric, wide return-path spacing, or layer transitions that the model omits. I'd walk through the specific concerns — impedance target versus computed trace geometry, and crosstalk coupling to neighbors — and ask the engineer to extend the simulation to cover those cases. If the extended model still looks acceptable, I'd want a plan to validate on the first prototype with TDR and crosstalk measurements before committing to volume. The goal is to make the decision evidence-based and to leave the engineer with a stronger model, not to override them.
**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's extended simulation still disagrees with your concern and schedule pressure favors shipping the design? How would you structure the prototype validation to catch a stack-up problem early?
