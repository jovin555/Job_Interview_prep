# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 97

## Q1: How would you approach designing the power-up and configuration sequence for an FPGA that loads its bitstream from external flash, when the flash device itself has a non-trivial wake-up latency and the board may be power-cycled rapidly?

**Answer:** The core problem is that the FPGA's configuration controller starts driving its configuration interface a fixed (and often short) time after its own power rails are valid, but the flash device may still be in its internal power-up or reset-recovery state and not yet able to respond. If the FPGA issues its first read command into that window, it either gets no response or garbage, and configuration fails — and on a rapid power cycle the flash may not have fully discharged, so its behavior at the next power-up is not the same as a cold start.

My approach is to treat this as a sequencing and handshake problem rather than a timing-margin problem. First, I'd characterize the flash's actual wake-up requirement from its datasheet — power-up time, reset-release-to-ready time, and any minimum time the device needs after its supply is stable before it will accept commands — and compare that against the FPGA's configuration start delay and the board's rail ramp. If there's no margin, I don't try to tune it; I add explicit control. That means holding the FPGA's configuration start (via its program/reset or a configuration-controller hold input, depending on the device family) until a supervisor circuit confirms both that the FPGA rails are good and that a delay sufficient for the flash has elapsed. A supply supervisor with a programmable delay, or a small sequencer, is the usual tool.

For rapid power cycling specifically, I'd make sure the flash's reset is actively driven from the same supervisor rather than left to an RC, so that a fast off-on transition still produces a clean, full reset pulse rather than a partial one. I'd also confirm the flash's supply discharges predictably — a bleed resistor or an active discharge on the rail can be necessary if the load is very light, otherwise the device can sit in an indeterminate state between power cycles. On the FPGA side, I'd verify the configuration mode pins are strapped correctly and stable before configuration begins, since a marginal strap during a fast ramp is another way to get an intermittent boot failure.

Verification is the other half: I'd test cold start, warm restart, and repeated rapid power cycles at the extremes of the supply range and temperature, and instrument the configuration interface (or use the FPGA's configuration status outputs) to confirm the flash is actually ready before the first command. If the failure only shows up on a fraction of boards, that points to margin in the ramp or the supervisor threshold, so I'd measure the actual ramp on several units rather than trusting the nominal.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to solve this in hardware sequencing versus adding a small microcontroller to manage the boot? What would you check first if configuration succeeds on the bench but fails in the field after a brownout?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a global clock network and a regional/banked clock resource for a logic block that only needs to run at a modest rate, when the device's global clock resources are nearly exhausted?

**Answer:** I'd start by being clear about what the two resource types actually buy you. Global clock networks are designed for low skew and high fanout across the whole device, and they're a scarce resource — there are only a handful per device. Regional or banked clock resources reach a limited spine or quadrant, have somewhat higher skew and more limited fanout, but they're far more numerous. So the decision is really about matching the reach and skew requirement of each clock to the cheapest resource that satisfies it, and reserving globals for the clocks that genuinely need device-wide reach.

For a logic block running at a modest rate, the questions I'd ask are: how far does this clock physically need to travel, how many loads does it drive, and how much skew can the receiving logic tolerate? If the logic is confined to one region and the clock is slow relative to the fabric, a regional resource is almost always adequate — skew that would matter at 400 MHz is often irrelevant at tens of MHz, as long as the logic is properly timed against the actual clock. The trap is using a global for a slow, localized clock out of habit and then running out of globals for the one high-speed clock that truly needs low skew.

I'd also look at whether the "modest rate" clock is really a separate clock at all. Often it can be derived as a divided or gated version of an existing global clock, or the logic can be moved into the domain of a clock that already has a global resource, which frees a global entirely. Where a genuinely independent slow clock is needed, I'd assign it to a regional resource and then confirm in the timing report that the resulting skew is accounted for — the tool will time it, but I want to see that the skew isn't eating a large fraction of the period.

The verification step matters: after assignment, I'd check the clock routing report to confirm the tool actually used the resource I intended (it may promote or reassign), and I'd review any clock-domain crossings that result, because moving a clock to a regional network can change the relationship between domains in ways that affect synchronization assumptions.

**Possible follow-ups:** What are the risks of letting the placer promote a regional clock to a global automatically? How would you handle a design where two regional clocks in different quadrants need to exchange data?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the transceiver link passes its built-in PRBS checker at the physical layer, but application-level data is occasionally corrupted, and the corruption only appears at full line rate?

**Answer:** A passing PRBS check tells me the serial deserializer, CDR, and analog front end are basically healthy — the eye is open enough and the bit error rate at the physical layer is acceptable. So the corruption is almost certainly above the SerDes: in the digital datapath between the transceiver's parallel output and the application logic, in the framing/packetization, or in the clock domain crossing where the recovered-clock domain hands data to the fabric clock domain. The fact that it only appears at full line rate is the key clue — it points to something that has no slack at full rate, like a FIFO that's marginally sized, a CDC that works when there are idle cycles but not when the pipe is saturated, or a backpressure path that isn't exercised below full rate.

My first move is to separate "is the data wrong coming out of the transceiver" from "is the data wrong after our logic." If the device has a way to inject and check a known pattern at the parallel interface (many transceiver IPs support this), I'd run it at full rate — if that passes, the SerDes and its internal FIFO are fine and the problem is downstream. If it fails, the issue is in the transceiver's rate-matching or its interface to the fabric.

Assuming it's downstream, I'd look hard at the CDC between the recovered-clock domain and the fabric domain. At full line rate there are no gaps, so any CDC that relies on the writer occasionally being idle will fail. I'd check whether the CDC is a proper asynchronous FIFO with correct gray-coded pointers and enough depth for the worst-case phase difference, or whether it's something weaker. I'd also check the framing: at full rate, a status or header field that's updated combinationally can be sampled mid-update, producing a valid-looking but wrong word.

Then I'd instrument. Bringing a small amount of state out to a logic analyzer or a debug FIFO — the write/read pointers, an error counter, the first bad word and its neighbors — usually localizes it quickly. If corruption is a single bit or a burst, that's a strong hint: single-bit suggests a CDC or timing issue on one lane; a burst suggests a FIFO overflow or a framing slip. I'd also confirm the link is actually running at the intended rate and not being over-clocked by a slightly wrong reference, which can pass PRBS at the SerDes but break rate assumptions upstream.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a FIFO overflow from a CDC metastability issue using only counters? What would you change in the design to make this class of bug impossible rather than just fixed?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the return-current path for a high-speed digital board where a signal transitions between two reference planes (e.g., from a ground plane to a power plane) on its way through a via?

**Answer:** The principle is that a high-speed signal's return current follows the path of least impedance, which at high frequency is directly beneath the trace on the adjacent reference plane. When the signal via passes through a plane pair and the reference changes — say from ground to a power plane — the return current has to get from one plane to the other, and if there's no low-inductance path for it, it will find a long, loop-forming path that radiates and creates ground bounce. So the design goal is to give the return current a short, low-inductance transition right at the via.

The standard fix is a stitching capacitor placed close to the signal via, connecting the two reference planes, so the return current can hop across through the capacitor instead of wandering. The capacitor needs to be physically near the via — its placement matters more than its value, because the loop area is what sets the inductance. I'd size it for the frequencies of concern and use a small package with low ESL; a 0.1 µF in an 0402 or 0201 is typical, sometimes a smaller value in parallel if the band of interest is high. Where many signals cross the same plane boundary, I'd place a cluster of stitching caps and, if the design allows, add stitching vias between the planes as well.

The better structural answer, where the stack-up permits, is to avoid the reference change entirely: route the high-speed signal on a layer whose adjacent reference is the same plane throughout, or arrange the stack-up so that the two reference planes it crosses are already capacitively coupled by a thin dielectric — a closely spaced power/ground pair behaves as a distributed stitching capacitor and makes the transition much more forgiving. I'd review the stack-up early specifically to see which layer transitions will force a reference change and plan the via and cap placement around them.

Verification is by layout review against a checklist — every high-speed via that changes reference gets a nearby stitching cap, and the return path is continuous — plus, where it matters, a field solver or 3D extraction of the transition to confirm the impedance discontinuity is acceptable. On the bench, a failing return path often shows up as increased jitter or eye closure on the affected link, or as radiated emissions at the transition, so I'd keep those as the diagnostic signatures.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide how close "close enough" is for the stitching capacitor? What's the trade-off between adding stitching vias versus relying on plane capacitance?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer presents a timing closure report showing all constraints met, but you notice the report was generated with a set of timing exceptions (false paths and multicycle paths) that the engineer added to make the design close. The engineer argues that the exceptions are legitimate because "those paths don't need to meet single-cycle timing." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** My first reaction is not to accept or reject the claim, but to make the exceptions auditable. Timing exceptions are legitimate and often necessary — a multicycle path on a slow enable, or a false path on a genuinely asynchronous crossing, is correct design practice. The problem is that an exception is also the easiest way to make a failing design "pass," and once it's in the constraints file it silently removes the path from analysis forever. So the review question isn't "are exceptions allowed," it's "is each exception justified, documented, and verified by something other than the timing report."

In the review I'd go through the exceptions one at a time with the engineer and ask, for each: what is the physical reason this path doesn't need single-cycle timing, and what else proves that? For a multicycle path, the proof is that the launching logic holds the data stable for the declared number of cycles — that has to be shown in the RTL, not asserted. For a false path, the proof is that the two ends are genuinely asynchronous and are synchronized properly (a real CDC structure), or that the path is structurally unreachable. If the engineer can't articulate the reason and point to the mechanism, the exception comes out and we look at the real timing.

I'd also want the exceptions to be visible and reviewed as a set — a constraints file where exceptions are added quietly during closure is a process smell. I'd ask for them to be listed with a comment explaining each, and ideally cross-referenced to the CDC or multicycle analysis that backs them up. And I'd want independent evidence: CDC checking on the asynchronous paths, and for multicycle paths, either a formal check or a targeted simulation that exercises the multi-cycle behavior.

The tone matters here. The engineer isn't wrong to use exceptions — they may well be correct — and I don't want to make them defensive or encourage them to hide the next one. I'd frame it as "help me understand why each of these is safe," and where an exception is justified, I'd say so and record it. Where it isn't, we fix the design or add the missing proof. The outcome I'm looking for is a constraints set that a reviewer can trust, not a report that says "met."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you catch an unjustified exception that was added months ago and everyone has since forgotten about? What's your rule for when a multicycle path is acceptable versus when it's hiding a real problem?
