# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 25

## Q1: How would you approach designing a clock domain crossing (CDC) scheme for a multi-bit data bus where the source and destination clocks are asynchronous, the data changes frequently, and you need to minimize latency while ensuring data integrity?

**Answer:** For a multi-bit bus crossing between truly asynchronous clock domains with frequent data changes, the fundamental challenge is that each bit can metastabilize or be sampled at a different time, producing a corrupted word. The first decision is whether the data is a continuous stream or a packetized/word-based transfer. For continuous streaming, an asynchronous FIFO is the standard solution — it handles the rate mismatch, provides full/empty flags for backpressure, and uses Gray-code pointers (or equivalent) for the read/write pointer crossing to ensure only one bit changes at a time. The FIFO depth must be sized based on the worst-case burst analysis, not just average rates. For word-based transfers where data changes frequently but has valid qualifiers, I'd consider a handshake-based approach — but with frequent changes, a full four-phase handshake adds significant latency. A better approach is to use a small asynchronous FIFO even for word transfers, or use a valid/ready protocol with proper synchronization of the control signals. The key trade-off is latency versus safety: you can't have both minimal latency and absolute safety with a wide asynchronous bus without a FIFO or handshake. If the frequency relationship is known and stable (even if asynchronous), you can use a multi-stage synchronizer on a data-valid signal and ensure the data is stable long enough — but this requires careful analysis of setup/hold margins across process, voltage, and temperature. I would also verify the design with formal CDC verification tools or at minimum with constrained-random simulation that models metastability.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine the required FIFO depth for this scenario?
- What happens if the FIFO becomes full — how would you handle backpressure in your design?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the configuration bitstream loads successfully, but the design functions correctly at room temperature and fails only when the board is heated to its maximum specified operating temperature?

**Answer:** This is a classic temperature-dependent failure, and I'd approach it systematically. First, I'd characterize the failure precisely — what exactly fails, at what temperature threshold, and does it recover when cooled? This tells me whether it's a timing margin issue, a power integrity issue, or a component parametric drift. The most common cause in my experience is timing margin degradation: as temperature rises, transistor switching speeds slow down, reducing setup/hold margins on critical paths. I'd start by running the timing analysis at the slow-slow process corner with the maximum operating temperature modeled — if the design barely meets timing at the worst case, that's a strong suspect. I'd also check the power supply rails at temperature — voltage droop under load can worsen at high temperature due to increased resistance and reduced efficiency, which directly erodes timing margins. Another angle is to check for temperature-sensitive components: oscillators or PLLs can drift, and if the design relies on a specific clock frequency relationship, that could break. I'd use a thermal chamber to reproduce the failure, then use on-chip debug features (ILA, logic analyzers, or embedded instrumentation) to capture the exact failure point. I'd also check the configuration memory — some FPGAs have temperature-sensitive configuration cells, though this is less common. Once I identify the failing path or mechanism, the fix might be adding pipeline stages, tightening timing constraints to force the tools to optimize specific paths, improving power delivery, or adding thermal management. The key is to reproduce the failure reliably before attempting fixes, otherwise you can't verify the solution.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish between a timing margin issue and a power integrity issue in this scenario?
- What specific timing reports would you examine first?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a power distribution network (PDN) for an FPGA with multiple voltage rails, where the core rail (0.85V) has a transient current demand of up to 20A with a slew rate of 1A/ns, and you need to ensure the voltage stays within ±3% of nominal?

**Answer:** This is a demanding PDN requirement — 20A at 0.85V with 1A/ns slew rate means the target impedance is extremely low. The ±3% budget on 0.85V is ±25.5mV, and with 20A transients, that translates to a maximum PDN impedance of about 1.25 milliohms across the frequency range of interest. I'd approach this in layers. First, the voltage regulator module (VRM) must have sufficient bandwidth and transient response — a multi-phase buck converter with fast loop response is essential, and I'd look at the VRM's output impedance curve to understand where its bandwidth rolls off. Below the VRM bandwidth, the VRM handles the transients; above it, the decoupling network must take over. Second, the bulk decoupling (electrolytic or high-value ceramic capacitors) handles the mid-frequency range, typically 10kHz to 1MHz. Third, the high-frequency decoupling — an array of small-value ceramic capacitors (0402 or smaller) placed as close as possible to the FPGA power pins — handles the 1MHz to 100MHz+ range. Fourth, the PCB power plane itself provides the lowest-impedance path at very high frequencies; I'd use a dedicated power plane for the core rail, placed adjacent to a ground plane with minimal dielectric thickness to maximize plane capacitance. I'd also consider embedded capacitance materials if the budget allows. The critical analysis is the frequency-domain target impedance: I'd model the entire PDN — VRM output impedance, capacitor arrays (including their ESL and ESR), plane inductance, and FPGA package inductance — and verify the impedance stays below the target across the full frequency range. I'd also perform time-domain transient simulation with a current profile that matches the FPGA's worst-case switching pattern. Finally, I'd verify on the bench with high-bandwidth probing at the FPGA power pins, measuring the transient response during actual operation.

- What measurement setup would you use to verify the PDN performance on the bench?

## Q4: How would you approach implementing a high-speed data path in an FPGA that must process a continuous stream of 16-bit samples at 500 MSPS, perform a 64-tap FIR filter, and output the filtered result without any dropped samples, while also meeting timing at the target clock frequency?

**Answer:** The first consideration is the clock architecture. A 500 MSPS sample rate with a 64-tap FIR filter is too fast for a single clock domain in most FPGAs — the filter would need to run at 500 MHz, which is challenging for the logic fabric. I'd use a parallel processing approach: either a time-division multiplexed (TDM) architecture with multiple filter instances running at a lower clock rate, or a polyphase decomposition of the FIR filter. For example, if I run the logic at 250 MHz, I'd process two samples per clock cycle using two parallel filter paths — this is a polyphase decomposition where the even samples go through one sub-filter and odd samples through another, with the results combined appropriately. For a 64-tap filter with 2x parallelism, each sub-filter has 32 taps. The key advantage is that the filter coefficients are the same, but the input data is decimated by 2 in each path, and the outputs are interleaved. I'd also use the FPGA's DSP slices for the multiply-accumulate operations — they're optimized for this and can run at higher clock rates than general logic. The filter structure would be systolic or semi-systolic to pipeline the adder tree and maintain throughput. I'd also consider using block RAM for coefficient storage if they're not constant, though for a fixed FIR filter, the coefficients would be hardcoded into the DSP slice configuration. For the input capture, I'd use the FPGA's I/O serializer/deserializer (ISERDES) to capture the 500 MSPS data — the parallel data bus from the ADC would be captured using the I/O clock at 500 MHz, then transferred to the fabric clock domain at 250 MHz using a small FIFO or register stage. The critical timing analysis would be on the DSP slice chain — ensuring the adder tree meets timing at 250 MHz, which typically requires careful pipelining. I'd also verify the design with simulation using realistic input data and compare against a reference model of the filter to ensure bit-exact correctness.

- How would you handle the case where the input data rate is 500 MSPS but the fabric clock is only 200 MHz — would you use a different parallelism factor?
- How would you verify that no samples are dropped during operation?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer on your team has implemented the clock distribution for a 500 MSPS ADC interface. During the review, you notice that the engineer has routed the clock trace with a via that creates a stub, and the via is placed very close to the ADC's clock input pin. The engineer argues that the stub is only 15 mils long and the clock is only 500 MHz, so it won't matter. How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a coaching opportunity, not just a design correction. First, I'd acknowledge the engineer's point — a 15-mil stub at 500 MHz does seem small, and it's reasonable to question whether it matters. But I'd then walk through the engineering analysis together. At 500 MHz, the wavelength in FR4 is roughly 12 inches, so a 15-mil stub is electrically very short — about 0.1% of a wavelength. The reflection coefficient from a stub this short is indeed negligible for the fundamental frequency. However, I'd point out that a 500 MHz clock has fast edges — the rise time might be 200-300 ps, which corresponds to a bandwidth of 1.5-2 GHz, not just the fundamental frequency. At those frequencies, the stub becomes more significant, though still small. The real question is whether the stub creates enough impedance discontinuity to cause jitter at the ADC clock input, and whether that jitter is within the ADC's timing budget. I'd suggest we quantify it: calculate the stub's effect on impedance, estimate the resulting jitter, and compare it against the ADC's aperture jitter specification. I'd also mention that the cost of fixing it now is trivial — moving the via or using a back-drilled via — while the cost of a marginal clock design is potentially a respin or a field failure. I'd frame it as: "Let's do the math together and decide based on data, not intuition." If the analysis shows it's truly negligible, I'd accept the design but document the analysis. If it shows risk, we fix it. The key is to teach the engineer how to evaluate signal integrity quantitatively rather than relying on rules of thumb, and to establish that design reviews are about rigorous analysis, not authority.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where the engineer's analysis shows the stub is truly negligible, but you still have a "gut feeling" it should be fixed?
- How would you teach a junior engineer to perform this kind of signal integrity analysis themselves in the future?
