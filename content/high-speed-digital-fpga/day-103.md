# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 103

## Q1: How would you approach designing the I/O timing constraints for an FPGA interface to an external source-synchronous ADC, where the data and forwarded clock arrive with a bounded but non-zero skew, and you need to guarantee capture across process, voltage, and temperature (PVT) corners?

**Answer:** The key is to stop thinking of the interface as a generic synchronous path and instead constrain it as a source-synchronous system with an explicit relationship between the forwarded clock and the data. I'd start by characterizing the ADC's output timing from its datasheet: the data valid window relative to the forwarded clock edge (setup and hold, or a data-valid window with a center or edge alignment), and the board-level skew introduced by the trace length mismatch between clock and data plus the connector. That gives me an arrival window at the FPGA pins.

On the FPGA side, I'd use the tool's input delay constraints (`set_input_delay` with min/max, referenced to the forwarded clock) rather than relying on default clock relationships, because the forwarded clock is not the same as the internal system clock — it's a separate clock that must be declared and, if it comes in on a clock-capable pin, routed through a BUFG or better a BUFIO/ISERDES path depending on the rate. For DDR capture I'd use the device's IDELAY/IDELAYCTRL primitives to shift the sampling point, and I'd budget the delay range so that the required tap falls comfortably inside the achievable range at all corners, not at the extreme.

The critical discipline is corner analysis: I'd run static timing at slow-slow, fast-fast, and the temperature extremes, because the data-valid window shrinks at one corner while the FPGA's setup/hold requirements tighten at another. If the window closes at any corner, the fix is usually to add a deliberate clock-to-data phase offset (via IDELAY on the clock or data) so the sampling edge sits in the middle of the window at the worst corner, then verify with a hardware eye scan or a built-in PRBS/pattern checker at temperature. I'd also make sure the constraints are documented and reviewed, because a missing input delay is exactly the kind of thing that passes timing analysis and fails in the field.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between edge-aligned and center-aligned capture for this interface? What would you do if the required IDELAY tap fell outside the primitive's range at the fast corner?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design that passes timing analysis at the nominal corner but produces occasional corrupted samples only when the board is cold (below the specified operating range), and the corruption disappears once the board warms up?

**Answer:** A cold-only failure points at a timing margin problem that the nominal-corner analysis doesn't capture, so my first move is to establish whether it's a setup or hold violation by looking at the failure signature. Cold often shifts device delays in a direction that eats hold margin on some paths and setup margin on others, and it also changes the behavior of the external components — oscillator startup, PLL lock time, and the ADC's own timing. So I'd separate "FPGA internal timing" from "interface timing" before touching anything.

Concretely: I'd re-run static timing at the cold temperature corner with the actual constraints, not just nominal, and check whether any paths go negative. If the internal timing is clean, I'd suspect the interface — the forwarded-clock capture point, the IDELAY calibration, or a PLL that hasn't fully locked before the logic starts consuming data. A cold-start sequencing bug (reset released before the PLL reports lock, or before the ADC's output is valid) is a very common cause of "works when warm" behavior, because warm restart timing differs.

I'd instrument with an on-chip logic analyzer or a lightweight error counter that flags the first bad sample and the state of the relevant control signals at that moment, then reproduce in a thermal chamber while sweeping temperature slowly. If I can correlate the first error with a specific event (PLL lock deassert, a calibration timeout, a marginal hold path), that narrows it fast. The fix depends on the cause: tighten the constraint and re-place if it's internal, add margin to the capture point or re-sequence reset if it's interface/startup, and then re-verify across the full cold-to-hot range rather than just the failing point.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a hold violation from a setup violation purely from the error pattern? What role does the PLL lock signal play in your reset sequencing?

## Q3: How would you approach deciding whether to use a vendor-provided hard memory controller versus building a soft controller in fabric for a DDR3 interface, given that both must meet the same bandwidth target?

**Answer:** I'd frame it as a trade between resource cost, timing closure risk, and control over the implementation. The hard controller lives in dedicated silicon, so it doesn't consume fabric logic or routing, it's characterized by the vendor across PVT, and it typically comes with the PHY, calibration, and leveling logic already integrated — which is a large amount of subtle, hard-to-verify work you don't have to own. The cost is reduced flexibility: you're bound to the vendor's feature set, its latency, and its supported configurations, and debugging inside it is limited to the visibility the vendor exposes.

A soft controller gives you full control over scheduling, arbitration, and latency, and it can be tailored to an unusual access pattern, but you own the timing closure of the whole datapath, the calibration and leveling algorithms, and the signal-integrity-sensitive PHY behavior. That's a significant verification burden, and getting write leveling and read capture right across corners is where soft controllers usually come unstuck.

My decision rule: if the access pattern is conventional (burst reads/writes, a handful of masters, standard bandwidth) and the device offers a hard controller, I'd default to the hard controller and spend my effort on the system around it — arbitration, buffering, and the fabric-side interface. I'd only go soft if there's a hard requirement the hard controller can't meet (a very specific latency bound, an unusual burst structure, or a device without a hard controller), and then I'd budget serious time for calibration and corner verification. Either way I'd validate the choice early with a bandwidth and latency model, because the controller decision is expensive to reverse late in the project.

**Possible follow-ups:** What bandwidth and latency factors would push you toward a soft controller? How would you verify a soft controller's calibration across corners before committing to production?

## Q4: How would you approach reducing radiated emissions on a high-speed digital board that carries several independent clock sources and a multi-gigabit serial link, without degrading the serial link's bit error rate?

**Answer:** I'd start by identifying the dominant emitters rather than applying fixes broadly, because EMI work is easy to over-apply and hard to undo. The usual suspects on a board like this are the highest-frequency periodic sources — the serial reference clock and its harmonics, the memory interface's strobe and data switching, and any clock that runs continuously and couples to cables or chassis. A near-field probe scan and a spectrum sweep with the board in its enclosure tells me which source and which coupling path (radiated from a trace, conducted out a cable, or leaking through a seam) actually matters.

For clock sources, spread-spectrum clocking is the highest-leverage tool when the standard permits it: modulating the clock frequency slightly spreads the harmonic energy and can drop peak emissions substantially. The catch is that SSC changes the instantaneous frequency, which can stress a serial link's CDR if the modulation rate or depth is too aggressive, so I'd apply SSC to the sources that don't feed the high-speed serial reference, or use a shallow, slow profile on the ones that do, and re-measure BER afterward. For the serial link itself, I'd keep the reference clean and rely on other measures: common-mode chokes on I/O and power lines leaving the board, ferrite beads and filtering on cable interfaces, shielding cans over the noisiest clock circuitry, and careful stack-up so high-speed traces are referenced to a solid plane and don't run near board edges or seams.

The PCB-level fixes — edge plating, stitching vias around the perimeter, keeping return paths continuous under every high-speed trace — address radiated coupling without touching signal integrity. I'd verify by re-running the emissions scan and the link's BER/eye test together, because the whole point is to confirm the EMI fix didn't buy emissions margin at the cost of link margin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide how much SSC depth is safe for a given serial link? What would you check first if emissions improved but BER degraded after adding a common-mode choke?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA board, and a junior engineer has proposed a stack-up that you believe will cause impedance and crosstalk problems on the high-speed lanes. They've run a simulation that shows it working and are confident. How do you handle the situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat it as a technical disagreement to resolve with evidence, not a hierarchy problem, and I'd be careful not to dismiss their simulation out of hand — if they've done the work, that deserves respect and a real look. My first step is to understand exactly what their simulation modeled: the stack-up geometry, the dielectric properties, the trace spacing and reference planes, and critically what it did *not* model. Simulations of high-speed lanes often look clean because they assume ideal references, ignore the connector and via transitions, or use nominal dielectric constants without corner variation. If the model is missing the via stubs, the connector launch, or the return-path discontinuities, the "it works" result is answering a narrower question than the one that matters.

So I'd ask them to walk me through the assumptions, and I'd propose we extend the model together to include the transitions and the corner dielectric values, and compare against the impedance target and crosstalk budget. If it still passes with the full model, I'd genuinely reconsider my concern — I've been wrong before, and a well-constructed simulation is strong evidence. If it fails once the transitions are included, the disagreement resolves itself on technical grounds and they've learned something valuable about what a simulation has to include to be trustworthy.

Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative and frame it as "let's make the model match the real board," not "you're wrong." The goal is a correct stack-up and an engineer who trusts the review process, and both are served by resolving it with data rather than authority.

**Possible follow-ups:** What specific things would you check in their simulation setup to judge whether it's trustworthy? How would you handle it if they turned out to be right and your concern was unfounded?
