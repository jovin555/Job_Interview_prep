# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 90

## Q1: How would you approach designing the power-up and configuration sequence for an FPGA that loads its bitstream from external flash, when the flash device itself has a non-trivial access latency and the board may be power-cycled rapidly?

**Answer:** The core problem is that the FPGA's configuration controller starts issuing commands to the flash as soon as its configuration supply is valid, but the flash may still be in its own power-up or wake-from-deep-power state and not yet able to respond. If the FPGA doesn't get a valid response, it either retries (if the controller supports it) or aborts configuration and the board comes up dead — intermittently, depending on how the two rails happen to ramp relative to each other.

My approach would be to treat this as a sequencing and handshake problem rather than a timing-margin problem. First, I'd establish the power sequencing so the flash's supply is valid and stable before the FPGA's configuration bank is released — either through a dedicated sequencer, a supervisor IC with a power-good chain, or by gating the FPGA's config supply with a load switch controlled by the flash's power-good. Second, I'd check whether the FPGA's configuration controller supports a config-rate divider or a startup delay; slowing the initial configuration clock gives the flash time to complete its internal initialization without needing external logic. Third, I'd look at whether the flash has a dedicated reset or hold pin that can be driven by the FPGA (or by a supervisor) to hold it in a known state until the FPGA is ready to talk to it.

For rapid power-cycling, the concern is that the flash may not have fully discharged between cycles, so its internal state machine is in an undefined state when power returns. A supervisor with a proper reset threshold and a minimum reset delay handles this — you want the flash held in reset until its supply has crossed the threshold and stayed there for the specified time. I'd also verify the bulk capacitance on the flash rail is adequate so a fast cycle doesn't leave it partially charged.

Verification: I'd characterize the ramp on the bench with a programmable supply and a relay to simulate fast cycling, capture the config clock and flash chip-select with a scope, and confirm the first command is issued only after the flash is ready. I'd repeat across temperature and across multiple boards, because the failure mode is marginal and won't show up on a single unit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to solve this in hardware sequencing versus in the FPGA's configuration controller settings? What would you do if the flash vendor's datasheet doesn't specify a wake-up time?

## Q2: How would you approach mitigating via stub effects on a high-speed serial channel that must transition between the top and bottom layers of a thick backplane?

**Answer:** A via that passes through a thick backplane but is only used to connect an inner layer has an unused portion — the stub — that behaves as an open-circuited transmission line. At multi-gigabit rates that stub creates a resonance that shows up as a notch in the channel's insertion loss and a corresponding peak in return loss, and it degrades the eye at exactly the frequency content the link needs. The longer the stub relative to the data rate's wavelength, the worse it gets, and a thick backplane makes it worse because the stub is physically long.

The first-line mitigation is to avoid creating the stub in the first place: use blind or buried vias so the via only spans the layers it needs to connect, or use backdrilling to remove the unused barrel below the last connected layer. Backdrilling is the standard production answer for thick backplanes — it removes the stub mechanically, at the cost of an extra drill step and a small annular-ring tolerance to account for. The depth control matters: you have to leave enough of the barrel to guarantee the connection but remove enough to push the stub resonance above the band of interest.

If backdrilling isn't available — cost, panel size, or the fabricator can't hold the depth tolerance — the next options are to move the transition to a thinner region of the board, to route the signal on layers closer together so the stub is short, or to accept the stub and compensate at the link level with transmit pre-emphasis and receiver equalization, provided the channel budget still closes. That's a trade-off: you're spending link margin to buy back a manufacturing simplification, and you need to verify with a channel simulation that the equalizer can actually recover the eye at the target BER, not just that the link "comes up."

Verification: I'd extract the via with a 3D field solver, not a 2D approximation, because the stub resonance is a 3D effect. Then I'd run the full channel — package, via, connector, backplane trace, via, package — through an IBIS-AMI or similar link simulation and look at the eye and BER contour at the receiver. On hardware, I'd measure with a VNA on a test coupon that replicates the via stack, and confirm the notch is where the simulation predicted.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the backdrill depth tolerance, and what happens if the fabricator drills too deep? What's the trade-off between backdrilling and moving to a blind/buried via process?

## Q3: How would you approach choosing between a global clock network and a regional/banked clock resource for a logic block that only needs to run at a modest rate, when the device's global clock resources are nearly exhausted?

**Answer:** The instinct is to treat global clock resources as precious and push everything possible onto regional clocks, but the decision has real consequences for timing, skew, and place-and-route, so I'd work through it rather than defaulting.

First, I'd ask what the clock actually needs to do. A global clock network is designed for low skew across the whole die and for fanout to arbitrary logic — that's what you're paying for in routing resources and power. A regional or banked clock is cheaper in those resources but confines the loads it can reach and typically has different (often higher) skew characteristics and a more limited fanout. So the question is whether the logic driven by this clock is localized or spread out. If it's a small block that will place in one region, a regional clock is a good fit. If it drives logic that P&R will want to spread across the die — or if it has to interact with logic on a global clock — the regional clock becomes a constraint that fights the placer.

Second, I'd look at whether the clock is truly independent or whether it's related to a global clock. If it's the same frequency and phase as a global clock (e.g., a divided version), I'd consider whether it can be generated from the same MMCM/PLL and distributed on the global network, or whether it can be a clock enable on the global clock instead of a separate clock. A clock enable is often the cheapest answer: it keeps everything on one global clock, avoids a new clock domain entirely, and costs only the enable logic. That's frequently the right call for "modest rate" logic.

Third, if a separate clock really is needed, I'd check the device's clocking architecture — how many regional clocks per bank, whether they can be driven from the MMCM, whether they can reach the I/O and the memory controller. Some regional clocks can't drive certain hard blocks, which would force the decision.

The trade-off I'd articulate: global clocks cost routing and power but give you placement freedom and predictable skew; regional clocks save those resources but constrain placement and may complicate timing closure if the logic grows. If global resources are nearly exhausted, I'd first audit whether some existing global clocks are being used for logic that could move to a clock enable or a regional clock — reclaiming a global clock is often better than squeezing the new one onto a regional network.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify after implementation that the regional clock's skew is acceptable for the logic it drives? What are the failure modes if you put logic on a regional clock and the placer later needs to spread it?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the timing analysis reports that all constraints are met, but the design fails in hardware only when the device is operated at the maximum specified junction temperature?

**Answer:** Static timing analysis signs off at a specific corner — typically slow-slow process, worst-case voltage, and a junction temperature the tool assumes. If the design passes at that corner but fails hot in hardware, the first question is whether the corner the tool analyzed actually matches the hardware condition. The most common causes are: the constraint set doesn't cover a path that matters (a false path or a missing clock relationship that lets a real path go unanalyzed), the tool's temperature assumption is optimistic relative to the actual junction temperature, or the failure isn't a timing failure at all but a thermal or electrical one — a marginal supply, a reference clock drifting, a component whose behavior changes with temperature.

I'd start by separating "timing" from "everything else." Reproduce the failure in a controlled way — heat the board in a chamber or with a hot-air tool while running a test pattern that exercises the failing function, and confirm the failure temperature is repeatable. Then instrument: bring out a debug signal or use the device's internal logic analyzer to see whether the failure is a setup violation (data arriving late), a hold violation (data changing too early), or something else entirely — a PLL losing lock, a memory interface failing calibration, a supply drooping.

If it looks like a timing margin issue, I'd re-run STA at the actual worst-case junction temperature and the actual voltage the board delivers at that temperature, and compare slack. I'd also check whether the design has any paths that the tool marked as false or multicycle that are actually real — those are the classic "passes STA, fails silicon" culprits. And I'd check whether the failing path is a clock domain crossing that was "verified" by a synchronizer but has a data path that's too long for the destination clock period at temperature.

If it's not timing, I'd look at the power delivery: at max junction temperature the leakage current is highest, the regulator may be at its limit, and the core voltage may sag on transient loads — which shows up as timing-like failures. I'd scope the rails at the failure point. I'd also check the reference clock's temperature stability and the PLL's lock behavior over temperature.

The fix depends on the cause: if it's a real timing path, constrain it correctly and close it; if it's a supply margin issue, fix the PDN or the sequencing; if it's a component behavior, derate or change the component. The key discipline is not to "fix" it by loosening a constraint until the tool agrees — that just moves the failure.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a setup failure from a hold failure using on-chip debug? What would you check first if the failure only appears after the board has been hot for several minutes rather than immediately?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer on your team has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** The technical issue is real and not negotiable — an unsynchronized signal crossing into another clock domain is a metastability hazard, and "stable long enough" is an assumption about timing that isn't guaranteed by the design. But the way I'd handle it depends on the person and the setting, and the goal is to get the design fixed and the engineer to understand why, not to win the review.

In the review itself, I'd slow down and ask questions rather than pronounce. I'd ask them to walk me through the timing: what's the source clock, what's the destination clock, what's the relationship between them, and what guarantees the signal is stable across the destination clock edge every time. Usually the engineer will realize the guarantee doesn't exist once they articulate it — the "stable long enough" claim is really "stable relative to the source clock," which says nothing about the destination clock's sampling instant. If they don't get there, I'd explain metastability concretely: the destination flop can go meta when the input changes within its setup/hold window, and the probability isn't zero just because it's low, especially across thousands of boards and years of operation.

I'd also make it clear this isn't a criticism of their competence — it's one of the most common mistakes in digital design, and the fix is standard. Depending on the signal, the fix is a two-flop synchronizer for a single-bit level signal, a handshake or FIFO for multi-bit data, or a pulse synchronizer for a one-cycle pulse. I'd ask them to propose which one fits and come back with the change, so they own the fix.

If the engineer pushed back — "it's been running fine in the lab" — I'd point out that's exactly the signature of a metastability bug: it works until it doesn't, and it fails rarely and unpredictably, which is the worst kind of field failure. I'd frame the standard as "we don't ship designs that rely on probability for correctness" rather than "I'm the lead and I said so."

After the review, I'd follow up to make sure the fix is in and that the CDC is covered by whatever CDC checking the team uses — a lint tool or a formal CDC check — so this class of issue gets caught systematically rather than depending on review attention.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer implemented the fix but didn't seem to internalize why it mattered? What would you do if the same engineer made a similar CDC mistake on a later design?
