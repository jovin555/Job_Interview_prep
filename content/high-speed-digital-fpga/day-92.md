# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 92

## Q1: How would you approach designing a spread-spectrum clocking scheme for a high-speed digital board where several clock sources (processor, FPGA, and a high-speed serial reference) must coexist, and you need to reduce radiated emissions without breaking the serial links?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd establish is which clocks are allowed to be modulated and which are not. Spread-spectrum clocking (SSC) works by deliberately modulating the clock frequency with a low-frequency profile — typically down-tracking or center-spread — so the energy that would otherwise sit at a single harmonic gets smeared across a band, lowering the peak. The catch is that any receiver that must recover a clock from that stream, or any link whose reference is shared with a modulated source, will see the modulation as jitter. So the decision tree is: for the processor and FPGA system clocks, SSC is usually acceptable because they're consumed by on-board PLLs that can track the slow modulation; for a high-speed serial reference, I'd be very cautious — many SerDes standards either forbid SSC on the reference or require the transmitter and receiver to use the same modulation profile, and mixing a clean reference with a modulated one, or vice versa, can cause the CDR to lose lock or the link to report excessive jitter.

Practically, I'd start by identifying the dominant emission sources from a pre-compliance scan or from first principles — the highest-frequency, highest-slew signals are usually the culprits. If the FPGA's system clock is a major contributor, I'd enable SSC on that source with a modulation depth and rate that stays within the FPGA PLL's tracking bandwidth, and verify the PLL still locks and the internal timing margins don't degrade. For the serial reference, I'd keep it clean unless the standard explicitly permits SSC, and instead attack the emission at the board level — common-mode chokes on the reference path, careful return-path design, and shielding if needed. The key discipline is to treat SSC as one tool among several and to validate it end-to-end: a spectrum scan to confirm the emission reduction, plus link margin and PLL lock measurements across temperature and voltage to confirm nothing broke.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you verify that the SSC modulation profile is actually being tracked correctly by a downstream PLL rather than causing intermittent loss of lock? What modulation depth and rate would you consider typical, and what constrains those choices?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the device configures successfully and runs correctly, but after some time the configuration appears to be lost — the device stops responding and only recovers after a full reconfiguration?

**Answer:** This is a classic "configuration loss" symptom, and I'd treat it as a power-integrity and reset-integrity investigation first, because a genuine loss of configuration is relatively rare compared to a device that has been driven into a state where it looks dead. The first question is whether the device is actually losing its bitstream or whether something else — a hung PLL, a stuck state machine, a brown-out on an I/O rail — is making it unresponsive. I'd instrument the board to monitor the configuration-related status pins and any "init done" or "ready" handshake the system exposes, and log them over time so I can see whether the failure correlates with a supply glitch, a temperature rise, or a specific traffic pattern.

On the power side, I'd look hard at the core and auxiliary rails for transient droops — a fast current step from the fabric can pull a rail below the device's brown-out threshold momentarily, and depending on the device that can trigger a reconfiguration or leave it in an indeterminate state. I'd scope the rails with a wide bandwidth probe at the device pins, not at the regulator output, and look for droops that coincide with the failure. I'd also check the configuration flash interface — if the device re-reads or checks its bitstream periodically, a marginal flash access or a slow flash wake-up after a power event can cause exactly this. On the reset side, I'd verify that the reset supervisor holds reset through the full ramp of every rail and releases it cleanly, and that there's no glitch on the reset line from nearby switching.

If the evidence points to a true configuration loss, I'd look at whether the device is being disturbed by an external event — an ESD strike, a supply transient, or a watchdog that's resetting it — and whether the configuration memory is being corrupted by something like an incomplete reconfiguration attempt. The fix is usually a combination of hardening the power sequencing and reset, adding a supervisory device that can force a clean reconfiguration on failure, and confirming the flash interface has adequate margin.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a true configuration loss and a device that's simply hung but still configured? What role would a watchdog or external supervisor play in your recovery strategy?

## Q3: How would you approach choosing between a global clock network and a regional/banked clock resource for a logic block that only needs to run at a modest rate, when the device's global clock resources are nearly exhausted?

**Answer:** The global clock network is a scarce, high-fanout resource with low skew and low jitter, and it's the right choice for clocks that genuinely need to reach logic spread across the whole device — the system clock, the memory controller clock, the transceiver reference distribution. When those resources are nearly exhausted, the question becomes whether a given clock actually needs global reach or whether its fanout is confined to a region. A logic block running at a modest rate, with all of its consumers physically clustered, is a strong candidate for a regional or banked clock resource, because the regional network has adequate performance for that use and frees a global resource for something that truly needs it.

The trade-offs I'd weigh: regional clocks typically have somewhat higher skew and may have restrictions on which clock-capable pins or routing they can reach, so I'd confirm the placement of the logic and the clock source is compatible with the regional resource. I'd also check whether the clock needs to interact with logic in another region — if the block's outputs feed logic far away, the clock itself may not need to travel, but any synchronous interface between regions does, and that's where a CDC or a shared global clock becomes necessary. Another consideration is whether the tool will actually honor the regional assignment; I'd constrain it explicitly and verify in the implemented design that the clock is on the intended resource and that timing is met.

The broader principle is to treat global clock resources as an architecture budget: inventory them early, assign them to the clocks that need device-wide reach, and push everything else onto regional resources or, where possible, derive slower clocks locally from a global source rather than adding another global net.

**Possible follow-ups:** What are the risks of deriving a slower clock locally from a global clock versus using a dedicated regional clock source? How would you verify after implementation that the regional clock assignment was actually honored?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the decoupling network for an FPGA's core voltage rail, where the rail supplies a large, fast-changing current to the fabric and must stay within a tight tolerance across a broad frequency range?

**Answer:** I'd think about it as a impedance problem across frequency rather than a "how many capacitors" problem. The goal is to keep the power distribution network's impedance below a target — derived from the allowable voltage ripple divided by the worst-case transient current — from the regulator's control bandwidth all the way up to the frequencies where the on-die capacitance takes over. That means the network has to be designed in layers: bulk capacitance to handle the low-frequency, large-energy transients and to keep the regulator from having to respond instantly; mid-frequency ceramic capacitance to cover the band where the bulk caps' ESR and ESL make them ineffective; and high-frequency, low-ESL capacitance placed as close to the device pins as physically possible to cover the fast edges.

The critical practical points are loop inductance and placement. Every capacitor's effectiveness is limited by the inductance of its mounting and the path back to the device, so I'd prioritize short, wide connections and via-in-pad or via-adjacent placement for the high-frequency caps, and I'd be careful about spreading caps across the board where their loop inductance defeats them. I'd also model the plane pair — the power and ground planes form a natural high-frequency capacitor, and their spacing and dielectric matter. For a rail with a fast current step, I'd run a PDN impedance simulation across frequency, identify the anti-resonances where the network impedance peaks, and add or adjust capacitance to damp them.

Verification before layout sign-off is a combination of simulation and, where possible, measurement on a test coupon or a first-article board. I'd measure the rail impedance with a network analyzer or a two-port probe, and I'd measure the transient response with a fast current step — either a dedicated load or the device itself under a stress pattern — looking at the voltage deviation at the device pins. If the deviation exceeds the tolerance, I'd iterate on capacitance value, placement, or regulator compensation.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you identify and damp an anti-resonance between two capacitor banks with different self-resonant frequencies? What measurement would you trust most to confirm the PDN is adequate before committing to a production layout?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a teaching moment rather than a confrontation, because the engineer's reasoning is a very common and understandable shortcut — and the fix is usually small. My first move in the review would be to slow down and make the failure mode concrete rather than abstract. I'd ask the engineer to walk me through the timing: how long is the signal stable relative to the destination clock period, and what happens if the destination clock edge lands exactly on a transition? The point I'd want them to reach themselves is that "stable long enough" is a statement about typical behavior, not about the worst case — metastability is a probabilistic event that depends on the relationship between the signal transition and the sampling clock, and over millions of samples and across temperature and voltage, the probability of a bad capture is not zero. A single metastable capture on a critical control signal can put the design into an illegal state, and that's exactly the kind of failure that's hardest to debug in the field.

Once we're aligned on the risk, I'd work with them on the fix. For a single-bit control signal, a two-flop synchronizer is the standard, cheap solution, and I'd explain why it works — the first flop may go metastable, but the second flop gives the metastability time to resolve before the signal is used. If the signal is multi-bit or needs to be captured as a coherent value, we'd talk about handshaking or a proper CDC structure instead. I'd also make sure the design has a CDC lint check or a formal CDC verification step in the flow, so this class of issue gets caught systematically rather than relying on review vigilance.

The tone matters: I'd frame it as "here's a pattern worth internalizing" rather than "you got this wrong," and I'd follow up after the review to confirm the fix and make sure the engineer understands the general principle, because the same reasoning will come up again on other signals.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure a CDC verification step in the design flow so that unsynchronized crossings are caught automatically? If the signal were multi-bit and changed frequently, how would your guidance to the engineer change?
