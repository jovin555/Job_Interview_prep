# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 87

## Q1: How would you approach designing the clock domain crossing for a multi-bit Gray-coded counter value that increments continuously in a fast domain (e.g., 250 MHz) and must be sampled by a slower domain (e.g., 50 MHz) for logging, where the sampled value must always be a coherent snapshot and never a corrupted mix of bits?

**Answer:** The key insight is that Gray coding exists precisely to make this problem tractable: because only one bit changes per increment, a value sampled mid-transition can only be off by one count, never a scrambled mix of bits. So the design is: keep the counter itself in binary for arithmetic, then convert to Gray with a combinational XOR reduction before it leaves the fast domain. In the slow domain, register the Gray value through a two-flop synchronizer to resolve metastability, then convert back to binary for logging. The critical rule is that the Gray code must be registered in the source domain before crossing — you cannot synchronize a combinational Gray output directly, because glitches on the XOR tree would violate the single-bit-change guarantee. I'd also add a small handshake or a "data valid" toggle if the consumer needs to know the sample is fresh, and I'd constrain the synchronizer flops with the appropriate CDC attributes so the tool doesn't retime or optimize them away. The trade-off to be aware of: Gray coding only works for counters that increment or decrement by one; if the value can jump arbitrarily, you need a different scheme (e.g., a handshake or a dual-clock FIFO).

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What happens if the counter increments by more than one per cycle — does the Gray-code approach still hold?
- How would you verify in simulation that the CDC is actually safe, given that metastability is a probabilistic effect?

## Q2: How would you approach mitigating via stub effects on a high-speed serial channel that must transition between the top and bottom layers of a thick backplane?

**Answer:** A via stub is the unused portion of a plated through-hole below the layer where the signal enters or exits — it acts as an open-ended transmission line stub that resonates at a quarter-wave frequency and causes a notch in the channel's insertion loss, which shows up as jitter and eye closure at high data rates. The first thing I'd do is quantify the problem: estimate the stub length from the stack-up, compute the quarter-wave resonance (roughly, resonance frequency ≈ c / (4 × stub length × √εr)), and compare it to the signal's Nyquist frequency and its harmonics. If the resonance is well above the band of interest, no action is needed. If it's in-band, the options in rough order of preference are: (1) back-drill the via to remove the stub, which is the most effective and is standard practice on thick backplanes; (2) use blind or buried vias so the stub never exists in the first place, though this raises fabrication cost and constrains the stack-up; (3) route the signal on a layer close to the bottom so the stub is short; or (4) accept the loss and compensate with equalization — pre-emphasis on the transmitter or a CTLE/DFE in the receiver — but that's a band-aid, not a fix. I'd also verify with a 3D electromagnetic simulation of the via transition, because the simple quarter-wave formula ignores the via's capacitance and the antipad geometry.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide how much back-drilling depth is enough without over-drilling into the signal layer?
- What other via-related effects besides the stub would you check at multi-gigabit rates?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the decoupling network for an FPGA's core voltage rail, where the rail supplies a large, fast-changing current to the fabric and must stay within a tight tolerance across a broad frequency range?

**Answer:** The core rail's decoupling problem is fundamentally a broadband impedance problem: the goal is to keep the power distribution network's impedance below a target — roughly the allowable voltage ripple divided by the worst-case transient current — across the frequency range from DC up to where the on-die capacitance takes over. I'd approach it in layers. At the low-frequency end, the bulk capacitors and the regulator's control loop handle the slower transients; the regulator's bandwidth sets where that handoff occurs. In the mid-band, a spread of ceramic capacitors with different values and packages covers the decade or so where the bulk caps' ESL makes them ineffective — the point is not to use many identical caps but to stagger values so their self-resonant frequencies overlap and there are no impedance peaks between them. At the high-frequency end, the on-package and on-die capacitance dominates, and the PCB's job is just to get charge there with low inductance: short, wide traces or planes, vias placed close to the capacitor pads, and minimal loop area between the cap and the FPGA's power pins. I'd verify the design by extracting the PDN impedance from the layout — including plane spreading inductance and via inductance — and simulating it against the target impedance curve, then confirming with a VNA measurement on the first article if the rail is critical. The classic mistake is treating it as a capacitor-count problem rather than an impedance-versus-frequency problem.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle the anti-resonance peak that can appear between two capacitor values with different self-resonant frequencies?
- What role does the FPGA's on-die capacitance play, and how would you find out how much there is?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the timing analysis reports that all constraints are met, but the design fails in hardware only when the device is operated at the maximum specified junction temperature?

**Answer:** A clean timing report at the corner the tool analyzed, combined with a failure that only appears hot, points to one of a few things. First, I'd check which corner the timing analysis actually covered — if the report is only for the slow corner and the failure is at hot, that's suspicious, because hot is typically the slow corner for most processes, but not always, and the tool's derating may not match the real silicon. Second, I'd suspect that the constraint set is incomplete: a path that isn't constrained won't show up as a violation, and temperature-dependent effects like increased interconnect resistance or reduced drive strength can push an unconstrained or falsely-constrained path over the edge. Third, I'd look at whether the failure is really timing at all — thermal effects can change oscillator frequency, PLL lock behavior, or analog front-end performance, so I'd instrument the design with a debug core or use a scope on a test point to see whether the failure is a setup/hold issue or something else. Fourth, I'd check the power delivery: at high temperature, the regulator's efficiency and the capacitors' ESR change, so a marginal rail could dip further under load. My debugging sequence would be: reproduce at temperature with a controlled oven or thermal chamber, narrow down which block fails, then use the tool's timing report at the hot corner specifically, and cross-check against a gate-level simulation with back-annotated delays if the failure is subtle.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine whether the failure is a timing violation or a functional issue that only manifests at temperature?
- What would you do if the tool's hot-corner timing report is clean but the hardware still fails?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a teaching moment rather than a confrontation, because the engineer's reasoning isn't unreasonable on its face — it's just incomplete. The first thing I'd do is ask them to walk me through the timing: how long is the signal stable relative to the destination clock period, and what happens if the signal changes within the setup/hold window of the destination flop? The point I'd want them to reach themselves is that "stable long enough" is a statement about the typical case, not the worst case, and metastability is a probabilistic phenomenon that doesn't care about typical. I'd then explain the standard fix — a two-flop synchronizer for a single-bit level signal, or a handshake or FIFO for a multi-bit bus — and why the two-flop synchronizer works: it doesn't eliminate metastability, it gives it a full clock period to resolve before the value is used. I'd also point out that this is exactly the kind of thing that passes simulation and fails in the field, often only on a fraction of boards or at a particular temperature, which makes it expensive to find later. If the engineer pushed back, I'd offer to run a quick experiment: inject a signal that violates setup/hold in simulation and show the metastable behavior, or point to the vendor's CDC guidelines. The goal is to leave them with a durable mental model, not just a corrected netlist. I'd also make sure the review checklist captures this class of issue so it's caught earlier next time.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the engineer is senior and has been doing this for years without a problem?
- What would you add to the design review process to catch CDC issues systematically rather than relying on catching them by eye?
