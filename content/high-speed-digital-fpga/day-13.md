# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 13

## Q1: How would you approach designing a clock domain crossing (CDC) scheme for a multi-bit data bus where the source and destination clocks are asynchronous and have no known frequency relationship, and the data must be transferred without corruption?

**Answer:** For fully asynchronous CDC with no frequency relationship, I'd use a multi-stage approach. First, I'd determine whether the data is continuous or burst-based. For continuous multi-bit data, an asynchronous FIFO is the standard solution—it handles the gray-code pointer synchronization across domains and provides proper full/empty flag generation with multi-stage synchronizers. For the FIFO implementation, I'd use vendor-provided, verified asynchronous FIFO IP where possible, or carefully implement one with gray-coded read/write pointers to minimize multi-bit signal transitions. The key design considerations are: proper synchronizer chains (typically 2-3 flip-flops) for the pointer signals, ensuring the FIFO depth is sufficient for the maximum burst size plus synchronization latency, and careful attention to the full/empty flag generation to avoid false flags that could cause data loss or overrun. I'd also add assertions in simulation to verify no data corruption occurs across the full range of clock phase relationships and frequency ratios. For control signals that change infrequently, I'd use a simpler approach—a pulse synchronizer or handshake protocol (request/acknowledge) rather than a FIFO, since that's more area-efficient for sparse signals.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine the required FIFO depth for a given burst pattern? What are the risks of using a handshake protocol for high-frequency data transfers?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging a timing failure in an FPGA design that only occurs after place-and-route, when the post-synthesis simulation passes timing cleanly?

**Answer:** This is a classic symptom of issues that only manifest after physical implementation. I'd start by examining the timing report from the place-and-route tool, focusing on the specific failing paths. Common culprits include: unconstrained or incorrectly constrained paths (missing false paths, improper clock definitions, missing input/output delays), clock skew introduced by routing, and congestion causing long detours. I'd verify that all clocks are properly defined with correct frequencies and that all asynchronous crossings are properly constrained as false paths or with appropriate CDC synchronization. Next, I'd check the placement—if critical paths are spread across the device rather than localized, routing delays can exceed the budget. I'd also look at resource utilization; if the design is heavily congested (>85-90% in a region), the router may struggle to meet timing. The fix might involve: adding pipeline stages to break up long combinational paths, adjusting synthesis options (retiming, register duplication), or modifying placement constraints. I'd also verify that the timing constraints accurately reflect the real system requirements—sometimes the constraint itself is overly pessimistic or incorrect, and the design actually works in hardware. Finally, I'd run the design in hardware with a logic analyzer or chipscope to see if the design actually fails at speed, since timing analysis can sometimes flag paths that don't affect real functionality.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish between a constraint problem and a real logic path problem? What synthesis attributes or options would you consider to help the router meet timing?

## Q3: How would you approach designing a power distribution network (PDN) for an FPGA that has multiple voltage rails (core, I/O, auxiliary, transceiver) with different current demands and transient response requirements?

**Answer:** I'd approach PDN design systematically, starting with the FPGA's power requirements from the datasheet—voltage tolerances, maximum and transient currents, and decoupling recommendations. The design has three main layers: the voltage regulators, the PCB power planes, and the on-die/package decoupling. For the regulators, I'd select appropriate DC-DC converters or LDOs based on current requirements and noise sensitivity—for example, transceiver and PLL rails typically need cleaner power than core logic. I'd pay attention to the regulator's transient response and loop stability, especially for rails with high di/dt demands. For the PCB, I'd design dedicated power planes with proper stack-up—thin dielectric between power and ground planes creates low-inductance capacitance. The decoupling strategy is critical: I'd place a range of capacitor values (bulk, mid-frequency, and high-frequency) close to the FPGA power pins, following the vendor's recommended decoupling patterns. The key is to create a low-impedance path across a wide frequency range—bulk capacitors handle low-frequency transients, while small-value capacitors (100nF, 10nF, 1nF) handle higher frequencies. I'd also consider the mounting inductance of the capacitors—smaller packages (0402, 0201) have lower parasitic inductance. For verification, I'd perform a PDN simulation using tools like SIwave or PowerSI to check impedance across frequency, targeting the recommended impedance profile (typically below a few milliohms at the FPGA's switching frequencies). I'd also verify the voltage ripple at the FPGA pins with an oscilloscope during hardware bring-up, using short ground-spring probes to minimize measurement artifacts.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the interaction between multiple regulators sharing the same ground plane? What's your approach to selecting capacitor values and quantities for a specific rail?

## Q4: How would you approach implementing a high-speed data path in an FPGA that must process a continuous stream of 16-bit samples at 500 MSPS, perform a finite impulse response (FIR) filter with 64 taps, and output the filtered result without any dropped samples?

**Answer:** At 500 MSPS with 16-bit samples, this is a demanding throughput requirement that needs careful architecture. The first consideration is whether the FPGA can even clock the data path at 500 MHz—if not, I'd need to use a parallel processing approach. A common technique is time-division multiplexing: process multiple samples per clock cycle using parallel filter banks. For example, if the FPGA can run at 250 MHz, I'd process two samples per cycle using two parallel filter paths with appropriate cross-terms to handle the filter's state across samples. The FIR filter itself can be implemented using the DSP slices efficiently—a 64-tap filter at these rates would likely require a polyphase or multi-channel decomposition. I'd use the FPGA's DSP48 slices in a systolic array configuration, where each tap is a multiply-accumulate operation, and pipeline the data through the array. The key design considerations are: managing the filter's state across parallel channels (each channel needs its own delay line), ensuring the MAC operations are properly pipelined to meet timing, and handling the output summation tree efficiently. I'd also consider using the block RAM to store filter coefficients and possibly the delay line if it's too large for registers. For verification, I'd create a testbench that compares the FPGA output against a bit-exact C model of the filter, running both with the same input stimulus. I'd also verify the design handles the continuous stream without dropped samples by monitoring for FIFO overflow or pipeline stalls—the design must sustain the throughput indefinitely, not just in bursts.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the filter's initial transient response when the pipeline is filling? What's the trade-off between using DSP slices versus logic resources for the multipliers?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer on your team has designed the clock distribution for a 250 MHz ADC interface. During the review, you notice that the engineer has routed the clock trace with a 90-degree bend, creating a potential impedance discontinuity. The engineer argues that the bend is only 10 mils wide and the clock is only 250 MHz, so it won't matter. How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd approach this as a coaching opportunity while ensuring the design quality is maintained. First, I'd acknowledge the engineer's point—at 250 MHz, the wavelength is roughly 60 cm in FR4, and a 10-mil bend is electrically very small, so the impedance discontinuity is indeed minor. However, I'd explain that the issue is about design consistency and best practices, not just this specific trace. I'd walk through the reasoning: while a single 90-degree bend at this frequency may not cause measurable signal integrity problems, the design rule exists to prevent cumulative issues—when you have many bends, or when the design is later modified for higher frequencies, the practice of using 45-degree bends or curved traces becomes important. I'd also point out that the ADC's clock is critical for SNR—any jitter or reflection on the clock directly degrades the ADC's effective number of bits (ENOB). I'd suggest we measure the actual impact using simulation—run a quick signal integrity analysis to show the engineer the real effect of the bend versus a 45-degree alternative. This turns the discussion from opinion into data. If the simulation shows negligible difference, I'd still recommend the 45-degree bend for consistency with the rest of the board and future-proofing, but I'd acknowledge the engineer's analysis was valid. The key is to teach the engineer to think about when design rules matter and when they're conservative, rather than blindly following or ignoring rules.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation if the engineer's design was already in manufacturing and the bend was discovered after the fact? What's your approach to establishing design rules for your team that balance best practices with practical constraints?
