# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 77

## Q1: How would you approach designing the power-up and configuration sequence for an FPGA that loads its bitstream from external flash, when the flash device itself has a non-trivial access latency and the board may be power-cycled rapidly?

**Answer:** The core issue is that configuration is a multi-party handshake between the power rails, the FPGA's internal configuration controller, and an external memory device that has its own timing quirks. I'd start by treating the power rails as a precondition, not a parallel activity: the FPGA's configuration logic typically won't begin until the core and I/O rails are within tolerance and the internal power-on reset has released, so the sequencing circuit (a supervisor IC or a CPLD-based sequencer) must guarantee that ramp order and that all rails are stable before the FPGA is allowed to start. If the flash is a serial device with a wake-up latency — some parts need a dummy clock or a fixed delay after power-up before they'll respond — I'd make sure the configuration controller doesn't start clocking the flash until that window has elapsed, either by delaying the FPGA's config-start or by having the flash's own reset/select line held until it's ready.

For rapid power-cycling, the real risk is that the flash hasn't fully discharged or completed its internal reset before the FPGA tries to read it again, so the first read returns garbage or the device NAKs. I'd address this with a brown-out detector that holds the FPGA in reset until the supply has fallen below a threshold and come back up cleanly, rather than relying on a simple RC delay. I'd also verify the sequence on a scope across repeated fast cycles, and consider whether the configuration controller should retry on a failed read rather than latching a permanent error. The verification approach is to power-cycle the board many times at the shortest interval the product spec allows and confirm the FPGA always reaches its "init done" state.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide whether to add a dedicated supervisor/sequencer IC versus using the FPGA's own power-good inputs?
- What would you check first if the board configures reliably on a slow power cycle but fails on a fast one?

## Q2: How would you approach choosing between a global clock network and a regional/banked clock resource for a logic block that only needs to run at a modest rate, when the device's global clock resources are nearly exhausted?

**Answer:** Global clock networks are a scarce, high-value resource — they're designed for low skew and low jitter across the whole die, and they consume significant routing and power. Regional or banked clock resources are cheaper in both respects but only reach a portion of the fabric. So the decision is really about reach and skew tolerance versus resource pressure. If the logic block is confined to one region and its clock doesn't need to reach distant corners of the device, a regional resource is usually the right call — it frees a global resource for a clock that genuinely needs whole-chip reach, like a system clock or a transceiver reference.

The trade-off I'd weigh: does the clock need to interact with logic in other regions (e.g., feed a CDC into a global domain, or drive I/O in a different bank)? If so, a regional clock may force extra routing or a handoff that adds skew and complexity. I'd also check whether the tool can actually place the logic within the regional clock's reach — sometimes the intent is regional but the placer spreads the logic and the tool promotes it to a global anyway. The practical approach is to constrain the clock to the regional resource, run the placer, and inspect the clock report to confirm it wasn't silently promoted. If global resources are truly exhausted, I'd also look at whether some existing "global" clocks are actually low-fanout and could be demoted, rather than forcing everything new onto regional nets.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- What symptoms in the timing report would tell you a regional clock choice is causing problems?
- How would you handle a clock that needs to reach two regions that don't share a regional clock resource?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the termination and coupling scheme for a multi-gigabit serial link that must traverse a backplane connector, and how would you decide between AC and DC coupling?

**Answer:** The starting point is that a backplane adds loss, impedance discontinuities at the connector, and possibly a stub, so the termination and coupling choices have to be made together with the channel's loss budget. For termination, multi-gigabit serial links are almost always differentially terminated at the receiver, often with on-die termination inside the transceiver, so the board-level question is mainly whether the channel needs additional external termination or bias networks — usually it doesn't, and adding them can hurt. The connector itself is a discontinuity; I'd choose a connector rated for the data rate with controlled impedance and minimal stub, and keep the launch into and out of the connector as clean as possible.

AC versus DC coupling comes down to common-mode compatibility and whether the protocol needs DC information. AC coupling (series capacitors) blocks the DC common-mode difference between the two ends, which is essential when the transmitter and receiver have different common-mode voltages or when the link crosses a boundary between domains — it also protects against ground offset across a backplane. The cost is that AC coupling removes any DC content, so if the protocol relies on a DC-balanced or specific idle level, or if the receiver needs to see a DC bias for its own termination, AC coupling may not work without a bias network. DC coupling is simpler and preserves DC, but requires the two ends to share a compatible common-mode and ground reference. For a backplane link between boards that may have independent grounds or different supply domains, I'd lean AC coupling with the capacitors placed close to the transmitter, and verify the receiver's termination and bias still work with the coupling caps in the path.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you verify the coupling and termination scheme before the backplane is fabricated?
- What would you check if the link works on a short bench cable but fails through the backplane?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the DDR3 memory interface passes its built-in calibration and memory test at power-up, but fails intermittently only after the device has been running for several minutes and the board has warmed up?

**Answer:** A failure that only appears after warm-up points to a temperature-dependent timing or analog margin issue, not a logic bug — the calibration passed, so the interface was functional at the calibration temperature. The first thing I'd do is separate the two possibilities: is the failure in the memory device, the FPGA's I/O and delay chains, or the board's signal integrity? Temperature changes trace impedance, driver strength, and the delay of the FPGA's I/O delay elements, so a link that was calibrated with just enough margin at room temperature can drift out of the sampling window as it heats.

I'd approach it by re-running the calibration and margin analysis at temperature — many controllers expose per-bit read/write margin data, and comparing the eye at cold versus hot tells you whether the margin is shrinking. If it is, the fix is usually to increase margin: adjust the calibration to center the sampling point rather than just pass, add or tune on-die termination, or improve the board's signal integrity (shorter stubs, better reference plane, tighter skew). I'd also check whether the failure correlates with a specific data pattern or access pattern, which would point to crosstalk or a specific bit lane rather than a global timing shift. And I'd confirm the FPGA's temperature isn't exceeding the I/O timing derating assumptions used at design time — sometimes the constraint file assumes a junction temperature that the real thermal solution doesn't meet.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you distinguish a memory-device temperature effect from an FPGA I/O timing effect?
- What changes to the calibration routine would you consider to make it robust across temperature?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** The first thing is to not let it become a personal argument about who's right — it's a technical claim that can be evaluated, and the review is exactly the place to do that. I'd acknowledge the reasoning ("the signal is stable for many cycles") and then walk through why that reasoning doesn't actually guarantee correctness: stability in the source domain says nothing about the setup and hold window at the destination flop, and if the signal changes near the destination clock edge, the flop can go metastable. The fact that it "usually works" is the dangerous part — it's the definition of an intermittent failure that's hard to reproduce and expensive to find later.

I'd then move from the principle to the concrete: ask what the signal's source and destination clocks are, whether they're related, and what the consequence of a missed or metastable capture would be. If it's a single-bit control signal, the standard fix is a two-flop synchronizer (or a proper handshake if it's a pulse or a multi-bit value). I'd frame it as a small, well-understood change rather than a redesign, and offer to work through it with them rather than just handing down a verdict. The goal is that they leave understanding *why* the synchronizer is needed, not just that they were told to add one — that's what prevents the same issue appearing in their next design. If the review is time-boxed, I'd note it as a required action and follow up, but I wouldn't sign off on the design with an unsynchronized CDC in it.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle it if the engineer pushed back and said the synchronizer adds latency the design can't afford?
- What would you do if you found this same pattern in several places in the design rather than just one?
