# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 83

## Q1: How would you approach mitigating via stub effects on a high-speed serial channel that must transition between the top and bottom layers of a thick backplane?
**Answer:** A via stub is the unused portion of a plated through-hole below the layer where the signal enters or exits; at multi-gigabit rates it behaves as an unterminated transmission-line stub that creates a resonance and a sharp insertion-loss notch at a frequency set by the stub's electrical length. The first step is to quantify the problem: estimate the stub length from the stack-up, compute the quarter-wave resonant frequency (roughly c/(4·L·√εr)), and compare it against the signal's Nyquist and harmonic content. If the notch falls inside the band of interest, I'd consider, in order of preference: (1) back-drilling the via to remove the unused barrel below the exit layer, which is the most direct fix but adds process cost and depth tolerance concerns; (2) moving the signal to a layer closer to the bottom of the board so the stub is inherently short; (3) using blind or buried vias if the fabrication budget and layer count allow, which eliminates the stub entirely; or (4) if none of those are possible, compensating with equalization — the transmitter's pre-emphasis and the receiver's continuous-time linear equalizer or decision-feedback equalizer can partially restore the eye, but this trades margin and power for a physical problem that is better solved in the stack-up. I'd verify the choice with a 3D electromagnetic extraction of the via and a channel simulation that includes the connector and package models, not just a 2D trace model.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the back-drill depth tolerance, and what happens if the drill removes too much or too little of the barrel? How would you model the via transition if you don't have a full 3D field solver available?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the clock domain crossing for a multi-bit Gray-coded counter value that increments continuously in a fast domain (e.g., 250 MHz) and must be sampled by a slower domain (e.g., 50 MHz) for logging, where the sampled value must always be a coherent snapshot and never a corrupted mix of bits?
**Answer:** The key property that makes this safe is that a Gray code changes only one bit per increment, so a value sampled while it is transitioning can be off by at most one count — it can never produce a wildly wrong value from multiple bits resolving inconsistently. The standard approach is to register the Gray-coded value in the source domain, pass it through a two-flop synchronizer in the destination domain, and then convert it back to binary in the destination domain for use. The synchronizer handles metastability on the single changing bit; the Gray encoding guarantees that even if that bit is captured mid-transition, the result is either the old or the new count, both of which are valid. I would not synchronize a binary counter directly, because multiple bits can change simultaneously and the destination could latch a nonsensical combination. I'd also add a small handshake or a "data valid" pulse if the consumer needs to know a new sample is available, and I'd constrain the CDC path in the timing tool with the appropriate false-path or max-delay constraint so the tool doesn't try to meet an impossible setup/hold across the two domains. Finally, I'd verify with a CDC lint tool and, if the design warrants it, a formal CDC check to confirm no unsynchronized crossings slipped through.
**Possible follow-ups:** What is the maximum count rate this scheme can support before the Gray-code conversion itself becomes the bottleneck? How would you handle the case where the counter must also be reset, and the reset itself crosses domains?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the timing analysis reports that all constraints are met, but the design fails in hardware only when the device is operated at the maximum specified junction temperature?
**Answer:** A clean timing report at one corner does not mean the design is robust across all corners, so the first thing I'd check is which corner the report was generated for. Static timing analysis must be run at the worst-case slow corner for setup and the worst-case fast corner for hold, and at the maximum junction temperature the slow corner is the one that matters for setup. If the report was generated at a nominal or room-temperature corner, the design may simply have no margin left at the hot corner. I'd re-run timing analysis with the correct operating conditions — maximum junction temperature, worst-case voltage, and the slow process corner — and look for paths that were marginal. Beyond that, I'd consider whether the failure is actually a timing issue at all: at high temperature, leakage and drive strength change, PLL jitter and phase noise typically worsen, and any analog or mixed-signal blocks on the board (references, oscillators, power supplies) drift. I'd check whether the PLL is still locking and whether its output jitter is within the input requirements of the downstream logic. I'd also look for temperature-sensitive board-level effects — a marginal power rail, a decoupling network that loses effectiveness at temperature, or a connector whose impedance shifts. The disciplined approach is to separate the digital timing question from the analog and board-level questions by testing at the hot corner with a known-good clock source and a reduced clock frequency; if the design passes at reduced frequency, it's a timing margin problem, and if it still fails, the issue is elsewhere.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine the actual junction temperature in a running system, and how does it differ from the case or ambient temperature you can measure? What timing margin would you consider acceptable before signing off a design that must operate at the maximum junction temperature?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the decoupling and bulk capacitance network for an FPGA's transceiver power rails, where the rails see both high-frequency switching noise from the SerDes and slower, larger current transients from the fabric, and you need to verify the network is adequate before committing to the layout?
**Answer:** The transceiver rails have a wideband noise profile, so the decoupling network has to be designed across frequency rather than as a single capacitor value. I'd start by characterizing the two noise sources separately: the SerDes switching noise is high-frequency and relatively low amplitude, while the fabric current transients are lower-frequency but much larger in magnitude. The network needs low impedance across the whole band, which means a combination of bulk capacitance for the low-frequency transients, mid-frequency capacitors for the fabric load steps, and small, low-inductance capacitors placed as close to the transceiver power pins as physically possible for the high-frequency SerDes noise. The critical detail is the mounting inductance of the small capacitors — a 100 nF capacitor with a long trace to the pin can be useless at the frequencies where it's supposed to help, so I'd prioritize short, wide connections and via-in-pad or via-adjacent placement. I'd verify the design before layout by building an impedance model of the network — capacitor values, equivalent series resistance and inductance, mounting inductance, and the plane spreading inductance — and plotting the target impedance versus frequency against the expected current spectrum. If the impedance exceeds the target at any frequency where the current has significant content, the network is inadequate. I'd also check the plane capacitance and the anti-pad geometry around the transceiver vias, since those contribute to the high-frequency impedance. After layout, I'd re-extract the parasitics from the actual geometry and, if possible, measure the rail with a high-bandwidth probe to confirm the ripple stays within the transceiver's specification.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you measure the rail impedance in a running system, and what probe and setup would you use? How does the anti-pad geometry around a via affect the high-frequency impedance of the plane pair?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer on your team has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?
**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is separate the technical question from the interpersonal one. Technically, the engineer's reasoning is a classic misconception: a signal that is stable for a long time in the source domain can still be captured during its transition by the destination domain, and the resulting metastable state can propagate and cause intermittent, hard-to-reproduce failures. The fact that it "works" in simulation or on a bench is not evidence that it's safe, because metastability is a probabilistic phenomenon that may not show up until the design is in the field. I'd explain this concretely — walk through what happens when the destination flop samples the signal during its setup/hold window, and why a two-flop synchronizer is the standard mitigation. I'd avoid framing it as "you're wrong" and instead frame it as "here's the failure mode we're protecting against, and here's why the cost of the fix is small compared to the cost of a field failure." If the engineer still pushed back, I'd ask them to demonstrate the design under a CDC lint tool or a formal check, which would flag the unsynchronized crossing objectively. In a design review, my goal is to make the review a place where concerns are raised and resolved with evidence, not authority, so I'd want the engineer to understand the reasoning rather than just accept the change. I'd also follow up to make sure the fix is implemented and that the team's CDC review process catches similar issues earlier next time.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer's manager disagreed with your assessment and felt you were being overly cautious? What process changes would you propose to catch unsynchronized crossings before design review rather than during it?
