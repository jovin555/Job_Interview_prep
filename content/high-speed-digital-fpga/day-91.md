# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 91

## Q1: How would you approach designing the power-up and configuration sequence for an FPGA that loads its bitstream from external flash, when the flash device itself has a non-trivial access latency and the board may be power-cycled rapidly?

**Answer:** The core problem is that configuration is a multi-rail, multi-device handshake, and every participant has its own timing requirement. I'd start by enumerating the dependencies: the FPGA's own power sequencing requirements (core, auxiliary, I/O — often with a specified ramp order and a minimum ramp monotonicity), the flash device's power-up and wake-up time (some serial flash parts need a defined delay after VCC stabilizes before they accept commands, and some enter a low-power state after a previous session), and the FPGA's configuration controller behavior (how long it waits for the flash to respond, whether it retries, and what it does on timeout).

The design approach is to make the sequence explicit and enforced rather than incidental. A power supervisor or sequencer asserts the FPGA's reset/program pin only after all rails are in tolerance and stable for a defined margin, and only after the flash's own power-good and wake-up delay have elapsed. If the flash has a long wake-up, I'd either hold the FPGA in reset through that window or use a small controller (a supervisor with a programmable delay, or a tiny MCU) to gate the program release. The key is that the release of configuration must be a deliberate event with margin, not a race between two RC ramps.

For rapid power-cycling, the concern is that the rails may not have discharged fully before the next power-up, so the supervisor's reset threshold and the FPGA's internal POR may not re-trigger cleanly, and the flash may still be mid-operation from the previous session. I'd add a brown-out detector with hysteresis and a minimum off-time requirement, and verify that the discharge path (bleed resistors or load) brings rails below the reset threshold within the specified off-time. On the bench I'd characterize the actual rail decay with a scope and confirm the supervisor re-arms.

Verification is where this gets real: I'd test across the full supply ramp-rate range (slow ramp, fast ramp, hot-plug), across temperature, and with repeated fast power cycles — not just a single clean power-up. I'd also instrument the sequence (program pin, init/done, flash chip-select activity) so a failure can be attributed to a specific step rather than "it didn't boot."

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to use a dedicated sequencer/supervisor versus relying on the FPGA's internal POR and the flash's own power-on timing? What would you do if the flash's datasheet wake-up time is specified only as a typical value with no maximum?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the transceiver link passes its built-in PRBS checker at the physical layer, but application-level data is occasionally corrupted, and the corruption only appears at full line rate?

**Answer:** The PRBS checker passing tells me the SerDes analog front end, CDR, and equalization are working — the eye is open and the bit error rate at the physical layer is acceptable. So the corruption is almost certainly above the PMA: in the PCS, the fabric interface, the clock domain crossing between the recovered clock and the fabric clock, or the framing/gearbox logic. The fact that it only appears at full line rate is the strongest clue — it points at something rate-dependent, like a FIFO in the rate-matching path, a CDC that only fails when the phase relationship drifts into a bad window, or a backpressure/flow-control path that can't keep up.

My first move is to localize where the corruption is introduced. I'd bring the link up at full rate but insert a known pattern generator and checker at different points: at the PCS output (before the fabric CDC), at the fabric interface, and at the application layer. If the pattern is clean at the PCS but dirty at the fabric, the CDC or rate-matching FIFO is the suspect. If it's clean at the fabric but dirty at the application, it's framing, packetization, or the software/driver path.

The rate-dependence specifically makes me suspicious of the RX rate-matching or elastic FIFO: at full line rate the recovered clock and the local reference have a small frequency offset, and the FIFO slowly fills or empties, eventually hitting an overflow/underflow or a pointer wrap that corrupts a word. I'd check the FIFO status flags over time — if the fill level is drifting monotonically, that's the smoking gun. Similarly, if there's a CDC between the recovered-clock domain and the fabric clock, I'd verify it's a proper handshake or async FIFO and not a naive multi-bit synchronizer that happens to work at low rate because the phase relationship is stable.

I'd also look at whether the corruption is a single bit, a burst, or a whole word — the pattern tells you the mechanism. Single-bit errors scattered randomly suggest a marginal timing path or CDC metastability; burst errors suggest a FIFO overflow or a framing slip. And I'd check whether the errors correlate with traffic patterns (back-to-back packets, idle gaps) which would point at flow control.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a CDC metastability issue from a FIFO overflow as the root cause, given both produce intermittent corruption? What instrumentation would you add to the design to catch the corruption at the moment it happens rather than after the fact?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the clock domain crossing for a multi-bit Gray-coded counter value that increments continuously in a fast domain (e.g., 250 MHz) and must be sampled by a slower domain (e.g., 50 MHz) for logging, where the sampled value must always be a coherent snapshot and never a corrupted mix of bits?

**Answer:** The reason Gray coding is the right tool here is that only one bit changes per increment, so a sampling edge that lands during a transition can only ever capture either the old value or the new value — never a mix. That's the property that makes a multi-bit value safe to pass through a simple two-flop synchronizer per bit, which would be illegal for a binary counter where multiple bits can change simultaneously.

The design is: in the 250 MHz domain, maintain the counter in Gray code (or convert binary to Gray combinationally at the output). Pass each Gray bit through a two-flop synchronizer into the 50 MHz domain. Then convert the synchronized Gray value back to binary in the destination domain for use. The synchronizer per bit is acceptable precisely because of the single-bit-change guarantee — the worst case is that different bits resolve on different destination clock edges, but since only one bit is in transition at a time, the resolved value is always a valid Gray code corresponding to either the pre- or post-increment count.

There are a few subtleties I'd be careful about. First, the counter must genuinely be Gray-coded at the point of crossing — if I convert binary to Gray combinationally and the binary value has a glitch, the Gray output can glitch too, so I'd register the Gray value in the source domain before crossing. Second, the destination must tolerate the value being "stale" by up to a couple of destination clock cycles plus synchronizer latency — for logging that's fine, but I'd document the latency budget. Third, I'd add a constraint or a CDC tool waiver/annotation so the static CDC checker knows this is an intentional Gray-code crossing and doesn't flag it as an unsynchronized multi-bit bus — and equally, so a reviewer doesn't "fix" it by adding a handshake that isn't needed.

If the counter were binary and I couldn't change it, I'd use a handshake (request/acknowledge with a stable data hold) or a dual-clock async FIFO instead — but Gray coding is the cleanest solution when the value is monotonic and the destination only needs a coherent snapshot.

**Possible follow-ups:** What happens if the counter increments by more than one per source clock cycle — does the Gray-code guarantee still hold? How would you handle a counter that must also decrement, or wrap around?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the return-current path for a high-speed digital board where a signal transitions between two reference planes (e.g., from a ground plane to a power plane) on its way through a via?

**Answer:** The fundamental principle is that every signal current has a return current, and at high frequency the return current follows the path of least impedance — which means it flows on the reference plane directly beneath (or above) the trace, not through the ground network at DC. When a signal via transitions from a layer referenced to one plane to a layer referenced to a different plane, the return current has to jump between those planes, and if there's no low-inductance path for it to do so, it will find a long, high-inductance detour — which manifests as radiated emissions, crosstalk, and signal integrity degradation.

The standard fix is to provide a return path at the transition: place a stitching capacitor (or a cluster of them) between the two planes, located as close as possible to the signal via — ideally within a fraction of the signal's rise-time spatial extent. The capacitor provides the high-frequency return path across the plane boundary. The value isn't critical for the high-frequency component (the capacitor's ESL dominates), so I'd choose a small, low-ESL package (e.g., an 0402 or 0201) and place it adjacent to the via, with short, wide connections to both planes. For very high-speed signals I'd consider a via-in-pad or a dedicated ground via adjacent to the signal via if the transition is between ground-referenced layers.

The better design choice, when the stack-up allows it, is to avoid the plane transition altogether: route the high-speed signal on layers referenced to the same plane, or arrange the stack-up so that the signal's reference plane is continuous through the transition. If the signal must change reference planes, I'd try to make the transition between two ground planes (which can be stitched with ground vias, a much lower-inductance path than a capacitor) rather than between a ground and a power plane.

I'd verify this in layout review by tracing the return path for every high-speed signal that changes layers, and in simulation (3D field solver or at least a 2D extraction of the transition) if the signal is fast enough that it matters. On the bench, a near-field probe scan or a simple radiated-emissions pre-scan can reveal a plane-transition problem as a hot spot at the via location.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide the number and placement of stitching capacitors for a bus of several signals transitioning planes together? What's the risk of using a single large capacitor versus several small ones for this purpose?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer on your team has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** The first thing I'd do is make sure I understand their reasoning rather than just correcting them — "stable long enough" often means they've observed it working in simulation or on a bench unit, and I want to acknowledge that observation while explaining why it isn't a guarantee. The teaching point is that a direct connection between asynchronous domains is a metastability risk: the receiving flop can go metastable if the input changes within its setup/hold window, and whether that happens depends on the phase relationship between the two clocks, which drifts with temperature, voltage, and part-to-part variation. "It works on my board" is consistent with a design that fails on one board in a hundred, or fails only at temperature extremes — which for a data acquisition board is exactly the kind of latent defect you don't want escaping.

I'd frame it in terms of the specific signal: is it a single-bit level, a pulse, or a multi-bit bus? Each has a correct synchronization pattern — a two-flop synchronizer for a single-bit level, a pulse synchronizer or toggle handshake for a pulse, a handshake or async FIFO for a multi-bit bus. I'd walk through which one applies here and why the direct connection doesn't meet it. I'd also point at the CDC lint/static analysis tool output if it flags the path, because that turns it from my opinion into a tool-verified finding, and I'd ask them to add the path to the CDC constraint/waiver file only if it's genuinely intentional and safe — which it isn't here.

Then I'd make it a learning opportunity rather than a reprimand: ask them to propose the fix, review it with them, and add a CDC check to the review checklist so the next design catches it earlier. The goal is that the engineer internalizes the principle, not just patches this one signal. If the schedule is tight, the fix is usually small — a couple of flops and a constraint — so there's rarely a good reason to defer it.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer pushed back and said the synchronizer adds latency the control loop can't tolerate? How would you make sure this class of issue gets caught earlier in future reviews rather than relying on a senior reviewer spotting it?
