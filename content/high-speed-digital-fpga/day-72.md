# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 72

## Q1: How would you approach choosing between a global clock network and a regional/banked clock resource for a logic block that only needs to run at a modest rate, when the device's global clock resources are nearly exhausted?
**Answer:** The decision should be driven by fan-out and physical span, not by frequency alone. Global clock networks are a scarce, high-performance resource intended for signals that must reach registers spread across the whole die with low skew and controlled insertion delay. A regional or banked clock buffer, by contrast, can only reach a bounded geographic region of the fabric, but it consumes far less of the global budget and often has lower dynamic power because it drives a smaller tree.

My approach would be: first, enumerate every clock in the design and classify each by how far it must physically reach. Clocks that stay within one clock region — a local processing pipeline, a small peripheral block, a self-contained state machine — are candidates for regional resources. Clocks that feed logic scattered across multiple regions, or that must be phase-aligned with a device-wide reference, need global routing. Second, check whether the tool is already inferring a global buffer for something that doesn't need one; synthesis and place-and-route tools will often promote a high-fan-out signal to a global net automatically, and that promotion can be constrained or overridden. Third, consider whether the two clocks are actually the same clock — sometimes a design ends up with several near-identical clocks that could be merged into one global net with a single buffer, freeing resources without any regional routing at all.

The trade-off to articulate is that regional clocks reduce resource pressure and power but constrain placement: the tool must keep all loads inside the reachable region, which can hurt timing closure or force suboptimal placement elsewhere. So the decision is not purely about clock resources — it interacts with the floorplan. If the logic is genuinely local and the region is not congested, regional is the better choice. If the logic is spread out or the region is tight, the placement cost may outweigh the resource savings, and it's worth finding a different clock to demote instead.

- How would you verify after place-and-route that a regional clock's loads all landed inside its reachable region, and what would you do if the tool reported a violation?
- What are the implications for clock-domain-crossing analysis if two related clocks end up on different network types?

## Q2: How would you approach designing the termination and coupling scheme for a multi-gigabit serial link that must traverse a backplane connector, and how would you decide between AC and DC coupling?
**Answer:** I'd start by separating the problem into three concerns: impedance and reflection control, common-mode management, and the DC bias requirements of the transceivers at each end.

For impedance and reflections, the link is a controlled-impedance transmission line from transmitter to receiver, and every discontinuity — the connector, the via transitions, the package — must be minimized or compensated. The connector is usually the worst offender, so I'd select one with a specified impedance and a launch geometry that matches the trace, and I'd keep the stub length at the connector as short as physically possible. If the connector cannot be made transparent, a well-placed passive equalization network or a deliberate impedance taper can absorb some of the reflection, but the first move is always to reduce the discontinuity rather than compensate for it.

For coupling: AC coupling inserts series capacitors that block DC and low-frequency content, which is useful when the two ends have different common-mode voltages or when the link must be hot-swappable and you want to prevent DC current flow through the connector. The cost is that the capacitor forms a high-pass network with the line impedance, and its value must be chosen so the corner frequency is well below the lowest data content — otherwise you get baseline wander, where long runs of identical bits cause the average level to drift and the receiver's threshold to shift. DC coupling avoids that problem entirely and is simpler, but it requires the transmitter and receiver to share a compatible common-mode range and it provides no protection against ground-potential differences between boards.

So the decision rule I'd use: if the two boards are on the same ground reference and the transceivers' common-mode ranges overlap, DC coupling is simpler and avoids baseline wander. If there's any chance of a ground offset, hot-swap, or incompatible common-mode, AC coupling is the safer choice, but then I'd size the capacitors generously and confirm the receiver's equalizer can handle the resulting low-frequency response. I'd also check whether the transceiver already has internal AC coupling or a built-in bias network, because adding external capacitors on top of that can double the high-pass effect.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you simulate or measure baseline wander on an AC-coupled link, and what would you look for in the eye diagram?
- What changes if the link must also carry a low-speed out-of-band signal or a sideband channel?

## Q3: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the DDR memory interface passes its built-in calibration and memory test at power-up, but fails intermittently only after the device has been running for several minutes and the board has warmed up?
**Answer:** This is a temperature-dependent timing margin problem, and the key insight is that passing calibration at power-up only proves the interface works at the temperature and voltage present at that moment. As the board warms, both the FPGA's internal delay characteristics and the PCB's propagation delays shift, and a link that was calibrated with adequate margin at cold will drift toward the edge of its window.

My first step would be to characterize the failure rather than chase it. I'd want to know: does it fail on reads, writes, or both? Does it correlate with a particular address region or data pattern? Does it happen at a specific elapsed time, or after a specific amount of traffic? That tells me whether I'm looking at a timing-margin issue, a thermal issue in a specific component, or a refresh/retention issue.

Next, I'd look at the calibration itself. Most DDR controllers calibrate read and write leveling, and often per-bit deskew, at initialization. If the calibration is done once at cold and never re-run, the interface has no way to track the drift. Some controllers support periodic recalibration or temperature-compensated delay tracking; if the controller supports it, enabling periodic recalibration is often the direct fix. If it doesn't, I'd examine whether the calibration window itself is narrow — a link that calibrates with a wide margin at cold is much more robust to drift than one that calibrates near the edge.

I'd also check the obvious thermal suspects: is the memory device or the FPGA running hotter than expected? Is there airflow? Is the termination or ODT setting causing excess power dissipation that heats the die? A marginal power delivery network can also cause the rail to sag as current demand rises with temperature, which shifts the I/O timing.

The systematic approach is to instrument the interface — if the controller exposes per-bit error counters or a way to read back the calibration delay values, I'd log those over time and see whether they drift. If the delay values are drifting, that confirms a timing-margin problem and points to recalibration or a wider calibration window. If they're stable but errors appear, I'd look at the power rail and the thermal path instead.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine whether the drift is in the FPGA's I/O delays or in the PCB/memory device delays?
- What would you change in the board design to make the interface more robust to temperature drift?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the reset architecture for a high-speed FPGA design that contains multiple clock domains, a DDR memory controller, and high-speed transceiver links, where an improperly sequenced reset can leave the device in a non-functional state?
**Answer:** The core principle is that reset is not a single signal — it's a set of domain-specific resets with defined ordering and release conditions, and the architecture must make that ordering explicit and enforceable rather than relying on the relative timing of asynchronous events.

I'd start by defining, for each clock domain, what "reset" means and what must be true before that domain can safely come out of reset. The transceiver links, for example, typically need their reference clocks stable and their PLLs locked before the link logic can be released. The DDR controller needs its own initialization sequence — power and clock stable, then controller reset, then calibration — and the rest of the design must not access memory until calibration completes. The fabric logic in each domain needs its own reset synchronized to that domain's clock.

The architecture I'd build has three layers. First, a raw reset source — the external reset pin, a power-good signal, or a supervisory circuit — that is asynchronous to everything. Second, a set of reset synchronizers, one per clock domain, that take the raw reset and produce a domain-local reset that is asserted asynchronously but released synchronously to that domain's clock. This prevents the classic problem of a reset release that violates a register's setup/hold and leaves it metastable. Third, a sequencing or handshake layer that gates the release of downstream resets on upstream readiness signals — for example, the fabric reset is held until the DDR controller asserts "calibration done," and the link logic reset is held until the transceiver asserts "PLL locked."

The subtle part is that reset release must be monotonic and glitch-free. If a domain's reset is released and then re-asserted because an upstream condition dropped, the domain may have already started operating and can end up in an inconsistent state. So the sequencing logic should latch readiness — once a condition is met, it stays met until the next full reset — rather than passing the raw condition through.

I'd also make sure the reset architecture is testable and observable. In a complex design, the most common failure is not a missing reset but a reset that never releases because a readiness signal never asserts, or a reset that releases too early because a readiness signal was sampled before it was valid. Bringing out status bits for each domain's reset state and each readiness condition makes that debuggable.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a domain that has no clock during reset — for example, a transceiver whose reference clock comes from an external source that may not be present at power-up?
- What are the risks of using a single global reset for all domains, and when might that still be acceptable?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a teaching moment first, because the engineer's reasoning reflects a genuine and common misconception — that signal stability in the time domain is sufficient to guarantee correct capture. The issue isn't whether the signal is stable for a long time; it's that the receiving register's setup and hold window is defined relative to its own clock, and if the signal transitions anywhere near that window, the register can go metastable regardless of how long the signal then remains stable. Metastability is a probabilistic event, not a deterministic one, so "it's been stable for ages" doesn't eliminate the risk — it just means the failure is rare and therefore harder to find.

In the review, I'd walk through the specific signal: what generates it, what consumes it, and what the timing relationship is between the two clocks. If the clocks are asynchronous, there is no fixed phase relationship, so the transition will eventually land in the setup/hold window — it's a matter of when, not if. I'd explain that the standard solution for a single-bit control signal is a two-flop synchronizer in the destination domain, which reduces the probability of metastability propagating to an acceptably low level, and that for a multi-bit bus the solution is different again — either a handshake, a FIFO, or a Gray-coded encoding, depending on the data.

I'd also make the point that this is exactly the kind of issue that static analysis tools catch. Most FPGA toolchains have CDC checking that flags unsynchronized crossings, and part of the review process should be running that check and treating its output as a required item, not an optional one. If the team isn't already running CDC analysis as part of sign-off, that's a process gap worth fixing.

The tone matters here. The engineer isn't being careless — they've reasoned from a plausible but incomplete mental model. The goal is to correct the model, not to embarrass them, and to make sure the fix is understood rather than just applied. I'd ask them to propose the synchronization scheme themselves and then review it, which both confirms their understanding and gives them ownership of the fix.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you decide between a two-flop synchronizer, a handshake, and a FIFO for a given crossing?
- What would you do if the CDC tool flagged dozens of crossings across the design — how would you triage them?
