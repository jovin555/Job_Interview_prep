# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 116

## Q1: How would you approach designing the power-up and reset sequencing for an FPGA whose configuration engine, PLLs, and transceiver quads must all come up in a defined order, where an out-of-order release can leave the device configured but non-functional?

**Answer:** The key principle is that reset is not a single event but a dependency graph, and the sequencing logic must encode that graph explicitly rather than relying on RC delays or "it usually works" behavior. I'd start by enumerating every domain in the device — configuration engine, each PLL/MMCM, each transceiver quad, the fabric clock domains, and any external PHYs or memory controllers — and for each one write down its prerequisites: what supply rails must be stable, what reference clock must be present and locked, and what upstream block must have asserted its "ready" status. That enumeration becomes the specification for the sequencer.

In implementation, I'd use a small state machine (or a chain of them, one per subsystem) clocked from a always-available source — typically an internal oscillator or a free-running reference that doesn't depend on any PLL being locked, since a PLL can't be the clock for its own bring-up logic. Each state waits for a qualified condition: power-good comparators on the rails with appropriate thresholds and filtering, PLL lock with a debounce or a lock-loss re-check, transceiver calibration done, and configuration DONE. Only when all prerequisites for a stage are satisfied does the sequencer release that stage's reset. I'd make the release synchronous to the destination domain where possible, and use asynchronous-assert/synchronous-deassert reset synchronizers so that reset removal never violates recovery/removal timing.

Two details matter a lot in practice. First, the transceiver quads typically need their reference clock stable and their power rails within tolerance before calibration begins, and calibration itself takes time — the sequencer must treat "calibration in progress" as a distinct state, not just wait on a done bit that might be sampled too early. Second, I'd build in a timeout and a defined failure state for every wait: if a PLL doesn't lock within a bounded window, the sequencer should flag a fault and hold the downstream blocks in reset rather than hanging forever, because a hung bring-up is much harder to diagnose in the field than a reported fault. I'd also expose the sequencer state and each prerequisite status through a debug/telemetry register so that a board that comes up non-functional can be diagnosed by reading which stage stalled, rather than by probing.

Finally, I'd verify the sequencing in simulation with deliberately adversarial rail ramp profiles — slow ramps, fast ramps, non-monotonic ramps, and a rail that comes up late — because the failure mode of out-of-order release is exactly the case where nominal simulation passes and corner-case hardware fails.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a PLL that locks, then loses lock transiently during a supply glitch — do you re-run the whole sequence or just that stage? And how do you keep the sequencer itself from being disturbed by the same glitch that disturbed the PLL?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the DDR3 interface passes its built-in calibration and memory test at power-up, but fails intermittently only after the device has been running for several minutes and the board has warmed up?

**Answer:** A failure that appears only after warm-up and only after calibration has already passed points strongly at a timing margin that was adequate at the calibration temperature but is being consumed as the board heats — so the first thing I'd do is separate "the calibration data is now wrong" from "the calibration data is fine but the margin has shrunk." I'd read back the per-bit read/write leveling and delay values after warm-up and compare them to the values captured at cold calibration. If they've drifted, the calibration was marginal to begin with and temperature moved the delay chain past the sampling window; if they're unchanged, the issue is more likely a supply or reference drift, or a termination/ODT behavior change with temperature.

On the hardware side, the usual suspects for temperature-dependent DDR3 failure are the reference voltage (VREF) — whether it's a divider off a rail that drifts, or an internal reference — the ODT and drive-strength settings interacting with temperature-dependent output impedance, and the fly-by topology's write-leveling assumptions if the board's trace delays shift with dielectric constant over temperature. I'd measure the rails and VREF at temperature, and I'd check whether the failure correlates with a particular data pattern or a particular byte lane, since a single lane failing points at that lane's leveling or termination while all lanes failing points at a shared resource like VREF or the controller's timing.

In the controller, I'd enable the available margining: sweep the read capture delay and the write leveling offset at temperature and find where the eye closes, rather than just pass/fail. Many controllers expose per-bit delay taps and a built-in margin readout; running that at cold and at hot tells you how much margin you actually had. If the hot margin is thin, the fix is usually to re-center the calibration at the worst-case temperature, tighten the ODT/drive settings, or improve the board's power delivery and reference stability — not to simply re-run calibration at power-up and hope.

I'd also rule out the "it's not really the DRAM" cases: a regulator whose output droops as it heats, a thermal issue in the FPGA's own I/O bank supplies, or a clock reference that drifts. The discipline is to instrument temperature alongside the failure so the correlation is measured, not assumed.

**Possible follow-ups:** If re-centering the calibration at hot temperature fixes it but cold margin becomes thin, how do you decide which corner to optimize for? And how would you distinguish a DRAM-side thermal effect from an FPGA I/O bank thermal effect?

## Q3: How would you approach verifying that a high-speed FPGA design's timing constraints are complete and correct before sign-off, given that missing or incorrect constraints can produce a design that passes timing analysis but fails in hardware?

**Answer:** The core problem is that timing analysis only proves what you told it to prove — an unconstrained or falsely excepted path is silently "passing." So the verification has to attack the constraint set itself, not just the report it produces. I'd structure it in layers.

First, completeness: every clock in the design must be created and every clock domain relationship must be either constrained or explicitly declared asynchronous with a justified false path. I'd cross-check the constraint file against the design's actual clock list — the clocks reported by the tool after synthesis, the PLL/MMCM outputs, the transceiver recovered clocks, and any externally supplied clocks — and confirm each one appears. Any clock the tool found but the constraints don't mention is a red flag. Similarly, I'd review every false path and multicycle path individually and require a written justification tied to the logic's actual behavior, because these are where designs "pass" while being wrong.

Second, correctness of the I/O constraints: input and output delays, clock uncertainty, and the reference clock definitions must match the actual external device timing — the ADC's or memory's datasheet numbers, including board skew. I'd trace each external interface's constraint back to the datasheet and the board's length-matching report, because a plausible-looking but wrong input delay is a classic silent failure.

Third, I'd use the tool's own audits: unconstrained-path reports, clock-domain-crossing checks, and the timing summary's "no path" and "unconstrained" categories. I'd also run the analysis at all specified PVT corners, not just the typical one, and check that the constraint set is identical across corners so a corner isn't quietly passing because a constraint was dropped.

Fourth, I'd verify the constraints against reality where possible: gate-level simulation with back-annotated delays for critical interfaces, and on hardware, a margin measurement (e.g., sweeping a capture delay or using the transceiver's eye monitor) to confirm the analysis's claimed margin actually exists. A design that analysis says has 200 ps of margin but whose hardware eye closes at 50 ps of added delay has a constraint problem somewhere.

The cultural piece is that sign-off should require a constraint review by someone other than the author, with the exceptions list as the focus — because the exceptions are exactly where a well-meaning engineer can make a design "pass" that shouldn't.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you catch a constraint that is present and syntactically valid but numerically wrong — say, an input delay that's too optimistic? And what's your view on using multicycle paths at all in a high-speed design?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the interface between an FPGA and an external ADC that uses a source-synchronous, double-data-rate output bus, where the data and a forwarded clock arrive at the FPGA with an unknown but bounded skew between them?

**Answer:** The defining property of a source-synchronous DDR interface is that the clock travels with the data, so the FPGA doesn't need to know the absolute flight time — only the skew between the forwarded clock and each data bit, bounded by the ADC's output timing and the board's length matching. The design job is to guarantee that the sampling point lands in the middle of the data eye across PVT, given that bounded skew.

I'd start from the ADC datasheet's output timing: the data valid window relative to the forwarded clock edge, including the ADC's own tCO variation and the DDR nature (data changes on both clock edges). Then I'd add the board's skew budget — the difference in trace length between the clock and each data line, converted to time — and the FPGA's input setup/hold requirements. That gives the total skew the interface must tolerate. If the budget is tight, the first lever is board layout: match the clock and data traces to within a small tolerance and keep them on the same layer referencing the same plane, because skew is a physical property you can design away before you spend logic on it.

In the FPGA, the standard approach is to capture the data with the forwarded clock (or its inverse, depending on the ADC's edge relationship) using the device's dedicated input registers or IDDR primitives, and then use the I/O delay elements (IDELAY) to shift the sampling point. The key design decision is whether to use a fixed delay computed from the timing budget or a per-bit dynamic calibration. For a bounded-skew interface, a fixed delay chosen at the center of the calculated window is often sufficient and simpler; per-bit calibration is worth it when the skew is large or the board is long. I'd also make sure the forwarded clock is handled as a clock (through a clock-capable pin and the appropriate buffer) rather than as data, and that the clock domain crossing from the forwarded clock into the fabric clock is done with a proper FIFO or synchronizer, since the forwarded clock is asynchronous to the rest of the design.

Verification: I'd constrain the interface with input delay and clock uncertainty derived from the same budget, run at all corners, and then confirm on hardware by sweeping the IDELAY across its range and finding where the interface fails on each side — the width of the passing region is the real margin, and it should match the analysis.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between capturing on the forwarded clock's rising edge versus using both edges with IDDR? And if the ADC's forwarded clock is a DDR clock at the full data rate, what are the implications for the FPGA's input clocking resources?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer presents a timing closure report showing all constraints met, but you notice the report was generated with a set of timing exceptions (false paths and multicycle paths) that the engineer added to make the design close. The engineer argues that the exceptions are legitimate because "those paths don't need to meet single-cycle timing." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** The engineer may well be right — some paths genuinely don't need single-cycle timing, and multicycle paths are a legitimate tool. But the review can't accept "it's legitimate" as an assertion; the exceptions have to be justified path by path, because that's exactly where a design can be made to "pass" while being wrong. So I'd treat this as a teaching and verification moment rather than a confrontation.

My approach would be to ask the engineer to walk through each exception and explain, for that specific path, why the logic guarantees the relaxed timing: for a multicycle path, what mechanism ensures the data is stable for the extra cycles (a enable, a handshake, a counter); for a false path, why the path can never be sensitized or why the two domains are truly asynchronous and the crossing is handled by a proper synchronizer. If the explanation holds up and the mechanism is visible in the RTL, the exception stays and we document the justification next to it. If the explanation is "it works in simulation" or "it's slow logic," that's not a justification — it's a hope, and I'd ask for the exception to be removed and the path either fixed or the logic restructured so the relaxation is provable.

I'd also make the process concrete: require that every exception in the constraint file carry a comment naming the mechanism and the reviewer who approved it, and that the timing report be regenerated and reviewed with the exceptions visible rather than buried. That way the next reviewer isn't re-litigating from scratch, and the engineer learns that exceptions are a design decision with an owner, not a knob to turn until the report goes green.

The tone matters — the goal is that the engineer internalizes "can I prove this path is safe?" as the standard, not that they feel caught. If they can prove it, I want them to feel that the review validated their reasoning; if they can't, I want the gap to be obvious to them, not just to me.

**Possible follow-ups:** What would you do if the engineer's justification is plausible but you can't fully verify it in the review — do you block sign-off or accept it with a follow-up action? And how would you handle it if the same pattern of unjustified exceptions shows up again on the next review?
