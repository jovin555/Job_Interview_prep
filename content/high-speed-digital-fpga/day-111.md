# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 111

## Q1: How would you approach designing the interface between an FPGA and an external ADC that uses a source-synchronous, single-data-rate (SDR) output bus, where the data and a forwarded clock arrive at the FPGA with an unknown but bounded skew between them?
**Answer:** The core problem is that the forwarded clock and the data bits share a common source but arrive with a bounded, non-zero skew, so the capture point must be placed in the middle of the data valid window rather than assumed to be at the clock edge. I'd start by reading the ADC datasheet carefully to extract the forwarded clock's relationship to the data (edge-aligned vs. center-aligned), the specified skew window across PVT, and the output driver characteristics. From there I'd decide whether to capture directly in the FPGA's I/O registers using the forwarded clock as a clock, or to route the forwarded clock through a clock-capable pin into a PLL/MMCM and phase-shift it to center the sampling point. For an SDR bus, the simplest robust approach is often to use the forwarded clock as a capture clock in the I/O block and then apply a fixed phase offset (via IDELAY or MMCM phase shift) calibrated so the sampling edge lands mid-window. I'd constrain the interface with input delay constraints that reflect the datasheet's min/max skew, run timing analysis across all PVT corners, and verify with an on-chip eye scan or a sweep of the delay tap while checking for bit errors. If the skew window is wide relative to the clock period, I'd also consider oversampling or a small training pattern at startup to find the optimal delay per board.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide between using IDELAY taps versus an MMCM phase shift to center the sampling point? What would you do if the ADC's skew window is so wide that no single fixed delay works across all boards?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the DDR3 memory interface passes initialization and calibration at power-up, but produces intermittent read errors only after the board has been running for several minutes and warmed up?
**Answer:** A failure that appears only after warm-up points to a temperature-dependent timing or analog margin issue rather than a logic bug, so I'd treat it as a margin problem and try to characterize it rather than immediately chase a fix. First I'd confirm the correlation: log the error rate against board temperature and against time-since-power-on, and check whether the errors cluster on specific data patterns, specific addresses, or specific byte lanes — that narrows whether it's a timing margin, a signal integrity issue, or a particular DRAM device. I'd then re-run the memory controller's built-in calibration and read-leveling at the elevated temperature (many controllers support re-calibration or at least reporting of the calibration tap values), and compare the tap settings at cold vs. hot — a large shift suggests the timing margin is being consumed by temperature drift in the DRAM or the FPGA I/O. On the board side I'd look at whether the VREF or termination is drifting with temperature, whether the DRAM's refresh rate is adequate at high temperature (some parts need higher refresh above a threshold), and whether there's any thermal gradient across the memory devices causing skew between byte lanes. I'd also check the power integrity of the DRAM rail under sustained traffic at temperature. The fix usually comes from restoring margin: tightening the timing constraints to the datasheet's temperature-derated values, adjusting ODT/termination, improving decoupling or airflow, or increasing refresh rate if the DRAM requires it.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a DRAM-internal temperature effect from an FPGA I/O timing shift? What board-level changes would you consider if the calibration taps shift significantly with temperature?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the power-up and configuration sequence for an FPGA that loads its bitstream from external flash, when the flash device itself has a non-trivial wake-up latency and the board may be power-cycled rapidly?
**Answer:** The risk is a race between the FPGA's configuration engine starting to read the flash and the flash being ready to respond, especially on a fast power cycle where the flash may not have fully reset or may still be in an internal recovery state. I'd start by mapping the timing requirements from both datasheets: the FPGA's configuration engine's expected behavior after its own power-good and reset release, and the flash's power-on reset threshold, wake-up time, and any required delay before the first command. Then I'd design the sequence so the FPGA does not begin configuration until the flash is guaranteed ready — either by holding the FPGA's configuration start (via its program/reset pin or a power-good supervisor) until a delay that covers the flash's worst-case wake-up, or by having the FPGA's boot logic poll the flash with a status/ID read and retry until it responds. For rapid power cycling I'd pay particular attention to the discharge path on the flash's supply rail so it actually reaches a valid reset threshold before the next power-up, and to any supervisor that could otherwise release the FPGA while the flash rail is still decaying. I'd verify the sequence with a power-cycling test that includes the worst-case short-off-time case, and I'd add a supervisor with a defined reset delay rather than relying on RC timing alone.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a flash device that requires a specific command sequence after power-up before it will accept reads? What would you check first if the board boots reliably on a slow power cycle but fails on a fast one?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a high-speed FPGA design's timing constraints are complete and correct before sign-off, given that missing or incorrect constraints can produce a design that passes timing analysis but fails in hardware?
**Answer:** The fundamental problem is that timing analysis only proves what you told it to prove, so the verification effort has to focus on constraint completeness and correctness, not just on the "all constraints met" report. I'd start from a constraints checklist derived from the design's interfaces: every clock domain, every generated clock, every I/O interface with its external timing budget, every CDC path, and every false/multicycle path with a written justification. I'd cross-check the clock definitions against the actual clocking architecture (PLL/MMCM outputs, forwarded clocks, recovered clocks) to make sure no clock is left as an unconstrained or auto-derived clock that hides real paths. For I/O I'd verify the input/output delay constraints reflect the external device's datasheet min/max, not just typical values. I'd review every timing exception individually and require a justification tied to the logic's actual behavior — an exception that can't be justified in one sentence is a red flag. I'd also run the vendor's CDC and clock-domain checks, review the unconstrained-path and ignored-path reports rather than only the summary, and do a gate-level or post-route simulation of the critical interfaces. Finally I'd have a second engineer review the constraint file against the schematic and interface specs, since constraint errors are easy to overlook when you wrote them yourself.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you catch a path that is incorrectly constrained as a false path but actually needs to meet timing? What reports would you look at beyond the timing summary to find unconstrained paths?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer presents a timing closure report showing all constraints met, but you notice the report was generated with a set of timing exceptions (false paths and multicycle paths) that the engineer added to make the design close. The engineer argues that the exceptions are legitimate because "those paths don't need to meet single-cycle timing." How do you handle this situation?
**Answer:** I'd treat this as a review-quality issue first and a teaching moment second, and I'd avoid framing it as an accusation — the engineer may genuinely believe the exceptions are correct, and the review's job is to verify that belief rather than assume bad intent. In the meeting I'd ask the engineer to walk through each exception one at a time and explain, for that specific path, why the logic guarantees the multi-cycle or false-path behavior — what the source and destination registers are, what controls the enable or handshake, and what would happen if the path did violate single-cycle timing. That conversation usually separates the legitimate exceptions (a genuinely static configuration register, a path through logic that's only valid after a handshake) from the illegitimate ones (a path that's actually exercised every cycle but was excepted to make the report green). For any exception that can't be justified in that walkthrough, I'd ask for it to be removed and the design re-closed, and I'd offer to pair on the fix if the real timing is hard to meet. I'd also use it to reinforce the process: exceptions should be documented with a justification in the constraint file, and the review should look at the exception list as a first-class artifact, not just the pass/fail summary. The goal is that the engineer leaves understanding why an unjustified exception is worse than a failing report, because it hides a real risk until hardware.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure a design review so that timing exceptions get scrutinized rather than glossed over? What would you do if the engineer pushed back and insisted the exceptions were fine?
