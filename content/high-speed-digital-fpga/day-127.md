# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 127

## Q1: How would you approach designing the power-up and configuration sequence for an FPGA whose configuration engine, PLLs, and transceiver quads must all come up in a defined order, where an out-of-order release can leave the device configured but non-functional?

**Answer:** The key principle is that the FPGA's internal bring-up has a dependency chain, and the external circuitry must respect it rather than racing it. I'd start by extracting the vendor's sequencing requirements from the datasheet and configuration user guide: which rails must be stable before the configuration engine is released, what the minimum reset pulse width is, how long the device takes to load its bitstream, and when the PLLs and transceiver quads are allowed to be released relative to configuration completion. Then I'd design a supervisor chain — typically a small sequencer or a set of cascaded supervisors — that enforces those dependencies explicitly rather than relying on RC delays or hope.

The critical insight is that "configured" and "functional" are different states. The configuration engine can complete and assert DONE while the PLLs are still unlocked and the transceiver quads are still calibrating. So the release of downstream logic must be gated on the *last* of those events, not the first. I'd bring up the PLLs and wait for their lock signals, bring up the transceiver quads and wait for their calibration-done indications, and only then release the user logic out of reset. Each of those waits needs a timeout and a defined failure state, because a PLL that never locks or a quad that never calibrates should produce a diagnosable fault, not a silent hang.

I'd also think about what happens on a warm restart or a reconfiguration. The sequencing has to be idempotent — a re-trigger must not leave a PLL half-reset or a quad in an indeterminate state. And I'd verify the whole thing not just in simulation but with a power-supply sequencer that can deliberately violate the order, to confirm the design fails safe rather than failing mysteriously.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you detect and recover from a PLL that fails to lock within its timeout window? What would you do differently if the board could be power-cycled faster than the FPGA's own internal power-on-reset delay?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the timing analysis reports all constraints met, but the design fails in hardware only at the maximum specified junction temperature?

**Answer:** A clean timing report at the nominal corner that fails only at max junction temperature is almost always a sign that the analysis corner being reported isn't the corner the silicon is actually running at. My first move is to confirm which corner the report was generated for — slow-slow process, high temperature, low voltage is the worst-case setup corner, and if the report was run at nominal or at a fast corner, it tells you nothing about hot operation. I'd re-run static timing analysis explicitly at the worst-case setup corner and see whether the margin is genuinely positive or whether it was marginal all along.

If the corner analysis is clean but hardware still fails hot, I'd suspect the things STA doesn't model well: on-chip variation and clock uncertainty that were under-constrained, a clock that's being generated or divided in a way the tool didn't fully capture, or a path that crosses into a hard block (a transceiver, a memory controller, a DSP slice) whose internal timing isn't reflected in the fabric-level report. Temperature also shifts the behavior of the I/O and the board-level timing — a source-synchronous interface that was captured with barely adequate margin at room temperature can fail hot because the forwarded clock and data paths drift differently.

Practically, I'd instrument the design to find out *where* it fails rather than guessing. Bring out a heartbeat or a status register over a slow, robust interface, and use an on-chip logic analyzer to capture the failing transaction at temperature. If the failure correlates with a specific interface, I'd add margin there — adjust the capture clock phase, add a pipeline stage, or re-time the path — and then re-verify at the hot corner. The lesson I'd carry forward is that "timing met" is only meaningful when you state the corner, and sign-off should always include the worst-case corner explicitly.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish a fabric timing failure from a board-level or I/O timing failure at temperature? What role does on-chip variation play, and how do you account for it in your constraints?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the decoupling and bulk capacitance network for an FPGA's transceiver power rails, where the rails see both high-frequency switching noise from the SerDes and slower, larger current transients from the fabric, and you need to verify the network is adequate before committing to the layout?

**Answer:** The transceiver rails are a two-timescale problem, and the network has to be designed as an impedance profile across frequency rather than as a pile of capacitors. At the high end — hundreds of MHz into the GHz — the SerDes switching current is fast and the loop inductance between the die and the nearest capacitor dominates, so the small, low-ESL capacitors need to be as close to the package pins as physically possible, often on the back side directly under the ball field. At the low end — tens of kHz to a few MHz — the bulk capacitance and the regulator's control loop handle the slower, larger transients from the fabric, and that's where you size for the total charge and the allowable voltage deviation.

The middle of the spectrum is where designs usually go wrong: there's a gap between where the bulk caps stop being effective and where the small caps start, and the rail impedance peaks there. I'd build a target impedance curve from the rail's ripple specification and expected transient current, then choose capacitor values and placements to keep the impedance below that target across the whole band, accounting for the anti-resonances between different capacitor values. Spreading values too widely creates sharp anti-resonance peaks; using a few well-chosen values with low ESR/ESL parts and damping is usually better than a shotgun spread.

Before committing to layout, I'd verify with a PDN simulation that includes the package model and the plane geometry, not just an ideal capacitor bank. The plane pair and via inductance are part of the network, and a simulation that ignores them will look fine while the real board rings. I'd also plan a measurement — a port on the rail with a network analyzer or a fast current probe — so the assumption can be checked on the first prototype rather than discovered in EMC testing.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you damp an anti-resonance peak between two capacitor values? What changes about the network if the transceiver rail is shared with the fabric core versus dedicated?

## Q4: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the transceiver link passes its built-in PRBS checker at the physical layer, but application-level data is occasionally corrupted, and the corruption only appears at full line rate?

**Answer:** A PRBS checker passing tells you the serialiser, the channel, and the CDR are working — the bits are arriving. If application data corrupts only at full rate, the problem is almost certainly above the physical layer, and the rate dependence is the clue: it points at something that has no margin left once the link is saturated. I'd work up the stack systematically.

First, the framing and alignment layer: is the comma or alignment character being detected reliably, and is the word alignment stable? At full rate, a marginal alignment can slip occasionally and produce a burst of corrupted words that looks like data corruption but is really a re-alignment event. Second, the elastic buffer or FIFO between the recovered clock domain and the local clock domain — at full rate the buffer is being consumed and filled at nearly the same rate, so a small clock frequency offset (ppm-level) will slowly walk the buffer pointer, and if the buffer is too shallow or the clock correction scheme is inadequate, it will occasionally overflow or underflow. That produces exactly the "occasional corruption only at full rate" signature. Third, the user logic behind the transceiver — a width conversion, a gearbox, or a CDC that has a marginal path that only manifests when the data is back-to-back.

I'd instrument at each layer: count CRC or checksum errors per frame, log the elastic buffer occupancy over time, and check whether the corruption is a single bit, a burst, or a whole word. A single bit suggests the physical layer after all (or an encoding issue); a burst or a whole word suggests alignment or buffer. I'd also run the link with a known pattern at full rate and compare received versus transmitted words to characterise the error. The fix depends on the layer, but the discipline is the same: don't accept "PRBS passes" as proof the link is good — it only proves the analogue channel.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish an elastic buffer overflow from a word-alignment slip from the error signature alone? What clock-correction scheme would you use if the two ends are on independent oscillators?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer has implemented a critical control signal that is generated in one clock domain and consumed in another, and they've connected it directly without any synchronization, arguing that "the signal is stable long enough that it will always be captured correctly." How do you handle this situation?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a teaching moment first and a design correction second, because the engineer's reasoning is a very common and very understandable misconception — and if I just override them, they'll make the same mistake next time. I'd start by acknowledging the intuition: yes, the signal is slow relative to the destination clock, so it *usually* works. Then I'd walk through why "usually" isn't good enough — metastability is a probabilistic failure, so a design that works in the lab can fail in the field at a rate that depends on temperature, voltage, and data patterns, and it will fail at the worst possible moment. I'd explain that the fix is cheap: a two-flop synchronizer for a single-bit level signal, or a proper handshake or asynchronous FIFO if the signal is really a multi-bit word in disguise.

I'd also make the review process do some of the work. If the team doesn't already have one, I'd push for a CDC lint check or a formal CDC verification step in the flow, so this class of bug is caught by tooling rather than by whoever happens to be reviewing. That turns a one-off correction into a systemic improvement. And I'd follow up with the engineer after the fix to make sure they understand *why* the synchronizer works, not just that they added one — the goal is that they become the person catching this in the next review.

The tone matters: I want the engineer to feel that raising a design for review and being corrected is a normal, safe part of the job, not a failure. If the review becomes adversarial, people stop bringing marginal designs forward and the problems surface later and more expensively.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the engineer pushed back and said the synchronizer adds latency the design can't afford? What would you do if you found the same pattern in several other places in the design after this review?
