# high-speed-digital-fpga — Day 102

## Q1: How would you approach designing the configuration and boot path for an FPGA that loads its bitstream from external flash, where the flash device has a non-trivial wake-up latency and the board may be power-cycled rapidly?
**Answer:** The core problem is that the FPGA's configuration controller starts polling the flash as soon as its own configuration supply is valid, but the flash may still be in its internal power-up sequence and will not respond to the first read commands. If the FPGA gives up after a fixed number of retries, a fast power cycle can leave the board unconfigured. I would approach this in layers. First, at the power sequencing level, ensure the flash's supply and the FPGA's configuration bank come up in an order and with a ramp that respects both devices' datasheets, and add a supervisor or sequencer so the FPGA does not release its configuration reset until the flash supply is stable plus its specified wake-up time. Second, at the FPGA level, configure the longest configuration-start delay and the maximum number of initialization retries the device supports, so the controller tolerates a slow flash. Third, at the board level, add a pull-up or default state on the flash's chip-select and hold lines so the flash is not accidentally selected or held in a low-power state during ramp. Fourth, consider a small CPLD or supervisor that holds the FPGA in reset until it has verified the flash is responsive — this decouples the two devices' timing. For rapid power cycling specifically, I would check the flash's minimum power-down-to-power-up time and the discharge path on its supply; if the bulk capacitance holds the rail up during a fast cycle, the flash may not have fully reset, so a bleed resistor or an active discharge may be needed. Finally, I would verify all of this on the bench with a programmable supply that can produce fast cycles at the extremes of temperature, and instrument the configuration pins to confirm the sequence rather than assuming it.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether to add an external supervisor versus relying on the FPGA's internal configuration controller? What would you check first if the board configures reliably on a slow bench supply but fails on a fast-cycling production tester?

## Q2: How would you approach debugging an FPGA design where the transceiver link passes its built-in PRBS checker at the physical layer, but application-level data is occasionally corrupted, and the corruption only appears at full line rate?
**Answer:** A passing PRBS check tells you the serialiser/deserialiser and the analog channel are healthy — it exercises the physical layer with a known pattern and the receiver's own error detector. Corruption that appears only at full line rate and only at the application layer points to something above the PMA/PCS: the gap between "bits arrive correctly" and "the words I hand to my logic are correct." I would work through it systematically. First, confirm the PRBS result is genuinely clean at the same line rate and over a long enough dwell — a PRBS check that passes at a reduced rate or for a short window is not the same evidence. Second, look at the datapath between the transceiver's parallel output and the application logic: the gearbox and width conversion, the elastic buffer between the recovered clock and the fabric clock, and any CDC between the transceiver's output clock and the user clock. At full line rate the elastic buffer is closest to its limits, so an under- or over-flow, or a CDC that is marginal, will show up only then. Third, check the framing and alignment logic — comma/alignment-word detection, channel bonding, and any assumption about idle patterns. If the link uses 8b/10b or 64b/66b, a mis-framed word boundary can pass PRBS (which may be injected after the gearbox) but corrupt application payload. Fourth, look at flow control and backpressure: if the application occasionally cannot accept a word and the design drops or overwrites it, that is a logic bug, not a link bug, and it will correlate with full-rate traffic. I would instrument with a pattern generator and checker at the application layer — a known incrementing sequence through the full datapath — and use the transceiver's error counters and the elastic buffer status flags to localise where the first error appears. Then I would reproduce at temperature and voltage extremes, because marginal CDC and buffer timing often only fail at the corners.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you distinguish an elastic buffer overflow from a CDC metastability issue using only the transceiver's status registers? What would you change in the design if the corruption turned out to be a word-alignment issue that only appears after a long idle period?

## Q3: How would you approach designing the decoupling network for an FPGA's core voltage rail, where the rail supplies a large, fast-changing current to the fabric and must stay within a tight tolerance across a broad frequency range?
**Answer:** The core rail is the hardest decoupling problem on the board because the load is a broad-spectrum current step — the fabric can change its switching activity in a single clock cycle, so the current has energy from DC up to the clock frequency and its harmonics. I would design the network as a cascade of impedance bands, each handled by a different capacitor type and placement, and verify the composite impedance against the target. At the highest frequencies, the on-die and package capacitance plus the smallest case-size MLCCs placed directly under the FPGA handle the first few nanoseconds; their loop inductance, not their capacitance, is the limit, so placement and via count matter more than the nominal value. In the mid-band, a spread of larger MLCCs in several case sizes avoids anti-resonances between parallel capacitors of the same value. At the low-frequency end, bulk tantalum or polymer capacitors and the regulator's own loop bandwidth handle the slower, larger transients. The design target is a flat impedance curve below the allowable ripple divided by the transient current, across the whole band, with no peaks where a capacitor's self-resonance meets another's. I would model the PDN with the vendor's or a commercial tool, including the package and die model, and check the impedance peaks; then verify on the bench with a VNA on a bare board and with a dynamic load or the FPGA itself running a worst-case activity pattern while measuring the rail with a low-inductance probe. The common failure modes are too few vias to the plane, capacitors placed on the wrong side of the board with long traces, and a regulator whose loop is too slow to catch the mid-band.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide how many vias per capacitor are enough, and what would you measure to confirm it? What would you do if the measured rail showed a resonance peak right at the FPGA's clock frequency?

## Q4: How would you approach designing the return-current path for a high-speed digital board where a signal transitions between two reference planes (e.g., from a ground plane to a power plane) on its way through a via?
**Answer:** The return current of a high-speed signal follows the path of least impedance, which at high frequency is directly beneath the trace on the adjacent reference plane. When the signal changes reference planes — say from a layer referenced to ground to a layer referenced to a power plane — the return current must also change planes, and if there is no low-inductance path between those two planes near the via, the return current is forced to detour, creating a loop that radiates and a discontinuity that reflects. The standard fix is to provide a return path: place a stitching capacitor between the two planes as close as possible to the signal via, so the return current can hop across through the capacitor's low impedance at the frequencies of interest. The capacitor's placement and its own via inductance dominate its effectiveness, so it must be within a small fraction of a wavelength of the signal via, ideally a few millimetres. Alternatively, if the two planes are the same net (e.g., both ground but split), stitch them with vias directly. If the planes are ground and a power plane, a stitching capacitor is the usual answer, and its value is chosen so its self-resonance is above the signal's highest significant frequency. I would also check the stack-up to see whether the transition can be avoided entirely by routing the signal on layers referenced to the same plane, which is the cleanest solution. In review, I would flag any via that changes reference planes without an adjacent stitching capacitor or via, and I would verify the fix with a 3D field solver or, at minimum, a TDR measurement on the first article.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you choose the stitching capacitor value and package for a 10 Gbps signal, and what would you measure to confirm the return path is adequate? What is the risk of using a stitching capacitor between ground and a noisy power plane, and how would you mitigate it?

## Q5: Behavioral question — You're leading a design review for a high-speed FPGA-based data acquisition board. A junior engineer presents a timing closure report showing all constraints met, but you notice the report was generated with a set of timing exceptions (false paths and multicycle paths) that the engineer added to make the design close. The engineer argues that the exceptions are legitimate because "those paths don't need to meet single-cycle timing." How do you handle this situation?
**Answer:** The report is only as trustworthy as the exceptions behind it, so my first move is to treat the exceptions as the thing under review, not the timing numbers. I would not accept or reject the claim in the meeting — I would ask the engineer to walk through each exception and justify it from the design's functional behaviour: why is this path genuinely false, or why does this path have N cycles of settling time, and what in the RTL or the interface protocol guarantees that? A legitimate multicycle path has a structural reason — a register that is only enabled every N cycles, or a data path whose result is captured by a slower handshake — and that reason should be visible in the code and in the specification. A false path should be a path that can never be sensitised, not a path that is inconvenient. I would ask for the exceptions to be documented with that justification and reviewed by a second engineer, and I would ask whether the design was verified with the exceptions in place — because a false path that is actually sensitised will pass timing and fail in hardware. If the justification is weak, the right outcome is to remove the exception and fix the design, even if it costs a pipeline stage or a re-architecture, because an unjustified exception is a latent field failure. I would frame this as protecting the engineer, not attacking them: the goal is a report we can all sign our names to. I would also use it as a coaching moment about how exceptions propagate into sign-off and why the review exists. If the engineer is confident the exceptions are correct, I would ask them to prove it with a targeted simulation or a formal check that exercises the path, and I would follow up in the next review rather than leaving it open.
**Possible follow-ups:** How would you structure a review process so that timing exceptions are caught and justified before sign-off rather than discovered in the report? What would you do if the engineer's justification turned out to be correct but the exception was still masking a real path that the simulation did not cover?
