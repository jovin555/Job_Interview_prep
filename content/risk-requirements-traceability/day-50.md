# risk-requirements-traceability — Day 50

## Q1: How would you approach establishing traceability for a risk control measure that is implemented as a hardware watchdog timer monitoring a firmware heartbeat, when the hardware and firmware teams each document their portions in separate specifications with no cross-references?

**Answer:** The core problem here is that the risk control is a *system-level* function — the watchdog timer alone doesn't mitigate the hazard, and the firmware heartbeat alone doesn't either. The mitigation exists only in their interaction. So the first step would be to define the control at the system level in the risk management file, with a clear description of the complete mechanism: the firmware must periodically toggle a heartbeat signal, and the hardware watchdog must reset the system if that heartbeat stops within a specified timeout window.

From there, I'd create a system-level requirement that captures the integrated behavior — something like "the system shall reset within X milliseconds of the firmware ceasing to update the heartbeat, and the reset shall return the system to a safe state." This system requirement becomes the anchor point. Then I'd work with each team to ensure their subsystem requirements explicitly reference this system requirement and describe their portion of the mechanism. The firmware requirement would specify the heartbeat update rate and the conditions under which the heartbeat is intentionally stopped (e.g., during a detected fault). The hardware requirement would specify the watchdog timeout period, the reset behavior, and the tolerance on the timeout.

The key traceability links would be: hazard → risk control measure (in the risk management file) → system-level requirement → subsystem requirements (hardware and firmware) → design elements → verification activities. The verification is particularly important here — I'd want a system-level test that actually stops the heartbeat and confirms the reset occurs, because neither the hardware test (watchdog timeout with no heartbeat source) nor the firmware test (heartbeat toggles correctly) alone proves the integrated control works. I'd also ensure that the hardware and firmware requirements documents cross-reference each other or, better yet, both reference the same system-level requirement number so that anyone reviewing either document can trace to the complete control.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle the situation where the hardware watchdog timeout specification and the firmware heartbeat rate specification are inconsistent — for example, the firmware updates the heartbeat faster than the watchdog timeout, but there's no analysis showing the timing margin is adequate? What verification evidence would you consider sufficient for the integrated behavior?

## Q2: How would you approach determining whether a requirement in the SRS is truly a "safety requirement" derived from risk management, versus a "performance requirement" that exists for functional reasons, and how would you decide which traceability links are necessary for each type?

**Answer:** The distinction matters because safety requirements demand a different level of rigor in traceability and verification. A safety requirement exists to mitigate a specific hazard identified in the risk analysis — it has a direct line back to a risk control measure. A performance requirement exists to meet functional or business needs — it may still need verification, but it doesn't carry the same regulatory weight.

I'd start by asking a simple question for each requirement: "If this requirement were not met, what would happen?" If the answer involves patient or user harm, it's likely a safety requirement. If the answer involves degraded performance, poor usability, or failure to meet a specification target, it's a performance requirement. But I'd also look at the origin — safety requirements typically trace back to a specific hazard or risk control measure in the risk management file, while performance requirements trace back to user needs, marketing requirements, or system-level performance targets.

For safety requirements, I'd expect full bidirectional traceability: hazard → risk control → safety requirement → design implementation → verification test. The verification test must demonstrate that the control actually mitigates the hazard under the fault conditions identified in the risk analysis. For performance requirements, I'd still want traceability to design and verification, but the depth of analysis and the strictness of the verification conditions might be less demanding.

There's a gray zone, though — some requirements serve both purposes. For example, a motor speed limit might exist both to ensure effective therapy (performance) and to prevent excessive force on the patient (safety). In that case, I'd treat it as a safety requirement for traceability purposes, because the safety aspect imposes the stricter verification demands. I'd document the dual nature explicitly in the requirements and risk management files so the intent is clear to reviewers.

One practical approach is to add a field in the requirements database or traceability matrix that classifies each requirement as safety-derived, performance-derived, or both. This makes the distinction visible and auditable, and it helps verification teams prioritize their test design effort appropriately.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a requirement was originally written as a performance requirement, but a later risk analysis reveals that it also mitigates a hazard? What about the reverse — a safety requirement that later turns out to have no hazard associated with it?

## Q3: How would you approach creating a traceability scheme that captures the evolution of risk control measures and their associated requirements across multiple design iterations, given that both the risk analysis and the requirements specification are living documents?

**Answer:** The fundamental challenge is that traceability isn't a snapshot — it's a record of how the design and its safety rationale evolved. If the traceability scheme only shows the current state, you lose the ability to answer questions like "why was this control changed?" or "was the hazard re-evaluated when the requirement changed?"

I'd approach this by treating traceability as a versioned relationship, not just a static link. Each requirement, risk control measure, and verification activity should have a version number and an effective date range. When a requirement changes, the traceability link doesn't just update — it creates a new version of the link, with the old version remaining visible in the history. This lets you reconstruct the state of the traceability at any point in the project's history, which is valuable for design reviews, regulatory audits, and post-market investigations.

Practically, this means the traceability matrix needs to be more than a spreadsheet with columns for "requirement ID" and "risk control ID." I'd want a database or requirements management tool that supports versioning and maintains a history of link changes. Each link should record not just what it connects, but when it was created, when it was modified, and why — ideally with a reference to the change request or design review that drove the modification.

I'd also establish a change impact assessment process. When a requirement changes, the traceability scheme should force the question: "Does this change affect any risk control measure? Does it affect the hazard analysis? Does it invalidate any verification activity?" This is where bidirectional traceability becomes essential — you need to be able to walk from the changed requirement up to the hazard and down to the verification test to assess the full impact.

For the risk analysis side, I'd expect the risk management file to be updated whenever a risk control measure changes, with the rationale documented. The traceability scheme should connect these updates — the risk control measure version should align with the requirement version that implements it. If the risk analysis changes the control (e.g., tightening a threshold), the corresponding requirement and verification test should be updated in lockstep, and the traceability should show that alignment.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the risk analysis and the requirements specification have drifted out of sync — for example, the risk analysis references a control that was removed from the SRS two revisions ago? What triggers would you put in place to catch this drift early?

## Q4: How would you approach verifying that a risk control measure implemented as a firmware-based state machine — for example, preventing transition from "standby" to "active" unless all sensor self-tests pass — is correctly traced through to the system-level hazard it mitigates, and that the verification test adequately covers the failure scenario?

**Answer:** The first step is to make the safety intent explicit. A state machine guard like this is often documented purely in functional terms — "the system shall not transition to active mode unless self-tests pass" — without stating why. The traceability needs to connect that functional behavior to the hazard it mitigates, which might be something like "device operates with a failed sensor, leading to incorrect therapy delivery." Without that explicit link, reviewers can't assess whether the verification is adequate.

Once the hazard link is established, I'd examine the verification test design. The key question is whether the test actually exercises the failure scenario the control is meant to mitigate. A test that confirms the system transitions to active mode when all self-tests pass is necessary but not sufficient — it doesn't prove the guard works. The critical test is the negative case: one or more self-tests fail, and the system must remain in standby (or transition to a safe state). I'd want to see tests that cover each sensor failure individually, and ideally a test where multiple sensors fail simultaneously, to ensure the guard logic handles all paths.

I'd also consider the state machine's edge cases. What happens if a self-test fails *during* the transition — mid-transition, so to speak? What happens if a self-test passes but the sensor fails immediately after the transition? The risk analysis should define the scope of what the control is meant to catch, and the verification should align with that scope. If the risk analysis says the control prevents operation with a sensor that failed *before* startup, then testing the pre-transition failure is sufficient. But if the hazard includes sensors that fail during operation, the state machine guard alone isn't the complete control — there needs to be a runtime monitoring mechanism, and that needs its own traceability.

For the traceability itself, I'd want to see the path: hazard → risk control measure (in the risk management file) → firmware requirement (the state machine guard) → design element (the state machine implementation) → verification test (both positive and negative cases). The verification test should be reviewed against the risk analysis to confirm it actually stresses the failure condition — not just that it exercises the state machine under nominal conditions.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the self-test routine itself can fail in a way that's undetectable — for example, the self-test passes because the sensor is reading a valid but incorrect value? How would you determine whether the state machine guard needs to be tested on the actual production hardware or whether testing on a development board with the same microcontroller is sufficient?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where the systems engineer has created a comprehensive traceability matrix linking every requirement to a risk control measure and a verification test. However, during a design review, the test lead points out that several verification tests are testing the wrong thing — for example, a test labeled "verifies overcurrent protection" is actually testing nominal current draw. The systems engineer insists the traceability matrix is correct because the requirement numbers match. How would you resolve this disagreement?

**Answer:** The systems engineer is technically correct that the traceability matrix is internally consistent — the requirement numbers do match. But that's precisely the problem: the matrix has become a paper exercise where the links are correct on paper but don't reflect reality. The traceability is only valuable if the verification test actually exercises the requirement it's linked to. A test that measures nominal current draw cannot verify overcurrent protection, regardless of what the matrix says.

I'd start by acknowledging the systems engineer's point — the matrix is consistent, and that's a necessary condition, but it's not sufficient. The purpose of traceability isn't just to have links; it's to ensure that every requirement is actually verified by a test that can detect failure of that requirement. I'd then propose a concrete review of the specific tests in question, bringing together the test lead, the systems engineer, and the design engineer who understands the overcurrent protection circuit. The goal would be to examine each disputed test and answer two questions: "What is this test actually exercising?" and "If the overcurrent protection were broken, would this test fail?"

In most cases, this kind of review resolves the issue quickly — when you look at the test procedure and the test setup, it becomes obvious that the test doesn't stress the protection circuit. The test might be measuring the current draw under normal load, which tells you nothing about whether the protection trips at the threshold. Once that's clear, the fix is to either modify the test to actually stress the protection (e.g., by applying a fault condition or loading the output beyond the threshold) or to acknowledge that the test verifies a different requirement and create a separate test for the overcurrent protection.

If the systems engineer still resists, I'd escalate the discussion to focus on the regulatory risk. In a medical device context, a traceability matrix that links a verification test to a requirement without the test actually exercising that requirement is a compliance gap. If an auditor or a notified body reviewed the matrix and the test procedures, they would likely identify the discrepancy, and the consequences could range from audit findings to delays in regulatory approval. That's a project-level risk that needs to be addressed regardless of who's right in the technical disagreement.

I'd also use this as an opportunity to improve the process. The root cause is likely that the traceability matrix was built from requirement numbers without verifying that the test content matched the requirement intent. I'd suggest adding a peer review step where the test lead and the design engineer review each verification test against its linked requirement before the test is executed — not after. This catches mismatches early, when they're cheap to fix, rather than during a design review when the test has already been run and the results are in the verification report.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the test lead and the systems engineer agree that the test is wrong, but fixing it would require significant rework and delay the project schedule? What criteria would you use to decide whether to accept the risk of proceeding with the inadequate test versus delaying the project to fix it?
