# risk-requirements-traceability — Day 26

## Q1: How would you approach establishing traceability for a risk control measure that is implemented as a hardware analog circuit with a trim potentiometer that must be calibrated during manufacturing — where the calibration setting itself is part of the risk control, and the verification test must confirm both the circuit function and the calibration tolerance?

**Answer:** This is a case where the risk control has two distinct aspects: the circuit topology (the analog comparator, its reference, and the hysteresis network) and the manufacturing process that sets the trim value. I'd treat these as separate but linked traceability items.

First, I'd document the risk control in the risk management file with a clear description of the hazard it mitigates and the failure condition it addresses. Then I'd split the traceability into two paths. The design requirement would specify the functional behavior — for example, the comparator must trip within a specified voltage range and reset within a specified hysteresis band. This traces to the schematic and layout design elements. The calibration requirement would specify the trim tolerance and the method for setting it — this traces to the manufacturing procedure and the calibration equipment.

For verification, I'd want three levels. The design verification would test the circuit over the full range of trim settings to confirm the circuit can be adjusted to meet the specification — this proves the design is capable. The production verification would test that the calibration process, as written, actually produces units that meet the tolerance — this proves the process works. And the incoming inspection or final test would confirm each unit's calibration setting is within the documented range — this proves the individual unit is correct.

The key insight is that the trim potentiometer creates a dependency between the design and the manufacturing process. If the trim range is too narrow, or the calibration procedure is ambiguous, the risk control fails even though the circuit design is sound. So the traceability matrix should include a link from the risk control to the calibration procedure, not just to the schematic. I'd also want to verify that the calibration procedure includes a step to record the final trim value, because if the trim drifts or is set incorrectly, there's no way to trace back to the root cause without that record.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the calibration tolerance is tighter than the measurement accuracy of the test equipment? What if the trim potentiometer is replaced with a digital potentiometer controlled by firmware — how would that change the traceability approach?

## Q2: How would you approach creating a traceability scheme that connects risk control measures to requirements when the system has a safety mechanism that can be bypassed or overridden by a clinician — for example, a "disable alarm" button on a patient monitoring device — and the override itself introduces a new hazard?

**Answer:** This is a classic case where the risk control creates a secondary risk, and the traceability scheme needs to capture that relationship explicitly. The override is itself a risk control measure — it mitigates the hazard of alarm fatigue or nuisance alarms that could cause a clinician to ignore or disable the alarm entirely. But it introduces the hazard of a clinically significant event going undetected because the alarm was silenced.

I'd structure the traceability in three layers. First, the original hazard (e.g., patient condition deteriorating undetected) traces to the primary risk control (the alarm). Second, the alarm's limitation (nuisance alarms causing desensitization) traces to the override control. Third, the override's new hazard (alarm silenced during a critical event) traces to a mitigation — typically a combination of a visual indicator that the alarm is disabled, a timeout that automatically re-enables the alarm, and possibly a log entry that records when and how long the alarm was disabled.

For the requirements, I'd want the override behavior specified with clear acceptance criteria: how the clinician activates it, what feedback they receive, how long it lasts, and what happens when it times out. The traceability matrix would show the override requirement tracing to both the hazard it mitigates (nuisance alarms) and the hazard it creates (missed critical events). This bidirectional trace is essential because it forces the team to acknowledge that the override is not a free improvement — it has a cost in safety that must be managed.

The verification approach would need to test both directions: that the override works as intended (alarm is silenced, indicator is shown, timeout occurs) and that the secondary mitigation works (the alarm re-enables after timeout, the log records the event, the visual indicator is unmistakable). I'd also want a usability assessment or human factors evaluation, because the override's safety depends on the clinician understanding what they're doing — a purely technical test can't capture that.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you determine whether the override should be a one-time action or a timed disable? How would you trace the human factors requirements in this scenario, given that they're not typical pass/fail engineering requirements?

## Q3: How would you approach handling a situation where a risk control measure is traced to a verification activity, but the verification activity is a software unit test that runs on a host PC (not the target hardware), and the risk control involves timing behavior that depends on the microcontroller's clock speed and interrupt latency?

**Answer:** This is a fundamental question about verification fidelity. The host PC unit test can verify the logic of the risk control — for example, that the state machine transitions correctly, or that the timeout value is computed correctly from the input parameters. But it cannot verify the actual timing behavior, because the host PC has a different clock speed, different interrupt architecture, and different compiler optimizations than the target microcontroller.

I'd approach this by splitting the verification into two levels. The host PC unit test verifies the algorithmic logic: given a specific input sequence, does the software produce the correct output? This is valuable because it's fast, repeatable, and easy to debug. But for a timing-dependent risk control, I'd require a second level of verification on the target hardware — either on a development board with the same microcontroller or on production hardware — that measures the actual timing behavior under worst-case conditions.

The key question is what "worst-case" means for this specific control. If the risk control is a timeout that disables a motor when no valid sensor reading is received within 200ms, the worst case is when the firmware is busy handling other interrupts or executing high-priority tasks, so the timeout check might be delayed. The target hardware test would need to create that worst-case scenario — for example, by generating a burst of interrupts while the sensor signal is removed — and confirm the motor is disabled within the required time.

I'd also want to understand why the verification was done on the host PC in the first place. If it was a resource constraint (no target hardware available), that's a planning issue that needs to be escalated. If it was a misunderstanding about what the unit test can and cannot verify, that's a training issue. Either way, the risk management file should clearly state the verification method and its limitations, and the traceability matrix should show the host PC test as one level of evidence and the target hardware test as the definitive evidence for the timing behavior.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the timing requirement has a tolerance band — for example, the motor must be disabled within 200ms ± 50ms — and the host PC test verifies the logic but the target hardware test shows the actual timing is 180ms? How would you document that? How would you handle a situation where the target hardware test is flaky and sometimes passes and sometimes fails?

## Q4: How would you approach determining whether a risk control measure that is implemented as a hardware fuse (one-time, non-resettable) needs to be verified on every production unit, or whether verification on a sample basis is sufficient?

**Answer:** This is a question about the nature of the risk control and the failure modes of the verification itself. A fuse is a passive component that either opens or doesn't — it doesn't drift or degrade gradually in normal operation. The risk control's effectiveness depends on the fuse's rating (current and voltage), its placement in the circuit, and the circuit's ability to actually blow the fuse under fault conditions.

The design verification is straightforward: you test that the fuse opens at the specified current, that the circuit can deliver enough current to blow the fuse under the fault condition, and that the fuse's opening time is fast enough to protect downstream components. This is done once on representative units and documented in the design verification report.

For production, the question is whether 100% testing is necessary. A fuse is a relatively simple component with a well-understood failure mode, and the manufacturing process doesn't typically affect its behavior — it's soldered in place, and the solder joint is the main variable. So I'd argue that 100% testing of the fuse itself is not necessary, but I would want 100% verification of the solder joint and the circuit continuity — which is typically covered by in-circuit test (ICT) or flying probe testing.

However, there's a subtlety: the fuse's effectiveness depends on the rest of the circuit. If the fault condition is a short circuit that should draw enough current to blow the fuse, but a manufacturing defect (e.g., a wrong resistor value) limits the current, the fuse won't blow and the protection fails. So the production test needs to verify not just the fuse, but the circuit's ability to blow it. This might be done by injecting a fault current and confirming the fuse opens — but that destroys the fuse, so it can't be done on every unit.

The practical approach is a combination: 100% ICT to verify the circuit topology and component values, sample-based destructive testing to verify the fuse actually blows under fault conditions, and a documented rationale in the risk management file explaining why the sample-based approach is sufficient. The rationale would cite the fuse's reliability data, the simplicity of the circuit, and the fact that the destructive test is representative of the production process.

**Possible follow-ups:** What if the fuse is a custom part or has a long lead time, and the sample-based testing would consume units that are needed for shipment? How would you handle a situation where the fuse is supposed to protect against a fault that only occurs under specific conditions (e.g., a short circuit at the output connector) that are difficult to simulate in production testing?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where the systems engineer has created a traceability matrix that links every risk control measure to a verification activity, but during a review, you discover that the verification activities are all scheduled for the very end of the project, after all design work is complete. The program manager argues that this is efficient because it avoids rework — the design is finalized before testing begins. The quality manager argues that late verification means design errors are discovered too late, causing expensive rework and schedule delays. How would you resolve this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that both perspectives have merit. The program manager is right that testing a finalized design avoids the churn of re-testing after every design change. The quality manager is right that discovering a fundamental design flaw at the end of the project is the most expensive possible outcome. The resolution isn't to pick one side — it's to recognize that verification isn't a single event but a continuum of activities that should be staged throughout the project.

I'd propose a tiered verification strategy. Early in the project, I'd want design reviews and analysis activities — DFMEA, worst-case circuit analysis, thermal analysis, timing analysis — that catch design errors before any hardware or firmware exists. These are relatively cheap and fast, and they can be done incrementally as the design matures. Mid-project, I'd want bench-level verification on development boards or early prototypes — this catches integration issues between hardware and firmware, and it can be done before the design is fully finalized. Late in the project, I'd want the formal verification campaign on production-representative units — this is the evidence that goes into the design history file.

The key is to map each risk control measure to the appropriate verification tier. Some controls, like component derating, can be verified by analysis early in the project. Others, like firmware timing behavior, need bench testing mid-project. And some, like the final system-level response to a fault condition, need the formal verification at the end. The traceability matrix should show not just what verifies each control, but when in the project that verification occurs.

I'd also address the program manager's concern about rework directly. The reason late verification causes rework is that the design is treated as "final" before it's been proven. If we stage verification activities throughout the project, we're not re-testing after every change — we're testing incrementally and using the results to inform the design. The formal verification at the end is then a confirmation, not a discovery process. This is a more efficient use of everyone's time.

Finally, I'd suggest a risk-based approach to scheduling. For risk controls that mitigate high-severity hazards, or that involve novel technology or complex interactions, I'd want earlier verification. For controls that are well-understood and have been used successfully in previous products, later verification might be acceptable. This prioritization ensures that the highest-risk areas get the most attention early, when it's cheapest to fix problems.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the program manager agrees with the staged approach but the budget only allows for one round of formal verification testing? What if a risk control measure is identified late in the project, after the verification campaign has already been planned and budgeted?
