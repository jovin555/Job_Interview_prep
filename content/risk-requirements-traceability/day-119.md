# risk-requirements-traceability — Day 119

## Q1: How would you approach tracing a risk control measure that is implemented as a *firmware-based rate limiter on an actuator command* — for example, firmware that clamps the commanded speed of a motor to a maximum rate of change — when the risk analysis credits the limiter as a control against a hazardous overspeed condition, but the firmware requirements only describe the clamping behavior functionally and never reference the hazard?

**Answer:** The core problem here is that the requirement captures the *mechanism* but not the *safety intent*, so the traceability link from hazard to requirement is implicit and fragile — anyone reading the firmware spec in isolation would see a performance/comfort feature, not a safety control. I'd approach it in three steps.

First, I'd make the safety intent explicit at the requirement level. Rather than rewriting the functional requirement, I'd add a derived safety requirement that states the hazard it mitigates, the failure condition it guards against, and the measurable acceptance criteria — for example, a maximum commanded rate of change, the tolerance on that rate, and the behavior when the limit is reached (clamp vs. fault vs. reject). The functional requirement stays as the "how"; the safety requirement carries the "why" and the verifiable threshold.

Second, I'd establish the bidirectional link in the traceability matrix: hazard → risk control measure → safety requirement → firmware design element (the specific module/function implementing the clamp) → verification activity. The key is that the risk control measure in the risk file points to the *safety* requirement, not just the functional one, so an auditor or reviewer can walk from the hazard to the code and the test without inference.

Third, I'd make sure the verification actually stresses the failure condition — commanding a step change larger than the limit and confirming the output ramps at no more than the specified rate, including at the boundary and under worst-case timing. A test that only confirms "the motor reaches the commanded speed" would pass without ever exercising the limiter.

**Possible follow-ups:** If the rate limiter is also relied on for a non-safety purpose (e.g., mechanical wear reduction), how would you keep the two justifications from diverging over time? What would you do if the firmware team argues that adding a separate safety requirement duplicates the functional one?

## Q2: How would you approach establishing traceability when a single risk control measure is implemented as a *distributed control loop* — for example, a temperature control where a hardware sensor, a firmware PID loop, and a hardware power switch each contribute to keeping a heating element within a safe range — and the risk analysis treats the whole loop as one control?

**Answer:** When the risk analysis credits a single control but the implementation is spread across three elements owned by different disciplines, the traceability has to do two things at once: preserve the "one control" view for the risk file, and decompose it into verifiable pieces for the requirements and test artifacts. I'd handle it by treating the loop as a control with allocated sub-functions.

Concretely, I'd decompose the control into its constituent functions — sensing, decision, actuation — and write a requirement for each, each with its own measurable acceptance criteria and each traced back to the same parent risk control measure. The risk file keeps one entry, but that entry links to a small set of allocated requirements rather than a single one. Then each allocated requirement traces forward to its design element and its verification activity.

The subtle part is the *integration* verification. Individually verifying the sensor accuracy, the PID behavior, and the switch response does not demonstrate that the loop as a whole keeps the element within the safe range — the emergent behavior is what the risk analysis actually credits. So I'd add a system-level verification that exercises the complete loop under representative and worst-case conditions, and trace that test to the parent risk control measure directly. That gives you both the decomposed evidence and the integrated evidence.

I'd also document the assumptions the risk analysis makes about each element — for example, that the sensor is the sole temperature input and the switch is the sole power path — because if any of those assumptions change, the control's effectiveness claim needs re-examination.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the PID loop is later retuned for performance reasons — what in the traceability scheme would flag that the safety case needs re-checking? If the sensor and switch are shared with other functions, how does that affect your confidence in the loop as a control?

## Q3: How would you approach handling a situation where a risk control measure is traced to a verification activity, but the verification activity is a *design review* whose checklist was written for a different purpose — for example, a general "power supply design review" checklist — and the risk control's specific failure condition is never explicitly assessed during the review?

**Answer:** A design review can be legitimate verification evidence, but only if the review criteria actually address the control's failure condition. A generic checklist that happens to touch the same subsystem is not evidence that the control works — it's evidence that the subsystem was reviewed for other reasons. So the traceability link exists on paper but doesn't demonstrate anything.

My approach would be to fix the review artifact rather than abandon the method. I'd add explicit review criteria derived from the risk control measure: what specific design attributes must be confirmed, what the reviewer must look for, and what the pass/fail judgment is. For a power-supply-related control, that might mean specific criteria about the trip threshold, the response path, and the component tolerances that affect it — not a general "is the supply adequately designed" question. The review record then captures the reviewer's explicit assessment against those criteria, which is defensible objective evidence.

I'd also make sure the reviewer has the risk analysis context, because a reviewer who doesn't know the control is a safety control can't assess it properly. And I'd check whether a review is genuinely the right method here or whether the control needs a test — some controls can only be assessed by review (layout rules, derating), but many can and should be tested, and a review shouldn't be used as a shortcut for a control that's testable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a given control is legitimately review-verifiable versus needing a test? What would you do about controls already "verified" by the generic review — re-review them, or re-verify by another method?

## Q4: How would you approach creating a traceability scheme that connects risk control measures to requirements when the system includes a *safety-related parameter set at manufacturing time* — for example, a maximum output current limit stored in non-volatile memory during production — where the parameter value itself is the control, and the value can differ between units?

**Answer:** This is a case where the control isn't a circuit or a piece of code — it's a *value*, and the traceability has to cover the value's specification, its programming, and its verification, not just the mechanism that reads it. The risk is that the traceability stops at "firmware reads the limit from NVM" and never addresses how the correct value gets there and how you know it did.

I'd structure it in three linked pieces. First, a requirement that specifies the parameter: its allowed range, its tolerance, and the safety rationale for the range — this is the requirement the risk control measure traces to. Second, a manufacturing/programming requirement that specifies how the value is written, by what process, and with what controls (checksum, read-back, access restriction) so the value can't be silently corrupted or set out of range. Third, a verification activity for each: a design verification that the firmware correctly enforces whatever limit is programmed, and a production verification that each unit's programmed value is within the allowed range and was read back correctly.

The unit-to-unit variation is the key wrinkle. Because the value can differ between units, the design verification can use representative values across the range, but the production test must confirm the actual value on each unit — that's a per-unit verification, not a sample. I'd trace the production test to the same risk control measure so the matrix shows both the design-level and unit-level evidence.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a service scenario where the parameter can be changed after shipment — does that create a new verification obligation? What would you do if the allowed range in the requirement and the range the firmware can physically accept don't match?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where the traceability matrix is complete and passes audit — every risk control measure links to a requirement and a verification activity — but a test engineer privately tells you that several of the linked tests were written by copying a similar test from a previous project and adjusting the labels, and nobody has confirmed the tests actually exercise this project's failure conditions. The systems engineer argues the matrix is compliant and the tests pass, so there's no problem. How would you handle this?

**Answer:** I'd treat this as a substantive quality issue rather than a documentation dispute, and I'd be careful not to let "the matrix passes audit" shut down the conversation — a matrix that links to tests which don't exercise the failure condition is compliant in form and hollow in substance, and that's exactly the kind of gap that surfaces badly later.

My first move would be to get concrete rather than argue in the abstract. I'd ask the test engineer to walk me through two or three of the suspect tests against the risk analysis entry they're supposed to verify — specifically, what failure condition the test induces and what evidence it produces. That turns a hearsay concern into a specific, checkable finding, and it also protects the test engineer from being seen as making an unsupported complaint.

With that evidence in hand, I'd bring the systems engineer and test lead together and reframe the question away from "is the matrix compliant" toward "does each linked test demonstrate the control works under its failure condition." I'd propose a targeted review of the flagged tests — not a blanket re-test of everything — and agree on a criterion: a test is adequate if it induces the failure condition and its pass criterion is tied to the control's acceptance threshold. Tests that fail that criterion get rewritten or replaced, and the matrix links get updated to reflect the real evidence.

I'd also want to understand the root cause, because copied-and-relabeled tests usually point to schedule pressure or a template-driven test process, and if I only fix the symptoms the same thing recurs on the next project. And I'd be transparent with the quality function about what we found and what we're doing, because quietly patching it and leaving the audit record claiming full coverage would be worse than the original gap.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle it if the targeted review finds that most of the tests are actually adequate and only one or two are weak — do you still escalate? What would you change in the test development process to prevent template-copied tests from being accepted as verification evidence in the first place?
