# risk-requirements-traceability — Day 83

## Q1: How would you approach tracing a risk control measure that is implemented as a *combination of a hardware interlock and a firmware acknowledgment* — for example, a relay that physically removes power from a heater unless firmware continuously asserts an enable signal — when the hardware and firmware teams each own half of the control and neither team's requirements document references the other?

**Answer:** The core problem here is that the risk control is a *composite* behavior, but the documentation is partitioned along team boundaries, so neither document alone describes the control that the risk analysis actually credits. The first step is to recognize that the risk management file is the authoritative source: it defines the control as a single integrated measure with a defined failure condition (e.g., "heater remains energized when it should not"). From that definition, I would derive a system-level requirement that describes the *observable* behavior of the composite control — something like "the heater shall be de-energized unless both the hardware interlock is closed and the firmware enable signal is asserted" — with a measurable acceptance criterion. That system requirement becomes the anchor that both teams trace to.

From there, I would decompose the system requirement into a hardware requirement (the relay must open within a bounded time when the enable signal is de-asserted, and must fail safe if the enable line is floating or shorted) and a firmware requirement (the firmware must assert the enable signal only when its preconditions are met, and must de-assert it within a bounded time on fault detection). Each of these carries a reference back to the parent system requirement and to the risk control ID in the risk management file. The key discipline is that the *interface* between the two halves — the enable signal's electrical characteristics, its default state, its behavior on reset, and its timing — must be captured in an interface control document that both teams reference. Without that ICD, the two halves can each be "correct" in isolation while the composite control fails.

For verification, I would insist on at least one system-level test that exercises the composite control end-to-end, because neither team's unit-level test can prove the integrated behavior. The hardware team's test can prove the relay opens when the enable line drops; the firmware team's test can prove the enable line drops on fault. Neither proves the control works as a whole, and neither proves the failure condition is actually mitigated. The system test is what closes the loop.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the hardware team's relay driver has a default state that is "energized" on power-up before firmware boots, how would that affect your traceability and your verification strategy?
- How would you handle the situation where the ICD exists but the firmware team has implemented a different default state than the one the hardware team designed for?

## Q2: How would you approach establishing traceability for a risk control measure that is implemented as a *timing requirement* — for example, "the system shall remove power from the heating element within 500 ms of detecting an over-temperature condition" — when the timing depends on a chain of hardware and firmware elements, each with its own latency, and the 500 ms budget is allocated across them?

**Answer:** A timing requirement that spans a chain of elements is one of the harder traceability cases because no single element owns the whole requirement, and the risk analysis credits the *end-to-end* behavior, not any individual element's latency. The first thing I would do is treat the 500 ms as a *budget* and decompose it explicitly: sensor response time, signal conditioning and ADC conversion, firmware detection and decision logic, communication or interrupt latency, and the actuator's physical response time. Each of those gets its own allocated sub-budget, and each sub-budget becomes a requirement on the responsible element. The sum of the sub-budgets, plus a defined margin for the worst case, must be less than or equal to the total. That decomposition is what makes the requirement traceable — otherwise you have a single requirement that no one can verify.

The traceability structure I would build has the system-level timing requirement at the top, linked to the risk control ID and the hazard it mitigates. Below it, the allocated sub-requirements, each linked to the parent and to the design element that implements it. Each sub-requirement is then linked to a verification activity — typically a bench measurement of that element's latency under worst-case conditions (temperature extremes, supply voltage extremes, component tolerance). The system-level verification then measures the end-to-end time and confirms it meets the 500 ms budget with margin.

The subtle part is that the *worst case* is not the sum of the typical latencies; it is the sum of the worst-case latencies, and those worst cases may not occur simultaneously in a way that is easy to reproduce. So the verification plan needs to either force the worst case (e.g., by operating at the temperature and voltage extremes simultaneously) or justify analytically why the worst cases are independent and can be summed. That justification is itself a verification activity — a timing analysis — and it needs to be traceable to the requirement just like a test would be. If the analysis shows the budget is tight, the right response is to add margin at the system level, not to relax the requirement, because the requirement is derived from the risk analysis and relaxing it changes the residual risk.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the end-to-end test passes but one sub-element's measured latency is close to its allocated budget, how would you decide whether that is acceptable?
- How would you handle a situation where the firmware's worst-case latency depends on interrupt priority and cannot be measured deterministically on the bench?

## Q3: How would you approach creating a traceability scheme that connects risk control measures to requirements when a single risk control measure is implemented *redundantly* — the same hazard is mitigated by two independent controls, and the risk analysis credits both — but the two controls are owned by different teams and were added at different times in the project?

**Answer:** Redundant controls are a case where the traceability scheme has to represent *logical* structure, not just links. The risk analysis credits both controls because it assumes they are independent — that is, a single failure cannot defeat both. So the traceability scheme needs to capture not only "hazard → control A → requirement → verification" and "hazard → control B → requirement → verification," but also the *independence claim* itself, which is a property of the pair, not of either control alone.

I would structure it with a single hazard entry in the risk management file, linked to two distinct risk control IDs. Each control ID links to its own requirement(s) and verification activity. Then I would add an explicit independence argument — a documented rationale, supported by evidence, that the two controls do not share a common failure mode. That argument might be verified by analysis (e.g., separate power domains, separate sensors, separate firmware tasks with no shared state) or by test (e.g., fault injection that defeats one control and confirms the other still functions). The independence argument is itself a traceable item, linked to both controls.

The fact that the two controls were added at different times is a documentation hazard, not a technical one. The later control may have been added without updating the earlier control's documentation, or the two may have been designed with different assumptions about the operating environment. The remedy is a reconciliation pass: bring both controls into the same traceability structure, confirm that the independence claim still holds given the current design, and confirm that each control's verification is still valid. If the independence claim cannot be substantiated — for example, if both controls depend on the same sensor — then the risk analysis is over-crediting, and the residual risk calculation needs to be revisited. That is a risk management issue, not just a documentation issue, and it needs to be escalated.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the two controls share a common power rail, does that defeat the independence claim, and how would you document that?
- How would you handle a situation where the earlier control's verification was performed on a previous hardware revision and the later control was added on the current revision?

## Q4: How would you approach handling a situation where a risk control measure is traced to a verification activity, but the verification activity is a *manual* test with no automated logging — the operator toggles a signal and observes an LED — and the risk file treats it as objective evidence?

**Answer:** The issue here is not that manual tests are inherently invalid — they are not — but that the *evidence* they produce must be objective, attributable, and reproducible. A manual test with no logging produces an operator's assertion that "the LED came on," which is weak evidence: it depends on the operator's attention, it is not time-stamped or traceable to a specific unit and configuration, and it cannot be re-examined later if the result is questioned. For a risk control, that weakness matters because the whole point of the verification is to demonstrate that the control actually mitigates the hazard.

My approach would be to strengthen the test rather than to reject it outright. First, define the acceptance criterion precisely: not "the LED comes on" but "the LED transitions from off to on within X ms of the signal being applied, and remains on until the signal is removed." Second, add instrumentation: a logic analyzer, a scope, or a data logger that captures the signal and the LED state with timestamps. That converts the manual action into an automated measurement, and the operator's role becomes "apply the stimulus and confirm the setup," which is a much weaker claim. Third, record the test conditions: unit serial number, firmware revision, ambient temperature, supply voltage, and any other parameters that could affect the result. Fourth, have the operator sign and date the record, and attach the captured data.

If instrumentation is genuinely impractical — for example, the signal is internal and probing it would require destructive access — then the test needs a different form of objective evidence: a photograph or video of the setup and the result, with a visible timestamp and unit identifier, plus a written procedure that another engineer could follow to reproduce the result. That is weaker than automated logging but still far better than an unsupported assertion. The key principle is that the evidence must allow a third party to independently assess whether the control was actually exercised and whether it passed.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- If the manual test is the only practical way to verify the control, how would you justify it to a regulatory auditor?
- How would you handle a situation where the operator's observation is correct but the test procedure does not specify the acceptance criterion precisely enough to be reproducible?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where the risk management file lists a risk control measure, the SRS contains a requirement that implements it, and the verification plan contains a test that verifies the requirement — but when you trace the links, you find that the requirement was written by the systems engineer, the test was written by the test engineer, and neither of them has ever read the risk analysis entry that justifies the control. The links exist on paper, but the *intent* of the control has been lost between the documents. How would you handle this?

**Answer:** This is a case where the traceability matrix is technically complete but functionally hollow — the links exist, but the *meaning* that should flow along them has been lost. The symptom is that the requirement and the test may be internally consistent with each other while both being disconnected from the hazard the control is supposed to mitigate. The test may pass, the requirement may be met, and the risk may still be unmitigated because no one checked whether the requirement actually addresses the failure condition in the risk analysis.

My first step would be to trace *backwards* from the risk control entry, not forwards from the requirement. For each risk control, I would ask: what is the failure condition it is meant to mitigate, and what would the system look like if that control failed? Then I would read the requirement and the test and ask whether they would detect that failure. If the requirement is written as a functional behavior ("the system shall disable the motor output within 100 ms of a fault signal") without reference to the hazard, and the test verifies the timing but not the fault condition that triggers it, then the link is nominal but the intent is missing.

The remedy is a joint review — not a documentation exercise, but a working session where the systems engineer, the test engineer, and someone who understands the risk analysis (often the quality or risk manager) sit down together and walk through each risk control. The goal is to confirm that the requirement, as written, actually implements the control, and that the test, as written, actually exercises the failure condition. Where it does not, the requirement or the test needs to be revised, and the revision needs to be traced back to the risk control. I would also add a field to the requirement and the test that captures the *safety intent* — a short statement of the hazard the control mitigates — so that future readers do not have to reconstruct it from the risk file. That field is what prevents the intent from being lost again when the documents are revised.

The broader lesson is that traceability is not just about links; it is about *shared understanding*. A matrix that passes audit but whose authors have never read the risk analysis is a matrix that will eventually fail in the field. The fix is cultural as much as procedural: make the risk analysis a document that engineers actually read, not just one that quality references.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you prevent this situation from recurring on the next project, given that the systems engineer and test engineer are different people with different priorities?
- If the joint review reveals that the requirement does not actually implement the risk control, how would you handle the schedule impact of revising it late in the project?
