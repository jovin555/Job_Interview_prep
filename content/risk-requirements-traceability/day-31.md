# risk-requirements-traceability — Day 31

## Q1: How would you approach establishing traceability for a risk control measure that is implemented as a hardware analog circuit with a trim potentiometer that must be calibrated during manufacturing — where the calibration setting itself is part of the risk control, and the verification test must confirm both the circuit function and the calibration tolerance?

**Answer:** This is a good example of a risk control that doesn't fit neatly into a simple requirement-to-test link, because the control has two distinct aspects: the circuit design itself (component values, topology, tolerance analysis) and the manufacturing process that sets the calibration. I'd start by decomposing the risk control into its constituent elements in the risk management file. The hazard is likely something like "over-voltage reaches the patient" or "sensor reading is inaccurate," and the control is the analog comparator circuit with its calibrated threshold. I'd create separate traceability links for the design element and the manufacturing element. For the design element, the traceability would go from the hazard → risk control measure → design requirement (e.g., "the comparator shall trip at X volts ± Y%") → verification activity (a bench test that sweeps the input voltage and confirms the trip point). For the calibration element, the traceability would go from the hazard → risk control measure → manufacturing requirement (e.g., "the trim potentiometer shall be set so that the comparator trips at X volts ± Y%") → verification activity (a production test or inspection step that confirms the calibration setting). The key insight is that the calibration tolerance is a derived requirement — it comes from the risk analysis, not from a functional specification. So I'd make sure the risk management file explicitly states the acceptable tolerance band and the rationale for it (e.g., based on the hazard scenario and the margin needed to ensure the control works across component tolerances, temperature, and aging). I'd also ensure the verification test for the calibration is written to actually stress the tolerance — for example, testing at the upper and lower edges of the acceptable band, not just at the nominal setting. Finally, I'd document the link between the calibration procedure and the risk control in both the manufacturing documentation and the risk management file, so that if the calibration process changes, the risk analysis is revisited.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where the calibration tolerance is tighter than what the trim potentiometer can reliably achieve across temperature and aging?
- Would you require 100% verification of the calibration on every production unit, or would sampling be acceptable? What factors would influence that decision?

## Q2: How would you approach creating a traceability scheme that connects risk control measures to requirements when the system has a safety mechanism that can be bypassed or overridden by a clinician — for example, a "disable alarm" button on a patient monitoring device — and the override itself introduces a new hazard?

**Answer:** This is a classic case where the risk control creates a secondary risk, and the traceability scheme needs to capture both the primary hazard and the secondary hazard introduced by the override. I'd start by ensuring the risk analysis explicitly identifies the override as a risk control measure for the primary hazard (e.g., "alarm fatigue causes clinician to miss critical alarms" → "provide a temporary alarm disable function") and also identifies the new hazard introduced by the override (e.g., "clinician disables alarm and forgets to re-enable it, so a critical condition goes undetected"). The traceability scheme would then have two branches. For the primary hazard, the traceability goes: primary hazard → override control → requirement (e.g., "the device shall provide a means to temporarily silence audible alarms") → verification (a test that confirms the alarm can be silenced and that the silencing is temporary). For the secondary hazard, the traceability goes: secondary hazard → mitigating control (e.g., "automatic re-enable after a timeout" or "visual indicator that alarms are silenced") → requirement (e.g., "the device shall automatically re-enable audible alarms after no more than X minutes" or "the device shall display a persistent visual indicator while alarms are silenced") → verification (a test that confirms the re-enable behavior and the indicator). The critical point is that the override must not be treated as a single control with a single traceability path — it has two sides, and both need to be traced. I'd also make sure the risk management file documents the residual risk after both controls are in place, and that the traceability matrix includes a link from the secondary hazard to its controls, so a reviewer can see the complete risk picture. One practical approach is to use a risk control ID scheme that groups related controls (e.g., RC-101 for the override, RC-102 for the auto re-enable, RC-103 for the visual indicator) and cross-references them in the traceability matrix, so it's clear they work together to mitigate both the primary and secondary hazards.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where the clinician override is a mechanical switch (e.g., a physical button) rather than a software function? Does that change the traceability approach?
- What if the override is intended to be permanent for certain patients (e.g., a patient with chronic conditions where alarms are always false)? How would that affect the risk analysis and traceability?

## Q3: How would you approach handling a situation where a risk control measure is traced to a verification activity, but the verification activity is a software unit test that runs on a host PC (not the target hardware), and the risk control involves timing behavior that depends on the microcontroller's clock speed and interrupt latency?

**Answer:** This is a common gap in embedded systems verification. The core issue is that a host-PC unit test can verify the logic of the timing algorithm — for example, that the firmware correctly computes a timeout value or that the state machine transitions as expected — but it cannot verify the actual timing behavior on the target hardware, because clock speed, interrupt latency, and other hardware-dependent factors are different. I'd approach this by first asking what aspect of the risk control actually needs to be verified. If the risk control is "the firmware shall disable the motor within 200ms of detecting an over-temperature condition," then there are two distinct claims: (1) the firmware logic correctly detects the condition and initiates the shutdown sequence, and (2) the shutdown actually completes within 200ms on the target hardware. The host-PC unit test can verify claim (1) — the logic — but not claim (2), because the timing depends on the microcontroller's clock speed, interrupt priorities, and the execution time of the shutdown sequence. So I'd structure the verification as a combination: a host-PC unit test for the logic (e.g., "given a simulated over-temperature input, the firmware enters the shutdown state and calls the motor-disable function"), plus a target-hardware test for the timing (e.g., "inject a fault on the actual hardware and measure the time from fault detection to motor disable"). The traceability would then show two verification activities for the same risk control, each covering a different aspect. I'd also consider whether a hardware-in-the-loop test is needed for the timing measurement, or whether a simpler approach — like a test that runs on the target hardware with a logic analyzer or oscilloscope — is sufficient. The key principle is that the verification activity must be appropriate for the claim being made. If the risk control's effectiveness depends on hardware timing, then at least one verification activity must exercise the actual hardware. I'd document this reasoning in the verification plan so that a reviewer understands why both tests are needed and what each one covers.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you determine whether a timing-related risk control needs to be verified on the actual production hardware, or whether a development board with the same microcontroller is sufficient?
- What if the timing requirement has a large margin (e.g., "disable within 200ms" but the actual shutdown takes 50ms)? Does that change your approach?

## Q4: How would you approach determining whether a risk control measure that is implemented as a hardware fuse (one-time, non-resettable) needs to be verified on every production unit, or whether verification on a sample basis is sufficient?

**Answer:** This is a question about verification strategy for a control that is inherently destructive to test — you can't blow the fuse on every unit and still ship it. I'd start by thinking about what the fuse is actually protecting against and what failure modes are relevant. The fuse has two aspects: (1) it's the right fuse for the application (correct current rating, voltage rating, interrupt capacity), and (2) it's correctly installed and connected in the circuit. For aspect (1), the verification is really a design verification activity — you verify once during design that the fuse rating is appropriate for the fault scenario (e.g., by injecting a fault and confirming the fuse blows before damage occurs). This is typically done on a prototype or a sacrificial unit, not on production units. For aspect (2), the verification is a production inspection — you verify that the fuse is the correct part, is installed in the correct orientation (if applicable), and has good solder joints. This can be done on every unit through automated optical inspection (AOI) or manual inspection, and it doesn't require blowing the fuse. So the answer is that you don't need to blow the fuse on every unit — you need to verify the design once (fuse selection and circuit behavior) and verify the manufacturing quality on every unit (correct part, correct installation). The traceability would reflect this split: the design verification activity (fault injection test on a sacrificial unit) traces to the risk control measure and the design requirement, while the production inspection activity traces to the manufacturing requirement. I'd also consider whether there's a sample-based test that makes sense — for example, periodically blowing a fuse from a production lot to confirm the supplier's batch quality — but that's a supplier quality activity, not a design verification activity, and it should be documented separately. The key principle is that the verification strategy must match the nature of the control: destructive testing is done on sacrificial units during design, and non-destructive inspection is done on every production unit.

- How would you handle a situation where the fuse is a custom part with no supplier data, and you need to verify its interrupt capacity?
- What if the fuse is part of a replaceable module (e.g., a battery pack) — does that change the verification approach?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where the systems engineer has created a traceability matrix that links every risk control measure to a verification activity, but during a review, you discover that the verification activities are all scheduled for the very end of the project, after all design work is complete. The program manager argues that this is efficient because it avoids rework — the design is finalized before testing begins. The quality manager argues that late verification means design errors are discovered too late, causing expensive rework and schedule delays. How would you resolve this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that both perspectives have merit. The program manager is right that testing early — before the design is stable — can lead to wasted effort, because you're testing a moving target and the results may be invalidated by the next design change. The quality manager is right that discovering a design error at the very end of the project is the most expensive possible outcome, because you have to rework the design, re-verify, and potentially re-run other tests that were affected by the change. The resolution isn't to pick one side — it's to introduce the concept of verification stages. Not all verification activities need to happen at the same time. Some verification activities are inherently early — for example, a design review of the schematic, a simulation of the analog circuit, or a unit test of a firmware module on a host PC. These can and should happen as soon as the relevant design artifact exists. Other verification activities are inherently late — for example, a full system-level test on production-representative hardware, or an EMC test that requires a complete, finalized unit. The key is to map each verification activity to the earliest point in the project where it can meaningfully be performed, and to schedule it there. I'd propose a verification plan that has multiple phases: early verification (design reviews, simulations, unit tests) during the design phase, integration verification (bench tests on prototypes, firmware-on-hardware tests) during the integration phase, and final verification (system-level tests, compliance tests) at the end. This way, design errors are caught as early as possible, but the final verification is still done on a stable design. I'd also suggest that the traceability matrix include a "verification phase" column, so it's clear which activities are early, which are late, and which are in between. This turns the disagreement from a binary choice into a planning exercise, and it gives both the program manager and the quality manager something concrete to agree on. Finally, I'd emphasize that the goal isn't to eliminate rework entirely — some rework is inevitable — but to make sure that the rework happens at the cheapest possible point in the project, which means catching errors as early as the verification method allows.

**Possible follow-ups:**
- How would you handle a situation where a late verification test fails, and the fix requires a design change that invalidates several earlier verification results?
- What criteria would you use to decide whether a verification activity can be moved earlier, versus whether it must wait for a stable design?
