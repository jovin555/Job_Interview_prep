# risk-requirements-traceability — Day 27

## Q1: How would you approach establishing traceability for a risk control measure that is implemented as a hardware analog circuit with a trim potentiometer that must be calibrated during manufacturing — where the calibration setting itself is part of the risk control, and the verification test must confirm both the circuit function and the calibration tolerance?

**Answer:** This is a case where the risk control has two distinct aspects that need separate traceability paths: the circuit design itself and the manufacturing calibration process. I'd start by decomposing the control into its constituent elements in the risk management file — the analog comparator function (trip point, hysteresis, response time) and the calibration requirement (the acceptable range of trim settings, the tolerance band, and the method for setting it). Each element would trace to its own requirement: the circuit function to a design requirement in the hardware specification, and the calibration to a manufacturing requirement, which might live in the production test specification rather than the SRS. The verification strategy would then have two legs. First, design verification confirms the circuit works across the full range of trim settings — you'd test at the minimum, nominal, and maximum acceptable trim positions to prove the circuit function is valid across the entire calibration range, not just at one sweet spot. Second, production verification confirms the calibration process itself — that a technician or automated test station can correctly set the trim within tolerance, and that the resulting unit meets the functional requirement. The traceability matrix needs to capture both legs explicitly, because a gap in either one means the risk control isn't fully verified. A common failure mode is verifying the circuit design thoroughly but never verifying that the manufacturing process can actually achieve the required calibration — or vice versa, verifying the process works on one unit but never proving the design is robust across the trim range. I'd also want to consider whether the calibration tolerance itself needs to be derived from the risk analysis — the acceptable range should be traceable back to the hazard scenario, showing that any trim setting within that range keeps the risk at an acceptable level.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the calibration tolerance is wider than the design verification test coverage? What if the trim potentiometer is replaced with a digitally-trimmed equivalent in a future revision — how would that affect the traceability structure?

## Q2: How would you approach creating a traceability scheme that connects risk control measures to requirements when the system has a safety mechanism that can be bypassed or overridden by a clinician — for example, a "disable alarm" button on a patient monitoring device — and the override itself introduces a new hazard?

**Answer:** This is a classic case where the risk control creates a secondary risk, and the traceability scheme has to handle both the primary control and the new hazard it introduces. I'd structure this as a chain rather than a single link. The primary hazard traces to the alarm function as a risk control measure. The alarm function traces to a requirement that it can be temporarily silenced by the clinician. But that silencing capability is itself a new hazard — loss of alarm notification — which needs its own risk analysis entry and its own risk control measures. Those secondary controls might include: a visual indicator that the alarm is silenced, a maximum silence duration after which the alarm re-arms automatically, or a requirement that certain high-priority alarms cannot be silenced at all. Each of those secondary controls traces to its own requirements and verification activities. The traceability matrix needs to show this branching structure explicitly — it's not enough to just link the alarm requirement to the primary hazard and stop. I'd also want to capture the rationale for why the override is acceptable: the risk analysis should document the benefit of allowing the clinician to silence the alarm (reducing alarm fatigue, allowing patient care activities) versus the residual risk of missed alarms. That rationale should be traceable to the design decision, so a reviewer can understand why the override exists and why the secondary controls are sufficient. In terms of verification, the secondary controls need just as much attention as the primary — the auto-re-arm timer needs a test, the visual indicator needs a test, and the non-silenceable alarm priority needs a test. A gap analysis would check that every hazard introduced by the override has at least one control, and every control has a verification activity.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the clinician override is implemented in firmware, but the primary alarm is a hardware circuit — how does that split affect the traceability? What if the override behavior differs between operating modes, such as normal mode versus a "transport" mode?

## Q3: How would you approach handling a situation where a risk control measure is traced to a verification activity, but the verification activity is a software unit test that runs on a host PC (not the target hardware), and the risk control involves timing behavior that depends on the microcontroller's clock speed and interrupt latency?

**Answer:** The core issue here is whether the host-PC test can provide meaningful evidence for a timing-dependent control. I'd start by asking what exactly the risk control depends on. If the control is a timeout that must fire within a specified window, and that window is tight enough that interrupt latency and clock accuracy matter, then a host-PC test cannot verify the actual timing behavior — the host environment has completely different timing characteristics. However, that doesn't mean the host-PC test is worthless. It can verify the logic of the control: that the timeout state machine transitions correctly, that the response action is correct, that edge cases like counter overflow are handled. So I'd structure this as a two-layer verification approach. The host-PC test verifies the logic layer — the algorithm behaves correctly given the right inputs. A target-hardware test verifies the timing layer — the actual timeout fires within the specified window on the real microcontroller. The traceability matrix would show both activities linked to the same risk control, with each covering a different aspect. The key question is whether the risk analysis specifies a timing requirement that can only be met on target hardware. If the control has a requirement like "shall disable the motor within 200ms of the last valid sensor reading," that requirement needs target-hardware verification. If the requirement is more abstract — "shall disable the motor when no valid reading is received" — then the host-PC test might be sufficient for the logic, but I'd still want a target test to confirm the timing is within the window assumed by the risk analysis. I'd also consider whether the host-PC test could introduce false confidence — if the test passes on the host but the timing is wrong on target, that's worse than no test at all, because the traceability matrix shows a "pass" that doesn't mean what it appears to mean. The traceability entry should clearly state what aspect of the control each test covers, so a reviewer can see that the timing aspect is verified on target and the logic aspect is verified on host.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you decide whether a given timing requirement is tight enough to require target-hardware testing, versus being loose enough that host-PC testing is adequate? What if the project schedule doesn't allow time for a target-hardware test — how would you handle that risk?

## Q4: How would you approach determining whether a risk control measure that is implemented as a hardware fuse (one-time, non-resettable) needs to be verified on every production unit, or whether verification on a sample basis is sufficient?

**Answer:** This is fundamentally a question about what failure mode the verification is meant to catch. A fuse has two relevant characteristics: it's the right fuse (correct current rating, correct type, correctly installed) and it functions correctly (opens at the specified current). These have very different verification implications. The "right fuse installed" aspect is a manufacturing quality issue — a wrong or missing fuse is a production defect, not a design flaw. That argues for 100% inspection during production, because the cost of a missing fuse is potentially catastrophic and the inspection is cheap (visual inspection, or electrical continuity check). The "fuse functions correctly" aspect is a design verification issue — does the fuse actually open at the specified current, does it clear the fault before downstream damage occurs? That's typically verified during design verification on a sample basis, because it's destructive testing — once you blow the fuse, you can't ship that unit. You'd test a representative sample across the expected fault conditions to confirm the fuse selection is correct. The traceability structure should reflect this split. The risk control traces to two verification activities: a production inspection step (100% of units) and a design verification test (sample basis, destructive). The risk analysis should justify why the sample-based approach is acceptable for the functional aspect — for example, because the fuse is a certified component from a reputable manufacturer, and the design verification confirms the selection is correct for the application. The production inspection catches the manufacturing errors that could occur unit-to-unit. I'd also want to consider whether there's a non-destructive way to verify fuse presence and correct rating on every unit — for example, a resistance measurement that confirms the fuse is present and has the expected cold resistance, without actually blowing it. That would be a stronger production check than visual inspection alone. The key principle is that the verification strategy should match the failure mode: design flaws are caught by design verification on samples, manufacturing flaws are caught by production inspection on every unit.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where the fuse is soldered directly to the board and can't be visually inspected after assembly? What if the risk analysis requires the fuse to be verified under worst-case fault conditions that are difficult to reproduce in production testing?

## Q5: (Behavioral) Imagine you're leading a project where the systems engineer has created a traceability matrix that links every risk control measure to a verification activity, but during a review, you discover that the verification activities are all scheduled for the very end of the project, after all design work is complete. The program manager argues that this is efficient because it avoids rework — the design is finalized before testing begins. The quality manager argues that late verification means design errors are discovered too late, causing expensive rework and schedule delays. How would you resolve this disagreement?

**Answer:** I'd start by acknowledging that both sides have a legitimate point. The program manager is right that testing a moving target wastes effort — if the design is still changing, test results become invalid and tests need to be redone. The quality manager is right that discovering a fundamental design flaw at the end of the project is the most expensive possible outcome. The resolution isn't to pick one side but to restructure the verification approach so it's staged rather than monolithic. I'd propose a tiered verification strategy. Early in the project, focus on design verification activities that catch fundamental architecture issues — things like worst-case timing analysis, thermal derating calculations, interface compatibility checks. These are analysis-based activities that can be done as soon as the design is sufficiently mature, and they catch the expensive-to-fix problems. Mid-project, as prototypes become available, run verification on representative hardware — this catches integration issues between subsystems. Late in the project, run the formal qualification tests on production-representative units — this is the final confirmation that the design meets all requirements. The traceability matrix would show each risk control mapped to the appropriate verification tier, not all to the final qualification phase. I'd also introduce the concept of "verification readiness reviews" — checkpoints where we confirm that the design is stable enough to justify starting a particular verification activity. This addresses the program manager's concern about wasted effort, because we don't start formal testing until the design is frozen. But it also addresses the quality manager's concern, because we're doing meaningful verification throughout the project, not just at the end. I'd frame this as a risk management decision: what's the cost of finding a defect at each phase, and what's the probability of a defect existing at each phase? Early verification targets the high-probability, high-cost defects; late verification confirms the low-probability, lower-cost residual risks. The key is to make the staged approach explicit in the verification plan and the traceability matrix, so everyone can see that every risk control has a verification activity, and those activities are scheduled at the point where they're most valuable.

**Possible follow-ups:** How would you handle a situation where a risk control measure can only be verified on production-representative hardware, but the production hardware won't be available until late in the project? What if the program manager pushes back on the staged approach, arguing that it adds overhead and complexity to the verification plan?
